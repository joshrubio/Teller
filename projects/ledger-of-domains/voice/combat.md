# Combat Narration — Shared Guide

Load alongside `style.md` when writing any battle or confrontation.

This file has two parts. **Universal Rules** apply to any story, any magic/power system, any setting — they're about narrative mechanics, not this universe's lore. **Setting Implementation** is LoD/BSA-specific (the Fuerza Domain, Clase-R, Park/Kiki). When reusing this guide for a different project, keep the Universal Rules section as-is and replace the Setting Implementation section with that project's own power system.

---

## Core approach: Sanderson model

**Spatial anchoring + clear micro-objectives + cause-effect chains.**

Every action answers three questions:
1. Where is the POV character relative to something fixed in the environment?
2. What are they trying to accomplish in this specific beat?
3. Did they succeed or fail, and what does that enable next?

The reader should never lose the POV character in space. Other characters exist as positions, sounds, and results — not as parallel narrative threads.

---

## Universal Rules

### 1. One axis of perception

Stay in the POV character's senses. They don't know what's happening behind them — they hear it, feel the shockwave, see the aftermath when they turn. Never cut to another character mid-chain.

When you need to show another character fighting, the POV character turns and looks. That gaze is the cut.

> **Wrong:** Park absorbió el golpe. Al otro lado del claro, Kiki ejecutó un gancho de izquierda...
>
> **Right:** Park absorbió el golpe. Al girarse vio a Kiki ya de vuelta al centro — había un gwisin en el suelo al sur que no había estado ahí un momento antes.

---

### 2. Distances and directions are real

Give cardinal directions and approximate distances. "Doce metros al norte." "Tres pasos a su izquierda." "El borde sur del claro." The reader builds a spatial map. When that map is clear, action within it is legible.

> **Wrong:** Se lanzó hacia él y lo interceptó.
>
> **Right:** El agente de contención estaba a doce metros al norte, sin escudo. Park fue ahí.

---

### 3. Micro-objectives drive each beat

The POV character doesn't just react — they have a specific goal at each moment. Name the problem, show the action taken to solve it, confirm success or failure. Then identify the next problem.

This register belongs to the *setup* of a fight, before real danger is established — see rule 8 for what replaces it once the fight turns lethal.

> El problema más urgente: dos gwisin convergiendo sobre el agente de contención desde el noroeste y el este. Si llegaban a la vez, el agente no podía manejar ambos.
>
> Park fue al noroeste primero.
>
> [action]
>
> Eso dejó al del este solo. El agente tuvo el ángulo.
>
> Park ya había identificado el siguiente problema.

---

### 4. Cause-effect chains, no montage

Each action produces a specific result that enables or blocks the next. No ellipsis, no time jumps inside a fight beat. If the enemy goes to the tree, say which tree. If the ground breaks, say where.

> **Wrong:** Park luchó con varios gwisin y los fue eliminando uno por uno hasta que el claro se vació.
>
> **Right:** Recibió el campo en el antebrazo, almacenó, giró sobre el pie izquierdo, soltó. El gwisin fue al árbol del borde sur — corteza arrancada, bordes menos precisos. No disuelto. Fuera de línea.
>
> Eso dejó al del este solo.

---

### 5. Environment accumulates damage

The clearing, the corridor, the room — it changes during the fight. Track it. Roots exposed, bark stripped, craters, displaced soil. At the end of a fight the space should be readable as a site where something happened.

Name the damage as it occurs, not in a summary at the end.

---

### 6. Powers are physical, not visual FX

Whatever the power system, ground it in the body. No beams, no glows, no launching effects from a distance unless the system explicitly allows ranged action (and even then, give it a physical mechanism, not a light show). Every use of power should require contact, proximity, or a stated physical medium, have a physical cost, and produce a physical result the reader can picture.

See Setting Implementation for how this applies to the current project's specific power system.

---

### 7. Show the cost

Power use at high intensity has visible cost. Whatever the setting's marker of strain is (a material glowing/discoloring, labored breath, a tremor, blood, exhaustion) — show it right where it happens. A beat of stillness before the next action.

If a character does something physically extraordinary, show what it costs immediately after — not at the end of the scene.

---

### 8. High-tier enemies never wait their turn

When an enemy is established as genuinely dangerous (unclassifiable threat level, faster/stronger than its type), it does not queue up politely while the POV character deals with something else. Two or more threats close simultaneously, or one attacks mid-sentence — literally interrupting a line of dialogue — to signal that standard timing does not apply here.

Corollary: cut the POV character's calm tactical narration ("el problema inmediato era...", "contó: X en el norte, Y en el sur...") once real danger starts. That register belongs to the *setup* of a fight (rule 3), not to the moment it turns lethal. Once the enemy proves it doesn't wait, the character stops being able to think in full sentences — action replaces analysis. Reserve one short reflective line per beat at most, and make it read as urgent, not composed.

> **Wrong (too composed under fire):** El problema inmediato: el anciano se movía norte, la quimera se movía desde el este simultáneamente. Si Park iba a uno, el otro llegaba al tercer agente sin cobertura. Park no eligió uno.
>
> **Right:** Park no tuvo tiempo de pensar una estrategia — solo de moverse. Los dos convergían. Llegó primero por medio segundo.

---

### 9. Casualties must read as real injuries, not naps

A downed ally is not "unconscious and fine" unless the prose earns that reading. Give each fall a distinct, visceral cause: a bone giving at the wrong angle despite protection, a body convulsing from overload, a scream that isn't a word, orientation collapsing so badly the character can't speak sense. Vary the failure mode per casualty — three allies falling the same clean way (hit → mark → faint) reads as low-stakes, even after the third one.

Do not let fallen allies linger as visible, calm background detail once they're down — a glance and a fact ("no se movió") is enough. Dwelling on them softens the threat instead of sharpening it.

---

### 10. Escalating threats recover faster and hit harder each exchange

If an enemy is meant to feel like it's winning, each return pass should cost the POV character more than the last and give them less time to recover. Track this explicitly: "más rápido que las dos veces anteriores," "se levantó antes de que [character] pudiera recuperarse del golpe anterior." The reader should feel the gap between attacks shrinking even as the character's options shrink with it.

---

### 11. Environment as a loaded weapon

A character whose power routes through physical contact, force, or a channelable medium is not limited to their own body as the point of application. Anything grabbable near the point of action — debris the fight itself produced, a broken piece of the setting — can be picked up mid-motion, charged/channeled with the power in question, and used with calculated precision at a specific weak point, not just "at" the enemy. This is the Itachi/Sasuke register: precise, economical, treating the destroyed terrain as improvised armory rather than relying only on direct body-to-target contact.

Rules for this beat:
- The object must be established as physically present (debris from earlier damage, not summoned).
- The grab-load-throw (or equivalent) happens in one continuous motion, described as such — no pause to "prepare."
- The target is a specific point (a seam, an unstable configuration, a weak edge), not a generic silhouette — this is what separates it from a random improvised attack.
- It buys time or disrupts, rather than resolving the fight outright — these are tempo tools, not finishers.

See Setting Implementation for a concrete example from LoD/BSA.

---

### 12. Choreography density — how a high-stakes exchange earns its length

A single decisive exchange (the kind meant to matter, not a quick dispatch) needs a minimum of three to four discrete micro-actions, not one motion compressed into "attack → result." Break every important exchange into its component parts:

1. **Read** — the character (or the enemy) clocks an opening, a tell, a vector.
2. **Feint or probe** — a move that isn't the real attack, meant to draw a reaction.
3. **Real response** — the committed action, grounded in what the feint revealed.
4. **Result + immediate cost** — what happened, and what it took out of whoever acted.

Skipping straight from "read" to "result" is what makes a fight feel rushed even when the stakes are high — the reader doesn't get to *watch* the decision get made, only the outcome. This is the difference between "Park absorbió el golpe y contraatacó" (one beat, reads as summary) and a fight the reader can replay in their head.

This also solves pacing/length problems honestly: don't pad with description or atmosphere between exchanges — split the exchanges themselves into more visible parts. A fight that feels short is usually a fight where every exchange skipped steps 1–2, not a fight that's missing scenery.

When two skilled combatants are matched (or a fighter is facing something that has started to adapt/predict them, per rule 10), the feint-probe step becomes load-bearing: showing both sides trying to read each other, not just landing hits, is what makes the fight feel like an actual contest instead of an alternating trade of blows.

> **Wrong (skips to result):** Park soltó el golpe. La quimera lo bloqueó y contraatacó, y Park apenas esquivó.
>
> **Right:** Park amagó al torso — no era el ataque real, solo para ver si ella seguía leyendo distancia o leía intención. Ella se movió para bloquear el torso. Ahí estaba el dato que necesitaba: todavía reaccionaba al gesto, no al vector real. Park cambió el ángulo a mitad de movimiento y el golpe verdadero fue al hombro. Conectó, pero ella ya giraba con el impacto — no lo absorbió limpio, lo redirigió lo suficiente para que el daño fuera parcial.

---

### 13. Give the enemy a moment that isn't a reaction

If every enemy beat is "responds to what the protagonist just did" or "copies a technique it saw," the enemy reads as a mirror, not a threat — however smart or fast the prose says it is. At least once per major enemy per fight, give it something that originates from it: a move the protagonist didn't set up, doesn't immediately counter cleanly, and has to actually deal with as a surprise.

This doesn't mean the enemy stops adapting (rule 10, rule 12's read/feint logic still apply) — it means adaptation isn't the *only* thing it does. Options:

- **Turn a loss into a new tool.** A severed limb, a cracked core, a wound — instead of only being damage, it becomes the source of the enemy's own move (a stump used as a bludgeon, a crack that vents Odem as a weapon).
- **Catch, don't just dodge.** A protagonist's committed, high-cost attack partially succeeding — connecting but getting caught, absorbed, or grabbed — reads as far more dangerous than another clean redirect. It also raises stakes: the protagonist now has to escalate again instead of the exchange resolving.
- **Draw on something the protagonist hasn't seen yet.** A capability that hasn't been used all fight, revealed at the moment the enemy is most pressured — signals it had more in reserve than the protagonist assumed.

The point isn't to make the enemy win — it's to make the protagonist's eventual success cost something and look earned, instead of the enemy existing only to demonstrate how clever the protagonist is.

## Example — full beat

> El problema más urgente: el agente de contención en el centro-norte, sin escudo, con dos gwisin cerrando desde el noroeste y el este a la vez.
>
> Park fue al noroeste primero.
>
> El gwisin venía recto. Park plantó el pie izquierdo, recibió el campo en el antebrazo derecho, almacenó. Giró sobre el pie izquierdo. Soltó. El gwisin fue al árbol del borde sur — corteza arrancada, bordes menos precisos. No disuelto.
>
> Eso dejó al del este solo. El agente de contención tuvo el ángulo.
>
> Park ya había identificado el siguiente problema.

## What to avoid

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| "Lucharon durante varios minutos" | No spatial information, no causality |
| Cutting to another character's interior mid-chain | Breaks the single perception axis |
| Power described as ranged visual effect with no physical mechanism | Violates rule 6 |
| Damage described only in end-of-fight summary | Reader loses spatial map during the fight |
| Character moves without stated reason or destination | Action without micro-objective = montage |
| Enemies attack one at a time, politely, in sequence | High-tier threats read as toothless — see rule 8 |
| POV character narrates full tactical options while under active attack | Reads as too composed for real danger — see rule 8 |
| Every casualty falls the same way (hit → mark → faint) | Repetition flattens stakes — see rule 9 |
| Fallen allies described as calmly resting/breathing on-page after the fact | Softens threat instead of sharpening it — see rule 9 |
| A dangerous enemy's return attacks feel identical in speed/cost to the first | No escalation — see rule 10 |
| Thrown improvised weapon aimed at "the enemy" with no specific target point | Reads as generic, not calculated — see rule 11 |
| A decisive exchange compressed to "attack → result," no feint/read/response steps | Fight feels rushed regardless of stakes — see rule 12 |
| Padding a short fight with atmosphere/description instead of more exchange beats | Wrong fix — split the exchanges, don't decorate around them — see rule 12 |
| Enemy only ever reacts to or copies the protagonist, never originates a move | Reads as a mirror, not a threat, no matter how strong it's described as — see rule 13 |

## Setting Implementation — Ledger of Domains / By Strength Alone

This section is project-specific. Replace it entirely when adapting this guide for a different work.

### Domain mechanics (rule 6 applied)

Fuerza (BSA) — Domain F=ma, dos fases disponibles para cualquier usuario:
- **Inercia:** absorbs impact at a surface, stores it, redirects it through body rotation. Can also be released into the user's own movement (stored impact → acceleration). Cost: requires stable footing on absorption; misdirected release strains the joint.
- **Masa:** concentrates mass at a specific point of contact (fist, elbow, foot, sole). The concentration is precise — one point, not distributed. Cost: high-mass concentration at a strike point requires recovery before the next concentration at a different point.

**Park** tends toward inercia as his primary axis — passive accumulation, redirection, using absorbed force to propel himself. But he is capable of masa concentration.

**Kiki** tends toward masa as her primary axis — concentrated strikes, amplified impact. But she uses inercia for dynamic movement (jumps, transitions, absorbing rebounds to chain into rotation). Her most dangerous moments combine both: inercia-assisted approach velocity + masa at the point of contact = F and m and a simultaneously.

Neither is locked to one phase. Tendencies ≠ limits.

Cost marker (rule 7): Clase-R going translucent at the edges (muscle and tendon visible through the material) = near-limit.

### Environment as loaded weapon (rule 11 applied)

A user with the inercia axis can load residual inertia into grabbed debris and throw it at a calculated weak point, instead of only absorbing through their own body.

> El segundo paso de retroceso lo puso sobre un fragmento del anclaje arrancado. Park lo recogió sin frenar el movimiento — la mano cerrándose sobre el metal en el mismo tiempo en que el pie tocaba el suelo — y cargó en él la inercia residual del retroceso. No apuntó al centro — apuntó a la zona donde tres rostros se solapaban, el punto donde la firma parecía más inestable.
