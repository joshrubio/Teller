# Chapter 93 — Stalled

---

Thirty years earlier, in another country, Voss wasn't the man who walked into a horseshoe-shaped room knowing exactly what each silence was worth. He was, above all, someone who had spent four months burning his days off on something nobody had asked him to do.

The Case 2 file had been cold for a decade when he opened it for the first time, almost by accident, looking for something else in the archive. Ten years without a single new lead meant, to most of his colleagues, nothing more than a case closed in practice if not on paper. To Voss it meant something else: that nobody had spent ten years actually looking at it. He started looking in his free time, without telling anyone, convinced that a cold file wasn't the same thing as a solved one.

He found the first real thread in a minor report from a rural gendarmerie, filed without AMAT classification because nobody at that station had known what they were looking at: repeated electrical failures in a group home, always around the same girl, always without technical explanation. The report was two years old when Voss read it. He requested three days off and went anyway, without authorization, without opening an operation file, on the thin pretext of a family visit nobody checked.

The town was small, the kind of place where a stranger gets noticed within the first hour. The group home had the tired look of every building that exists to gather up children nobody has claimed — paint peeling off the window frames, a yard with more dirt than grass. The woman who ran it hadn't slept well in weeks; it showed on her face before she said a word.

"I don't know what's wrong with her," she said, once Voss got her talking, introducing himself with a false name and a flimsy excuse about social services the woman didn't question much, more relieved that someone was asking than suspicious of why. "She just stares at people like she's seeing something that isn't there. The lights flicker when she gets upset. The other kids don't want to sleep in her room."

Voss found her that same afternoon, sitting alone in the dirt yard, set apart from the rest without anyone having set her apart — the kind of distance a child learns to put between herself and others on her own, without anyone teaching her to. She couldn't have been more than eight or nine. Something in the way she looked — not at him, exactly, but at something around him Voss didn't yet have the vocabulary to name — told him, before any instrument did, that he'd found what he'd spent four months looking for without telling anyone.

He never got close enough. A man was already sitting beside her, on the bottom step of the outdoor staircase, with the patience of someone who'd been there longer than he looked.

Voss stopped dead. Not from fear — from the kind of cold certainty that arrives before fear and sometimes replaces it entirely. The man didn't turn right away. When he did, he did it slowly, without urgency, as if the interruption didn't deserve more than that.

His hair was dark then, the face of someone in his thirties, nothing that matched the blurred 1982 photograph Voss had memorized. But the eyes didn't change. Grayish blue, pale, exactly the ones in the only real image that existed of Case 2—the one trait, Voss would learn much later, no Appearance Domain ever managed to disguise.

"You took longer than I expected," the man said, without standing up. "Four months. I thought it'd be two."

Voss didn't find his voice right away. When he did, what came out was more question than threat, something he'd spend years afterward ashamed of not having said with more authority.

"Who are you?"

"You know exactly who I am," the man said. "It's the only reason you've spent four months not telling anyone at your office. If you weren't sure, you'd have called for backup already."

It was true, and that was the part he'd find hardest to admit later: he hadn't called for backup because a part of him, the young, ambitious part that didn't yet know how to tell instinct apart from vanity, wanted to be the one who found it alone.

"What have you done to the girl?" he asked, a step closer, without touching the weapon he carried, which felt absurdly useless in that moment.

"Nothing," the man said, and something in his tone, strangely, sounded like the truth. "There's no need. What's happening to her was already happening before I got here. I just found her before you did, like usual."

"Why are you here?"

The man kept looking at her for a moment — at her, not at Voss — with an expression that didn't fit anything the file said about him. It wasn't greed. It wasn't the instrumental interest of someone assessing a resource.

"Because in a few years she's going to need to know someone saw her before an entire system decided what she is," he said. "And because I want to know what that system does with her when it thinks nobody else is watching."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting today," the man said, and for the first time something close to a smile, no warmth behind it. "Take her. You're going to do it anyway, with or without my permission, so let's spare each other the part where I pretend I can stop it."

"Why aren't you fighting?" Voss said, and the question came out more honest than he'd planned. "I've got ten years of file saying you don't let yourself get caught without a reason."

The man got to his feet, slowly, no hurry or threat in the movement, and for an instant — only an instant, one Voss would spend decades trying to reconstruct without ever quite managing it — it seemed like he was going to say something more. Something about what that system had done to someone else, at some point before, somewhere Voss had no way of naming yet. The sentence trailed off, or Voss never managed to remember it whole, not even on the nights, years later, when he still tried to reconstruct it word by word before falling asleep.

"Because I'm not the one you need to watch," he said instead, and that was all.

He walked off, unhurried, toward the tree line at the end of the yard, and by the time Voss reacted enough to follow, there was no one left to follow.

He kept the girl that same night. The paperwork was, in his own words to a supervisor who never asked more than necessary, simple: field discovery, follow-up on a minor unclassified anomaly pattern, subject transferred to standard evaluation. Not a single line mentioned a town, a staircase, a man whose eyes didn't age. Voss wrote the report himself, twice, until it was clean of anything he didn't want anyone else to read.

He never told anyone. Not the colleagues he shared an office with for the next twenty years. Not, much later, the American boy who would learn to read Odem with an ease that, secretly, would always remind Voss of a dirt yard in a town he couldn't even name anymore without looking it up on a map.

---

Cartwright was in the United States that week — something administrative at AMAT-NA he'd been putting off for months, the kind of trip nobody cancels twice. Voss walked into the horseshoe room alone, and noticed, with uncomfortable clarity, how much he'd come to depend on that presence without ever admitting it to himself.

Furrer didn't waste time on preamble.

"Three days ago I requested access to a thirty-year-old file," he said, setting a single folder on the table, thinner than any of the ones he'd brought to previous sessions, as if the contents didn't need bulk to carry weight. "A travel and expense record, evaluator Voss. Yours, from a period when you still signed as a field agent."

"I have thirty years of field reports, director Furrer," Voss said, his voice as level as he could hold it. "You'll have to be more specific."

"The Verhoeven find," Furrer said.

The room didn't react right away — most of those present wouldn't recognize the surname without context. But Voss felt the name as something physical, a concrete weight set down on a table that until that moment had felt secure.

"A clean field discovery," Voss said. "Documented at the time, approved without objection."

"Documented by you, without an independent witness," Furrer said. "And here's where it gets interesting. I located the director of the institution where Verhoeven was living at the time. Still alive. Still willing to talk, thirty years later, about a stranger who showed up asking about the girl, and about the fact that you, evaluator Voss, weren't the only visitor that week."

Voss said nothing. Somewhere in his body, something that had been still for thirty years moved again — the exact same sensation he'd felt at the Center, the night of the Incursion, listening to Park describe the man who had passed through the north wall. Back then he'd allowed himself not to chase it. Now, with Furrer's file open on the table, he no longer had that option: Kaspar hadn't just been near him once, thirty years ago. He'd apparently spent an entire career getting close to every Registry case AMAT managed to find — and Voss, without fully knowing it until this moment, had spent that same career serving as the entry point.

"I don't have a name, a photograph, or a signature," Furrer went on. "What I have is a woman who remembers a second man, vaguely described, present on the same property, the same day, according to her memory, mere hours before you took the girl away. And I have your own report, evaluator Voss, which doesn't mention anyone else at all."

"Thirty-year-old memories aren't evidence," Voss said, though the sentence tasted, even to him, like something weaker than he'd intended.

"You're right," Furrer said. "They're not evidence of what happened. They're evidence that you, a senior evaluator on this council, had undocumented contact with the subject of Case 2 at the exact same moment you 'found' the subject who would become Case 3. I'm not asking whether you lied about your closeness with a colleague. I'm asking whether you lied about having spoken with a maximum-classification fugitive."

Nobody in the room spoke for a moment that felt much longer than the clock would have confirmed.

"This isn't the same as last week," the session chair said, finally, and something in his tone no longer had the measured neutrality of before. "A conduct review over conflict of interest is an administrative process. Unreported contact with a maximum-classification subject is, potentially, far more than that."

"There's nothing to report about something that didn't happen," Voss said, though he already knew, with the same cold certainty from thirty years ago, that the sentence wasn't going to hold him up.

"Then it shouldn't worry you that we confirm it," Furrer said. "I formally propose opening the conduct review, this time with no room to leave it 'on record, not dismissed.' With a hearing date. With full access to the original Verhoeven find file, including any additional documentation evaluator Voss wishes to provide voluntarily before this council goes looking for it by other means."

Nobody in the room asked the question that had saved Voss the week before. There was no one there this time who already came in with the question decided in advance.

"The review is opened," the session chair said. "Formally. Hearing within a few weeks, date to be set by the instruction committee. Evaluator Voss, until then, your direct participation in the Valais group's case is suspended, not canceled. Someone else will present whatever remains pending on your behalf."

Voss nodded once, because he didn't trust his voice to come out whole if he tried to say anything more.

---

He left the building alone, no Cartwright waiting for him in the hallway this time, no one to share the exact weight of what had just happened. He walked four blocks before taking out his phone, not because he needed the distance, but because he needed to reach a point where his own voice, speaking, wouldn't still sound like someone freshly hit.

Maren picked up on the third ring, the background noise of St. Gotthard behind her — footsteps, a door, something metallic.

"The review's been opened," Voss said, without preamble, because he'd never needed one with her. "Formal this time. Date still unset, but it's serious now."

There was a brief silence on the other end, the kind of silence Maren used to process before reacting, not to fake surprise she didn't feel.

"About the case, or about something worse?" she asked, with a calm that, to Voss, in that moment, sounded like the most generous thing he'd heard in weeks.

"Something worse," Voss said. "Furrer found the Verhoeven find. The full version, or nearly."

Another silence, longer this time.

"You never told me that part," Maren said, no real reproach in her voice, just a statement of fact.

"I haven't told anyone in thirty years," Voss said. "I was going to go on not telling it, if I'd had the choice."

"And now?"

Voss stopped on the sidewalk, no particular reason, the city moving around him with its usual indifference.

"Now I don't know if I'm going to have the choice to keep choosing anything," he said. "I need you to do something for me, Maren. And I need you not to ask me why yet."

"Tell me what you need," she said, without hesitating, and for the first time since he'd walked out of that room, Voss felt like the ground, though tilted, was still holding him up.
