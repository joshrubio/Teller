# Chapter 75 — Forty-Seven Minutes

---

The Center had seventeen lit windows.

Oliver counted them from outside because he needed something solid — something that wouldn't move while everything else reorganized. The building at his back hummed with a stillness it hadn't had an hour ago, as if the stone had absorbed everything that happened inside it and was still digesting it. The east wing was still lit. The north access too. The building's profile against the dark Alpine sky had changed in two places where Voss had shifted blocks outward — visible mass in the silhouette, discreet, the kind of detail you'd only notice if you knew the facade by heart.

The irruption had lasted eleven minutes.

The rest — the colony, the five simultaneous fronts, the east-wing corridors, everything that happened after Kaspar crossed the north wall with Tomás and Sisi — had lasted thirty-six more. Forty-seven minutes total, from the alarm to the silence.

The silence belonged to Voss.

Oliver wasn't sure exactly when he'd stopped tracking the moving signatures and started holding up what was left standing. It had been gradual: the moment the weight of the colony in the east wing outstripped what he could follow at once, the moment Pier called everyone back to the central crossing. At some point, without anyone saying it out loud, the priority had changed.

---

Kaspar's signature had been unmistakable, until it wasn't.

Oliver had kept the Registry open through the whole operation — passive mode as a second layer of perception, instinctive by now. He'd followed the signature north, watched it pull away from the access, tracked it while the east wing consumed his attention. Somewhere between minute sixteen and seventeen, Kaspar's signature had crossed the edge of what the Registry could reach.

Sisi too.

Tomás too.

Oliver hadn't said anything at the time. He'd kept containing the front in front of him.

---

They were outside because inside wasn't safe.

The subjects under evaluation and most of the field personnel had gathered into a loose group on the north esplanade — some standing, some sitting on the cold ground, some still with their eyes fixed on nothing in particular. The temperature had dropped. The sky over the eastern ridgelines had that specific quality of mountain skies before dawn — not black, but a blue so deep it looked almost solid, no clouds, stars still visible in the highest band.

Riku had the splint braced on his knee, looking at the Center's facade. Nadia and Petra stood in silence. Dara had sat a meter from Pier, who hadn't said anything since they came out. Asha looked at her own hands — palms up, fingers slightly curled, the posture of someone checking that something she did is still holding.

A few people asked questions in low voices. Not many, not urgently — the kind of questions people ask without expecting an answer yet:

Where's Sisi?

When can we go back in?

What do we do about what's still inside?

Maren arrived from the south access before anyone asked her to, report in hand — eleven pages drafted while the chaos was still active, because that was how she processed what she couldn't hold any other way. Hana had stayed with the non-combat personnel, coordinating the damage inventory.

"Three subjects unaccounted for," Maren said, without opening the pages. "Sisi, Lev, Sabine. Lev's and Sabine's rooms are empty. Planned departure. The beds were made. They took what they needed."

Silence.

"When?" someone said.

"Unconfirmed," Maren said. "During the incident, probably. They used the chaos."

The information landed differently in different people. Pier closed his eyes for a second, turned away, visibly angry. He'd seen it coming. Petra looked at the building. Dara didn't do anything visible, but Oliver logged the small shift in her posture — the minimal adjustment of someone receiving something and filing it in the mental space she'd already had ready for it.

Asha kept looking at her hands. Riku dropped his face to his knees, shutting himself off completely.

Oliver looked at the Center's facade. He'd been carrying Anchor in his body since Voss's birthday — the first accidental copy, Sabine's Domain, which he'd used three hours ago to hold himself up in the north wing. Now it was the copy of someone who'd made her bed before leaving. Lev too. Both of them had measured the window and crossed it.

It wasn't betrayal. That was the part he couldn't find anywhere to put.

---

Voss came out through a point on the roof that hadn't existed forty seconds before.

He circled the east wing first — his hand brushing the facade stone for ten meters, one last check on what he already knew was sealed. Then he approached the group.

He'd come down standing on the same block of stone he'd shifted to seal the east wing — mass in motion forty centimeters off the ground, silent. He took a step when he reached the edge of the group. His weight did the rest.

He moved through the people with the same mechanics he'd used to read the building for forty-seven minutes — mass, angle, position. The Valais Center stood sealed behind him: an accumulation of stone with a geometry that matched no construction blueprint. He stopped where the space stopped opening and started closing in around him, at the same point where he'd stood before activating the Domain for the first time that night.

He looked at Oliver.

It wasn't a long look. It was the kind of look someone gives after forty-seven minutes calculating the cost of a system he designed and just watched fail in real time — not accusation, not gratitude, something harder to name. Oliver had been in the north wing when Kaspar said *still time* and hadn't taken the hand. Voss knew that. Or at least sensed it. What that meant about where things stood between Oliver and the Center was something both of them could read without naming it.

Voss looked down at Maren.

"Three non-combat casualties among security personnel," Maren said, continuing the report. "The rest, minor injuries, already treated. Two east-wing corridors out of use pending structural evaluation. The north access needs a full review."

"The north wing's structural integrity is guaranteed," Voss said. "Not because of the original state of the materials. Because of the current one."

A pause. Nobody asked what that meant.

"The colony is contained. The building is sealed on every entry and exit vector I identified tonight. Nothing goes in. Nothing goes out." He looked at the group. "That's enough for now. Tomorrow we'll talk about what to do with what's inside."

---

"I need to report what I found in the north wing."

It was Park. Standing, in the same field posture he'd walked into the Center with months earlier — straight-backed, voice calibrated to the tone of someone delivering information without dramatizing it.

"During the confrontation at the north access, the individual leading the irruption made direct eye contact with me." A pause. "He wasn't here to contain us. He was here to cross. The colony didn't attack: it cleared the way. Every move was calculated weeks in advance — the access points, the monitoring, the corridors. This wasn't an irruption. It was a planned extraction." Park glanced briefly at Voss. "The behavioral profile matches the intelligence pattern we've been tracking for months. I can't confirm identity. But I think we both know which case we're talking about."

Park left it there and looked at the group.

Then he looked at Oliver.

Oliver looked at Riku.

It wasn't a conscious calculation — it was the instinct to find something solid in his field of vision while he organized what he was about to say. Riku was looking back at him. He knew what was coming.

Oliver looked at Park. Then at Voss.

He thought about the bench in the village square. About the man with brown hair who knew things about the Registry he shouldn't know. About the nine days. About the decision not to report it, made back then and rebuilt in his head ever since — piece by piece, searching for the exact moment the argument had turned into self-justification.

He couldn't find it. That was the worst part.

"Sisi decided to leave," Oliver said. "He did something to her, I'm sure of it."

It wasn't a lie. It was the true part of something bigger he didn't say. It was the only way to keep her from being catalogued as an immediate danger — or worse.

The group processed it in silence. Pier's eyes opened. Maren didn't look up from the report, but something in her posture changed — minimal, visible only to someone who knew how to look.

Voss didn't say anything yet. Inside him, something that had stayed still for thirty years shifted just enough for him to notice — the concrete, uncomfortable feeling of recognizing a pattern before you have permission to name it. This wasn't the first time someone with that profile had approached a Registry case. Voss knew it with a certainty that came from no file in front of him. He was going to authorize a trace — in fact, in under a minute, he was going to hear Park propose exactly that. What he decided not to follow, in that same instant, was the internal thread that certainty opened inside him: where it came from, and why he recognized it so well.

---

"Yusuf has the trail," Park said.

Not as an abrupt shift — as the natural next step for someone who'd already processed what couldn't be recovered and had turned his focus to what could still be done.

"He started tracking at minute fourteen from the north perimeter. The forest cover to the north leaves a trace when the Transit Domain activates near organic matter — the signature's different, shorter, but locatable. He has a general direction." A pause. "It's not enough for immediate interception. It's enough to know which way they're moving."

"And for recovery?" someone said.

"Depends what we mean by recovery." Park didn't say it coldly, just precisely. "Sisi is an adult subject who made an informed decision. AMAT's legal framework for involuntary recovery requires evidence of coercion or active harm. We don't have that evidence. What we have is a trail, and Yusuf following it."

Silence.

"What I'm proposing," he went on, "is long-term tracking. No interception. No recovery operation. We document the direction of movement, the contact vectors, the group's support points. With that we build a map. And if there's ever grounds to act — or if someone decides to come back — we'll know where to look."

It was a good plan. Oliver knew it. The part nobody said out loud was just as visible: long-term tracking meant Yusuf spending months following people who'd chosen to leave. The line between that and pursuit depended on what they used the map for in the end.

Park knew that. He'd said it anyway.

---

Voss pinched the bridge of his nose.

It wasn't a dramatic gesture — two fingers pressing lightly on the bone, the kind of gesture someone makes when they're organizing too many things at once and need a second so the weight doesn't spill over.

The group saw it. Nobody said anything.

What Voss was organizing was Sisi's file, which he'd read on three separate occasions and classified all three times as extended-evaluation case, protocol pending, methodology unapproved. The Geneva group, nine months without a methodology, which he'd proposed precisely because he'd read that file and knew the system had no tools to read her. Tonight's incident — forty-seven minutes of stone moving through his hands, trying to contain something that had already decided its course long before the alarm sounded.

The gap between what he'd done and what he should have done was exactly nine months wide.

He lowered his hand.

"What happened tonight wasn't a security failure."

A pause. The group waited.

"It was a speed failure." He looked at the group — not at any one person, at all of them. "The system has the tools for what it already knows how to read. It doesn't have the tools for what it hasn't learned to read yet. And there are people here" — he glanced briefly at Pier and Asha — "who've spent months, some of you years, inside a framework that doesn't move at the speed you move. That's not a protocol problem. That's mine."

Nobody spoke.

"I've been pushing the right pieces from the inside at a speed that wasn't enough. Tonight I see that clearly." A pause. "The Geneva group will have an approved methodology before the end of the month. Not because I ask for it from here. Because I'm going in person and telling them what I saw tonight." He looked at the group one last time. "What each of you did here tonight deserves a system that reads you before you have to find another way out. That's what I'm going to try to build. With more urgency than I've used until now."

He didn't ask for a response. He stepped down off the stone block with the same calculated movement he'd used to climb it.

Pier was watching him.

"Eleven years," Pier said, without irony and without forced warmth. "About time."

Voss nodded. Only that.

Asha got up off the ground without announcing it. She'd kept her palms up since Maren arrived — the posture of someone checking that something she'd done was still holding. She closed her hands. Put them in her pockets and looked at the Center.

Pier looked at her for a second. He didn't say anything.

---

The first light arrived while operational staff were carrying the last crates out through the south access.

Not sunlight yet — the clarity that comes before the sun in the mountains, when the sky shifts from deep blue to pale blue and the silhouettes of the eastern peaks start separating from the background. The Center with its seventeen lit windows stood overexposed for a moment — the gray stone, the displaced blocks visible in the silhouette, the building's mass more present in the coming light than it had been in the dark.

Nobody was going back inside.

Maren had said so without drama, in the same cadence as the report: the Center was going under temporary closure protocol, the east wing sealed with the colony inside pending full evaluation, personnel redistributed to a backup facility Maren named only by its internal designation, no further detail — nobody asked, it wasn't the moment yet. She'd given the order before anyone asked her to. Hana was coordinating from the south access — cars, vans, two of the operational team's SUVs, a medical transport vehicle for the minor casualties. Non-combat personnel were already loading up.

Pier and Petra had stayed at the inner edge of the east wing — not inside, but at the threshold, using their Domains to keep the containment stable while the rest of the group gathered what they could. Pier with the gravity field reduced but active, crushing any movement that tried to reach the gaps between blocks. Petra repositioning a fragment twice after it found an opening on the north flank — a clean Swap, no argument.

Oliver packed up his things in four minutes.

There wasn't much in the drawers. On the desk was a folded sheet of paper — white, no envelope. It hadn't been there before tonight started.

He opened it. It was written in English, in clear handwriting with no flourish:

*There was one before you. Her name was Anke. She made the same choice you made tonight, for reasons that were also good. I watched what happened to her over eleven years.*

*When you need to know what comes next, you know how to find me.*

*— K*

Oliver read it twice. He folded it and put it in his jacket's outer pocket.

What was different after six months wasn't in the drawers. It was in his body, and that moved with him.

Voss was standing by the stone block when Oliver came out for the last time — the same spot he'd occupied all morning, as if the block were the command post and he'd never needed to move from it to operate the whole building. Oliver walked over without anyone telling him to.

"What did he tell you tonight?" Voss asked. No preamble, no naming him. *Tonight* did the work of every earlier question.

Oliver thought about the bench. About the man with brown hair. He thought about leaving it there — letting Voss assume the contact had happened during the irruption, in the north wing, when the signature was unmistakable and Kaspar said *still time*. It wasn't the truth. It wasn't a complete lie either. It was the space between the two where Oliver had been living for nine days.

He didn't say when. He said what.

"That there was someone before me," he said. "That she made the same decision I made tonight. That he watched her for eleven years."

Voss looked at him.

"And what happened to her?"

"He didn't tell me."

Silence. The sky to the east kept brightening.

"That," Voss said, after a moment, "is a kind of argument."

Oliver didn't answer.

Voss waited another second, the kind of pause that in him usually preceded a concrete request — *when we get there, I need you to tell me everything you talked about, with Maren present* — but it never came. Something in Oliver's face must have closed that door before he fully opened it, because what he said instead was different.

"When you're ready to tell it, you'll tell it," Voss said. "Not before."

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't entirely permission either. It was, more than anything, the acknowledgment of something Voss already knew and preferred not to force yet.

Oliver didn't correct him. In his head, the decision was already made, and it wasn't the one Voss had just named out loud: he wasn't going to tell Voss any of this, not now, not when they reached wherever they were going. What he knew — the name, the eleven years, the note still folded in his pocket — he was going to keep for the next time he found Kaspar. That conversation, when it came, was going to be with the one person who actually had the answers.

---

Riku stood by the SUV with his eyes on the eastern peaks, where the light was starting to separate the ridgeline from the sky. Dara sat on the ground beside him, back against the wheel, watching the eastern ridges where the first band of light was beginning to define the mountain's outline.

Oliver came over and sat down next to Dara without saying anything.

The three of them, silent for a moment.

"Sabine," Riku said.

Oliver didn't answer right away.

"Yeah," he said finally.

He carried Sabine's Domain in his body. Riku knew — they'd talked about it in session, what it meant to copy something from someone. He didn't say it. He didn't need to.

"Lev?" Riku said.

"Before it started. I saw the door on my way past."

Riku closed his eyes for a second. Then opened them.

Dara didn't say anything. She watched the ridgelines with the closed expression of someone cataloguing something she doesn't know how to resolve yet.

"How far out are we?" she said at last.

"Almost three hours," Oliver said.

Dara nodded. As if that number were enough for something to settle, or not, but either way the number was the number.

The engines started — the front one first, then the second. The sound in the mountain air was clean, with no echo — the kind of sound that turns a place into something that already was.

Riku stood up. He opened his right palm for a moment — the checking gesture from the last few months, so automatic he didn't think about it anymore. This time there was no active field. Just the hand.

Dara was already standing.

In the forest to the north, Yusuf was following a trail going cold with the light.

Oliver closed the Registry and got in the car.
