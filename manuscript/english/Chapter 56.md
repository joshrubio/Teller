# Chapter 56 — What Stays Buried

The sun finished climbing over Sorte while Park was still counting what he'd gained against what he'd lost, the count refusing to come out clean in either column. Yorman didn't move from his brother's side until Salcedo, with the practical gentleness of someone who'd done this before in another country, put a hand on his shoulder and told him they had to move before full daylight found a yard full of bodies no one with authority was supposed to find there.

They moved. The seven rescued — eight, counting Yorman — were split between the pickup and two vehicles Casimira managed to summon from a number Park didn't ask where she'd gotten. No one asked why a foreigner covered in someone else's blood was giving the orders. By then, no one on that mountain expected things to have an explanation that made any real sense.

Ronald's body rode in the bed of Salcedo's pickup, wrapped in the one clean blanket anyone could find, with Yorman sitting beside him the whole way without a word. Park drove behind, in the second vehicle, checking the rearview mirror every few minutes as if he needed to confirm that detail, at least, was still true.

---

"The cover story's going to be violence between rival collectives, with an illegal mining angle to explain the rest," Ferrer said, over video call, that same afternoon, her voice more tired than Park had heard it the whole arc. "It's going to cost me some favors I don't have, but with the whole country looking at Caracas this week, no one's going to ask too many questions about a coffee farm in Yaracuy."

"And Roa?"

"Dead in a clash between rival factions over a smuggling route," Ferrer said. "Only half a lie, again. There was a clash. There was a death. The rest is just a matter of which words you use to describe it."

Park stayed quiet a moment, the image of Contreras falling, his leg giving way, still fresh — Roa on the floor of the shed fresher still.

"I want to send you the full report," he said, finally. "All of it. Including the name Casimira confirmed."

Ferrer didn't look surprised.

"Send it," she said. "I can't promise you anyone's going to read it with the attention it deserves. I can promise it'll get filed under the highest classification I can give it without any real resources behind it to act on it. Right now, agent, that's the most this country and this branch can offer for what you found up there."

"And if that's not enough?"

"Then someday, someone with more resources than I have this week is going to have to open that file for a reason neither of us wants to imagine yet," Ferrer said. "All I can do is leave it written down so that day, if it comes, doesn't start from zero."

"How many files like this does this branch have stored away?" Park asked. "Things with no category, waiting for someone with more time to read them."

"More than you'd like to know," Ferrer said. "This isn't Lisbon or Budapest, agent. Here we don't have the luxury of a branch that can afford to investigate everything it finds. We have an entire country's worth of uncategorized things, and a branch the size of a handful of people to decide which of those things can wait and which can't."

He wrote the report that same night, in the hotel room, with more precision than he'd used in any report before it — the girl, the approximate date she'd been taken, the mechanism of the sacrifice, Casimira's warning about what's born of ten years of blood and has no good version waiting underneath. He softened nothing. He didn't know, as he wrote it, whether anyone besides Ferrer would ever read it in full, but he wrote it as if someone would, someday, when it was already too late for the warning to offer any comfort and it could only serve as a map.

---

Nélida met him at her door two days later, the photographs still covering the walls behind her, and Park understood, from the way she looked at him, that she already knew what was coming before he said a word.

"It wasn't him," Park said. "None of the people we got out that night was your son."

Nélida nodded, slowly, without crying — the kind of grief that had already passed through every possible phase and settled, long ago, into a kind of quiet resistance that had nothing to do with acceptance.

"I knew it," she said. "I knew the moment I saw you coming alone, without leading him by the hand. But thank you for coming to tell me in person instead of letting me hear it from Yusmary."

"I'm going to keep looking as long as I can," Park said, and knew, even as he said it, that the promise had an expiration date he didn't control.

"I don't doubt it," Nélida said. "Here we all make promises we don't fully control. It's the only thing we have left to keep from going crazy."

She served him coffee anyway, in the same mismatched glass from the first visit, and Park accepted it though he wasn't thirsty, because refusing would have been worse than staying to drink it in silence with a woman who'd spent eleven months learning to live with not knowing.

---

Yusmary found him at the market, the same plaza where they'd first met, and didn't ask for explanations Park wouldn't have known how to give her.

"Ronald would have been proud of how this turned out," she said, "though not of how it ended for him."

"I don't know if this counts as ended," Park said.

"Nothing that happens in this country counts as ended," Yusmary answered. "It counts as survived, or it doesn't. You survived. Yorman survived. That, here, is already more than most people get."

She didn't ask him to stay. She didn't ask him to come back. She gave him a short hug, the same kind of practical gesture Wilkins had handed out that night in the shed, and went back to her post without looking back.

Yorman was the hardest. Park found him at an uncle's house, on the outskirts of town, still looking like someone who hadn't finished deciding whether what had happened to his brother was real.

"I don't know what to say to you," Park admitted, sitting down across from him. "I never know quite what to say in these cases, and this time it's worse because it was because of me that you got to see him alive again before losing him a second time."

"He looked for me for four months without stopping," Yorman said. "Me, locked up in there, I didn't know if anyone was still looking or if they'd already written me off like the rest. Knowing that wasn't true, even if it was only for a minute before it all ended... " He stopped, swallowed, went on. "That's worth more than I can explain to you or to anyone."

Park didn't have an answer equal to that, so he didn't try to give one. He sat with him a while longer, in silence, and for the first time since arriving in Venezuela he felt that the silence, this time, wasn't a failure on his part.

Before he left, he gave Yorman Salcedo's direct number, not his own — he knew, with the same cold certainty he'd carried all week, that he himself wasn't going to be available to anyone in that country much longer, and he'd rather leave Yorman something that would actually work when he needed it, instead of a promise with an expiration date like the one he'd already given Nélida.

Casimira said her goodbye at her own door, a new cigar already lit between her fingers.

"I'm going to keep watching the mountain," she said. "Not because I think I can stop whatever comes out of it, if it decides to come back. Because someone has to be watching when it happens, and I'd rather it be me, with my eyes open, than someone who wouldn't even know what they were looking at."

"And if it doesn't come back?"

"Then I'll have spent the rest of my life watching a quiet mountain," Casimira said, "and I won't think a single day of it was wasted."

She shook his hand goodbye, a formal gesture that didn't quite fit everything they'd been through together those past days, and then, as if she'd thought better of it, hugged him instead.

"Take care of yourself, agent," she said, against his shoulder. "And if you ever come back to this mountain, let it be because you want to, not because you have to."

---

Park's phone buzzed that same night with a number he didn't recognize at first, until the text message opened on its own: *Heard from Salgado you're in Venezuela. Dad says watch out for dengue. I say watch out for everything else. — K.*

Park didn't know what to answer for a long while. In the end he wrote only three words — *I will, Kiki* — and put the phone away again, with the strange feeling that out of the whole week, that was the one conversation that hadn't cost him anything.

---

Cartwright called two days later, her voice carrying the kind of contained satisfaction she only allowed herself when something had gone better than her own numbers predicted.

"Successful mission, agent," she said, without preamble. "You identified the phenomenon, classified it, and contained what needed containing exactly within the window it needed containing in. The Washington operation went off clean partly because you made sure nothing on that mountain complicated it that morning. That's not going to show up in any official report, but I know it, and now Ferrer does too."

"It's not a victory, ma'am," Park said. "It's one more thing, bigger and more dangerous, loose in a forest, and a report filed on a server no one with real authority is going to open this week, or next."

"I know," Cartwright said, no defensiveness in her voice. "What exactly are you asking me for?"

"Don't let it sit," Park said. "I already saw what happens when something like that gets left waiting ten years for someone with authority to decide to look at it. I saw what it produces."

Cartwright went quiet a moment, the kind of silence Park had learned to recognize as calculation, not hesitation.

"I can't promise you a containment operation for an uncategorized entity in a country that doesn't even have a stable government yet," she said, finally. "But I can keep you close to where the problem's still a problem. Washington's going to need people in Caracas the next few weeks, maybe months, securing zones, easing the transition, containing whatever needs containing while the country decides what it's becoming. I'm assigning you that phase. You're going to keep doing exactly what you did in Yaracuy — contain, secure, ease the way — only now with authorization to spare and a whole city watching instead of a mountain no one wants to name."

"And the girl?"

"Still your report, and it's going to stay my problem for as long as I hold this post," Cartwright said. "That's not a no, agent. It's a not yet. Right now, the closest I can put you to Sorte without directly disobeying Ferrer is Caracas. Take it or don't."

Park took it.

---

The weeks that followed looked little like Yaracuy and even less like Budapest — less combat, more perimeters, more meetings with officers from an army that no longer knew whose side it was on that particular week, more hours explaining to people with more rank than him why a given zone needed to be cleared before a given convoy arrived. Contain, secure, ease the way. None of the three words felt like strength, not in the sense he'd always understood the word. It felt more like holding something with both hands while other people, in another country, decided what to do with what he was holding.

Park thought, more than once during those weeks, about Ji-ho dispatching the imugi on that island without it seeming to cost him anything — the same kind of absolute control Park himself had felt, for the first time really, tearing through Roa's yard. But Ji-ho had never had to stand there afterward, hands shaking, in front of something that could kill him without moving and that he himself had chosen to let run into a forest instead of finishing the job. Park didn't know if Ji-ho had ever felt that. He suspected not. He suspected that was, maybe, the real difference between the two of them.

Park never found out whether that girl was still on the mountain, whether she'd grow up, whether something worse had finished being born in whatever time it took someone to read what he'd left written. What he did know, with the same cold certainty he knew everything else, was that Casimira had been right: there was nothing good waiting underneath what he'd seen in that windowless room. It was real, it was dangerous, and he'd let it loose. He left Yaracuy carrying that certainty like a physical weight, not an abstract question — convinced that someday, in some report, someone was going to have to answer for what he hadn't been able to stop when he'd had the clearest chance he was ever going to get.

---

Maiquetía airport, days later, looked nothing like the rest of the country Park had traveled through — orderly lines, lights that didn't go out, an artificial calm that only existed because the whole world was watching there. He waited for his flight to Geneva in a plastic chair bolted to the floor, his bag between his feet, and for the first time since Hartford he let himself think about the five missions in a row without dividing them by country, as if they were, all together, a single question he hadn't finished answering.

Voss's call came nine days after Cartwright's, while Park was still in Caracas doing exactly that, and it closed, without Voss knowing or being able to know it, whatever window he had left to go back to Yaracuy and finish writing what the first report had left incomplete. By then, Ferrer had already filed everything Park had sent her under the highest classification her branch could offer without any real resources behind it — a file waiting, on some AMAT server, for someone with more authority and more urgency than either of them to decide, someday, that it was worth opening.

In Hartford he'd learned that strength was for knowing when protocol was wrong and acting anyway. In Seoul, that it was for winning even with someone better than him watching. In Lisbon, that it was for doing the right thing when no one else saw. In Budapest, that it was for staying on your feet after crossing a line there was no way back across. Four answers, each true in its own way, and none of the four had done him any good in front of a line of people waiting their turn to fill a bucket of water, or in front of a girl in a faded dress whose life no blow of his, however precise, was ever going to change.

He wondered, without finding an answer he liked yet, whether that was the question that mattered, or whether it was, really, the same question as the other four, finally said out loud: what exactly did he have, if what he had wasn't enough for the thing he most needed it to be enough for. It didn't feel like strength. Not the way he understood the word.

His flight was called. Park stood, shouldered his bag with more care than a weight like that called for, and walked toward the gate thinking that he was leaving Venezuela carrying more than a filed report and a name no one else yet knew to fear — he was carrying the first time, in five missions, that the question of his own family had stopped sounding like inheritance and started sounding like real doubt, his own, unresolved.

His phone buzzed in his pocket just as he handed over his passport at the gate. Out of habit, more than urgency, he checked it before putting it away for the next eight hours.

It wasn't Ferrer, or Cartwright, or any channel from the mission that had just closed. It was AMAT-EU's request system, the same one that had told him nothing but *pending* for five missions straight — the three requests he'd filed before Hartford, about the forty-three pages from 1952 classified under Geneva, that he'd stopped checking somewhere between Lisbon and Budapest. All three were still there, on the same screen, in the same status as always.

Except one.

*Follow-up report 1952-HFD-002 — access granted. Reviewer: Office of Operations, Geneva.*

Park stood there one second too long, the line behind him starting to grow restless, staring at the name of the office that had just opened, with no explanation, the file that had gone five missions untouched. Not someone's individual testimony, not a name or a disappearance date — the full follow-up report, the one that summarized what AMAT-EU had pieced together about 1952 in the decades since the event. The kind of document that, if it really said what Park suspected it said, was less about what had happened in Hartford and more about what a Ruler was, in general. The same office that, according to Voss, had begun his transfer to Valais before Cartwright even had a copy of the memo.

He put the phone away without opening the file, because eight hours of flying wasn't enough time to read something like that for the first time with the head he had that night, and boarded the plane thinking that, for the first time since he'd filed those three requests, someone in Geneva had decided he finally had the level of access he needed.
