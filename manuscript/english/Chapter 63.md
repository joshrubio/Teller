# Chapter 63 — Own Frequency

The first night Oliver paid real attention, he found nothing.

It was eleven twenty. The room was dark except for the light coming through the gap in the blinds — white, snow-white, the kind of light that in January in the Valais never fully disappears even without a moon. He lay with his eyes open and his hands on his chest and tried to do what Park had asked him.

*Find your own.* No instruction beyond that.

Oliver searched. The room's Odem arrived immediately — the contours of the bed, the ceiling, Pier's faint signature two floors down. That was what the Registry knew how to do. What Park wanted came before all of that: his own. The material the Registry started from before it ever pointed at anyone.

He didn't find it. He fell asleep without solving it.

The second night he understood why. The Registry looked outward — weeks of practice had built it that way, months of instinct, the constant habit of reading before deciding to read. Looking inward meant going against the natural current. It was like trying to see the eye that sees.

He wrote in the notebook: *The only direction that works is inward. Lev said it that way.* And underneath: *what does that actually mean?*

The third night, Oliver didn't write anything yet. He lay in the dark and let the voices come.

It wasn't deliberate at first. It was the kind of thinking that happens when the body already wants to sleep and the head hasn't finished yet. The voices were the ones he'd been listening to longest.

*You're not searching*, Park would say. *You're waiting for it to happen.*

Oliver corrected: active. He tried to search actively — directed concentration, intention turned inward. Nothing perceptible.

*Slower*, Park would continue, with that economy of words that admitted no reply. *The Registry identifies other people's signatures because other people's signatures have shape. Yours has no shape for you. It doesn't know what to look for, because it's never had to.*

That was better. Oliver held onto it a moment. Then he let it go, because it wasn't quite right — Park wouldn't speak in terms of Oliver's Registry. Park spoke in terms of mechanics, of Odem as substance, of what moves and why. The words Oliver was putting in his mouth were Oliver's own, dressed up as Park.

He tried Pier. *The difference between knowing where the ground is and feeling it under your feet*, he would have said, in the tone of someone quoting something he'd learned the hard way. Oliver pictured that tone — precision without urgency, the patience of someone who no longer needs anyone to believe what he knows. He tried to feel the ground.

He was still in bed.

*The body's already been doing this a long time*, he tried with Yusuf. That sounded right. It sounded like something Yusuf would say.

Except it wasn't what Yusuf would say. Oliver corrected himself in real time. That was something he was projecting onto Yusuf because he wanted someone in the group to have that kind of direct clarity, that trust in the physical before the analytical. What Yusuf would really have said was probably much shorter. Probably: *Stop.* And nothing else.

Oliver stopped.

Pier's signature was still below, two floors down. Riku to his right, in the room at the end of the hall. Asha a little further off. The passive signatures, the Registry's habit working while he tried the opposite.

Lev wouldn't have said anything. He understood that now. Lev had said *the only direction is inward* and then had waited — not because there was nothing more to teach, but because what came next couldn't be handed over. The next lesson was the silence itself. And Oliver hadn't heard it because he'd been waiting for the next sentence.

He held the silence.

Then he thought about what Voss would have said, if Voss explained it without a filter: *The Registry has no native mechanism for pointing at the operator. It's an instrument for external reading — designed outward, built outward, practiced outward. What Park's asking you isn't to use the Registry. It's to find what the Registry uses in order to function.*

That arrived differently.

It wasn't while lying in bed. It was when he got up.

Oliver hadn't planned to get up. It was Voss's thought that pulled him out of bed, almost without deciding it: if the Registry was an instrument for external reading, and what Park was asking was to find what the Registry used in order to function, then staying in bed searching inward with the same instrument was the mistake. The instrument needed to move so he could separate the noise from the signal.

He stood in the dark. The room was small — four steps in any direction. His feet on the stone floor, cold even through his socks.

He started with Park's sequence.

He hadn't copied it — he'd read the structure, which was different. But the structure had lived in his memory and his body for weeks now, and the movements came without effort: the weight distributed, the shift of the center of gravity Park made before any activation, the rhythm it had taken Oliver weeks to understand wasn't technique but listening. Park didn't move his body to prepare the Domain. He moved his body to know where the Odem was before he used it.

Oliver moved his feet. Distributed his weight. Looked for the rhythm.

Then he added Yusuf's — not the whole sequence, but the principle Yusuf had repeated across three different training sessions without ever naming it that way: *the ground always knows more than you do.* Pressure in the feet. Attention on the weight, not the posture. Letting gravity do half the work.

Pier's signature was still below, two floors down. Riku's, to his right. The Registry processing without Oliver having asked it to.

But underneath that — underneath the habit, underneath the Registry, underneath eighteen weeks —

Something.

It wasn't anyone's signature. It came before any signature, before habit, before practice. Distributed — in the feet against the floor, in the weight he carried, in the space between one movement and the next. Without the identifiable frequency of the copies he kept or the signatures he read. Just there.

Oliver stopped. He stood in the dark with his feet in the cold of the floor and did nothing more with it. He noticed it. He let it be.

In the notebook, the next morning, he wrote a single line: *I think I found it. I don't know what to do with it yet.*

---

Park didn't mention the exercise over the following days. He continued the sessions as if the conversation outside hadn't happened, and Oliver had already learned that this was part of the method — no waiting for confirmation, no waiting for progress to be named. What Park planted either grew on its own or didn't.

The days that week had a specific texture Oliver didn't catalogue while he was living it. The cold that hadn't let up since before Christmas. The Center's hallways with the heating always slightly under what was needed, the stone walls colder to the touch in the mornings than in the afternoons. Everyone's boots at the main entrance with the trail of packed snow on the floor. The dining hall at eight with Pier already up and reading, the coffee always a little stronger than Oliver expected.

There was something in that routine Oliver didn't yet identify as what it was. He only knew that when he came down to breakfast and Pier was already there and Riku appeared two minutes later and Nadia walked in with her coat on as if she'd come from outside even though she'd come from the residential hallway — something in that was stable. It was closer to a home than anything Oliver had had in a long time.

He didn't think of it in those terms at the time.

---

The afternoon temperature dropped before four, as always. Oliver picked up the notebook and walked through the inner courtyard corridor toward nowhere in particular — a habit of the last few weeks, a small detour that paid off on the way to anywhere. Someone had left a book open face-down on the chair in the corner of the study room. Oliver had never known whose chair that was, but there was always something of someone's on it.

From the corridor window, the valley was a white expanse that seemed to belong to another scale. Pier had once told him you could see the farms in the distance in summer. Oliver had been here most of the winter and hadn't realized he didn't know the Center in any other season.

In the outer courtyard, two floors down and to the right, Riku and Park were still in session. They'd been out there since before noon — Oliver had seen them leave. From this distance not much was visible: Park standing still, Riku in motion, the Static field bending the air in a radius only visible if you knew what to look for. The cold cut everyone else's sessions short except theirs.

He thought about that a moment longer than he'd thought about anything all day.

What it would be like in spring. The snow retreating from the edges, the valley regaining color in layers. The windows that had been shut for months opening for the first time. The inner courtyard with direct light instead of that diffuse January clarity. Pier with his coffee outside, if there was somewhere to sit. Riku without the cold forcing him to cut his circuits short the moment the field started to disperse. Dara training the Double with more room to work, Asha occupying every possible sound angle the moment it stopped being so cold.

Whether there'd be sessions outside once the weather turned. Whether Yusuf would lift some of the outdoor exposure restriction. Whether Sisi would still be here by then.

Whether he would still be here by then.

The last question had no known answer and he dismissed it anyway with the same ease with which one dismisses a thought that doesn't seem urgent. Of course he'd still be here. The following months already had a shape — training, sessions, the Registry's progression, the notebook filling up with handwriting getting smaller because he kept learning faster than he expected. He'd still be here.

What he hadn't thought until that moment was that assuming that was itself something. That it had been weeks since he'd woken up in the morning and the Center was the day's given context, not the place where he'd been put.

---

The conversation with Riku was short and took place the next day, at lunch.

The long table with its usual noise — Pier and Nadia in a discussion about something administrative, Sabine on her phone, Asha near the end of a story that apparently had a funny ending, though Oliver had only caught the middle. Riku, to Oliver's left, had his hand flat on the table with the index finger slightly raised — the open channel, the latent-Domain posture Oliver had learned not to mention because Riku did it without knowing.

Or knew perfectly well and didn't care. With Riku it was never entirely clear.

When the conversation drifted toward coffee, Riku tilted his head slightly.

"Tuesday, outside," he said quietly. "Did it cost more than it looked?"

"Yeah. The field dispersed before it stabilized. Indoors that adjustment barely exists."

"Right." Riku tapped the table once with his finger. "Maren says thirty percent less output outdoors."

"I think that's optimistic."

Riku looked at him sideways.

"Thanks," he said, in the exact tone of someone not thanking anyone.

Oliver couldn't help it — he laughed, briefly, enough for Riku to hear and not enough for the others to turn. Riku went back to looking at the table with something that wasn't a smile but occupied the same space.

"Next time," he said, "tell me in real time. Not after you've already run the full mental report."

"I thought it would distract you."

"It would distract me less than finding out afterward that I lost thirty percent and nobody told me while it was happening."

"Forty, probably."

Riku didn't answer that. He went back to his coffee.

"Fine," Oliver said.

There was a brief pause. Riku didn't get up yet.

"Tonight," he said, almost as if remembering. "If you've got time."

Oliver took a second.

"Chess rematch?"

"You're down four games."

"Three."

"Tuesday's ended before you formally surrendered. But it ended."

Oliver didn't argue that because it was probably true.

"Tonight," he said.

The general conversation reclaimed them. Oliver looked at the table a moment — Pier's coffee in its usual spot, Asha's gesture as she talked with her hands, Riku's index finger still raised without Riku knowing it. Or with perfect awareness. It remained unclear.

---

The afternoon was blue and cold. Oliver took the east wing stairs even though it wasn't the most direct route. He couldn't have said why he did it — only that the corridor with the window onto the inner courtyard had, at that hour, the kind of light that made the building more like itself.

In the first-floor common room, in passing, Sisi was sitting with Asha on the couch at the far end. Asha was talking with her hands, as always, describing something with the gesture of someone repeating a story that's already found its final shape. Sisi was listening — but not with the measured attention of the first weeks, that constant calibration of how much to give and how much to keep. She was listening a different way. Stiller. More present.

At some point she said something quietly that Oliver didn't catch from the hallway, and Asha laughed, and Sisi didn't quite laugh but something in that direction — a slight opening, an uncalculated instant.

Oliver kept walking without stopping. But he carried that image the rest of the way: Sisi, two weeks after arriving, taking up a couch as if she'd been taking it up for a long time.

---

It was Friday. Yusuf had finished the outdoor circuit before noon, and the building had carried since then that specific calm of afternoons when the group has already met what the day asked of it.

Pier was in the library corridor with his usual coffee and a book open that he wasn't reading. Back straight, arms on the table, the attention of someone who thinks more clearly with something in his hands even when that something isn't the object of the thought.

He looked up before Oliver said anything.

Oliver leaned against the doorway.

"Passive signatures are harder to bring down," he said. "The ones I've been seeing longest. They don't disappear when I stop paying attention."

Pier looked at him with the expression of someone receiving confirmation of something he'd already calculated.

"At first, Weight was something I did." He said it slowly, with the care of someone who'd chosen those words before. "Then it was something I was always doing. Then it was something I couldn't imagine not doing." A pause. "It's not loss of control. It's integration. The difference matters."

"Is there a way to moderate it?"

"Yes." Pier went back to the book. "But first you have to understand what you're moderating. That's where Maren can help you."

Oliver didn't leave right away. He settled more comfortably against the doorway.

"What are you reading?"

Pier looked at the book as if he'd forgotten it.

"Biography." He turned it to show the cover. "Évariste Galois. Nineteenth-century mathematician."

Oliver didn't know what he'd expected, but it wasn't that.

"Any particular reason?"

"He died at twenty, in a duel." Pier turned the book back toward himself. "The night before, he wrote out the foundations of what's now called group theory. He left notes in the margins: *I have no time, I have no time.*" A brief pause. "I'm interested in people who know exactly how much time they have and use it well."

Oliver looked at the book. Then at Pier.

"How many times have you read it?"

"Three." Pier opened the book to his bookmark. "The first to understand the mathematics. The second to understand the duel." He closed it calmly. "This time I don't know yet what for."

It was the first time Oliver had heard Pier say he didn't know something about himself. He didn't say it like a confession — he said it like someone stating a fact still pending classification, with the same neutrality he'd use for anything else. But it was there.

Oliver stepped away from the doorway and sat in the chair across the table. He picked up the first thing within reach on the side shelf without looking at the title — it turned out to be *Flora of the Western Alps*, a nineteen-forties edition, the spine worn and the pages slightly yellowed.

"Have you read it?" he said, because it seemed like exactly the opposite of something he'd want to read.

"Yes." Pier drank from his coffee. "There's a chapter on plants that only grow above eighteen hundred meters. Nothing lower, nothing higher. Only there." A pause. "There aren't many things that can only exist in one exact place in the world. I thought it deserved attention."

Oliver looked at the book. Then at Pier.

The library corridor, with Pier's coffee and the books on the shelves nobody had properly catalogued since the building had been the building, had that kind of stillness that invites you to stay longer than planned.

The conversation went on.

---

Maren had her evaluation lab on the second floor. Oliver arrived at the session with the sense that he already knew the shape of what he was about to hear, if not the exact vocabulary.

"How are you?"

"Riku's, Asha's, and Pier's signatures are there even when I'm not paying attention. This week it's clearer than last."

Maren nodded. Documenting as he spoke, with the rhythm of someone confirming something already measured another way.

"I picked it up three weeks ago in the baseline readings." She set down the pen. "It's not degradation, Oliver. It's the opposite. The passive-processing threshold has shifted. The signatures you've spent months reading have crossed a point where they no longer need active activation to be present. The Registry is processing more than you're deciding."

Oliver thought about the three nights searching for his own Odem. About the constant pull outward.

"Is it reversible?"

"I don't know," Maren said, without softening it. "The earlier cases didn't reach this phase with enough documentation." She looked at him. "What I do know is that there's a technique: not to switch it off, but to actively choose where attention goes at any given moment."

Oliver thought about Park. Outside, last week — *body or Odem? Which of the two do you use when you read someone's signature?* The question had seemed tangential then. Not now.

"Park planted something like that last week," he said. "Without explaining why."

Maren looked at him with the attention Oliver already recognized as *filing this away.*

"Park got to the right exercise from what he sees," she said, almost to herself. "Not from what he was told."

She set down the pen.

"One more thing. Eighteen weeks — you're reaching this point earlier than any documented case. The closest one took fourteen months." A pause. "That means we can walk through the process with you in real time. There's no exact precedent, but there's room. And there are people who know what they're looking at."

Oliver left the lab when the valley was already black and shapeless behind the north windows.

In the corridor, Riku's signature arrived from the residential wing without Oliver having decided it.

He noticed it. Put his hand on the wall. The stone was cold. He put his attention there — on the cold, on the stone, on the sound of his own footsteps.

Riku's signature dropped a little. It didn't disappear. But it dropped.

It was enough to know there was something to learn.

He slept well that night.
