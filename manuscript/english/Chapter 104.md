# Chapter 104 — Another Angle

---

Maren called before Voss had finished sitting down at the third-floor window, in front of which he'd been standing longer than he remembered deciding to — he'd simply stopped there, at some point between leaving his office and not arriving anywhere else.

"You don't sound good," she said, no preamble, the line carrying the background noise of St. Gotthard, footsteps and a door closing somewhere behind her voice.

"I haven't slept well."

"That's not what I said."

Voss didn't answer right away. Six years of working together had taught him that with Maren, silence didn't read as evasion — it read, correctly, as him deciding how much to say.

"The hearing date still isn't set," he said, finally. "Furrer's taking her time. She thinks the longer she drags it out, the more it rattles me."

"Is it working?"

"Not the way she thinks it is."

Maren didn't ask what way it was working. She stayed on the line a moment longer, in no hurry to fill the silence with anything, and hung up with the same economy she'd used to call — no formal goodbye, just the cut tone where a voice had been.

---

Urs Baumann ran the AMAT-Switzerland office with the same dull efficiency he supposedly ran his own life with — punctual, no surprises, no visible ambition beyond keeping things working. Voss had known him since before either of them held the position they held now. They owed each other nothing. That, oddly, was what made the conversation worth having.

"I'm not here to ask you to stick your neck out," Voss said, sitting across Baumann's tidy desk. "I'm here to ask for what you were already going to do anyway, said out loud where someone can hear it."

"And what was I already going to do?"

"Confirm that the Valais case has run well under my technical supervision. Not an opinion on the investigation. An operational fact, verifiable, that Furrer can't rewrite."

Baumann took just long enough to think it over that it was clear he really was thinking it over, not performing deliberation.

"I can say that," he said. "It's true, besides, which helps. But don't expect it to be enough on its own, Viktor. I control Switzerland. Furrer's playing across all of Geneva."

"I know."

"Then you know this is one piece, not a solution."

Voss nodded, already on his feet. He hadn't expected anything else — one real piece was worth more than a big promise with nothing behind it, and Baumann, if he was anything, was substance without promises.

---

Hana was waiting for him in the lobby when he got back from Baumann's office, her travel bag still at her feet, as if she'd come straight from the train without stopping anywhere first. Voss took a full second to place her out of context — Hana belonged to St. Gotthard the way she belonged to a screen or a task list, not to a Geneva lobby in the flesh.

"What are you doing here?" he said, not quite managing to keep it from sounding like the real surprise it was.

"I took three days off," Hana said, with the same economy she used organizing anything else. "Nobody at St. Gotthard knows yet, except Maren, who's covering for me. My mother sits on the AMAT-EU council, Spanish branch. I asked her to see you, and she said yes, but only if I was there too."

Voss went still a second longer — not because the council detail was impossible to guess, but because in six years he'd never once heard her mention it, not even in passing.

"I didn't know."

"It doesn't usually come up," Hana said, already walking toward the elevator, taking for granted that he'd follow. "But if you think it would help for someone on the council to hear a version of events that hasn't been filtered through Furrer's office, you've got twenty minutes. Her office is in the annex building."

"Why are you doing this?" Voss said. "The leave. The trip. You could have just given me the contact."

"I wanted to see my mother anyway," Hana said, without slowing her pace. "I'd been putting it off for months." And then, without any shift in register, as if it were the least important part of the sentence: "And a friend needed backup. I couldn't think of a better reason to finally take the days."

Hana's mother's office was smaller than the title suggested, two chairs facing the desk and no decoration that revealed anything personal. The woman listened to Voss's summary — ten minutes, no embellishment, the operational facts ahead of any defense — with the same closed expression Voss recognized, without being able to name it, as something inherited by her daughter. At the end, she promised nothing firm. She said she'd keep it in mind when the matter reached the council, that she appreciated someone taking the trouble to tell it in person instead of sending it in writing, and that Hana, sitting by the door through the whole meeting, hadn't said a word until they were outside.

"Well?" Voss asked, already in the hallway.

"That's the closest I've ever seen her come to saying yes to something without fully committing," Hana said. "With her, that's a lot."

Voss had no ready answer for that. He just said he was grateful, genuinely, and she nodded once, brisk, before picking the bag back up from where she'd left it by the office door.

Two pieces. Neither one, alone, changed anything. Together, they still didn't quite add up — he knew it without needing anyone to confirm it, the same instinct he used to gauge proportions in a room without meaning to.

---

The main hallway of the operations wing was, that afternoon, busier than Voss had seen it in weeks — not the usual administrative traffic, but something with real urgency behind it: two stretchers crossing toward the internal infirmary, someone shouting triage coordinates in German and French almost at once, a young agent leaning against the wall with a badly splinted arm, waiting his turn without complaint.

"Reykjanes contingent," someone said, passing by, without Voss asking. "Containment operation, went bad before it went right. Six wounded, none serious, a lot of noise."

Voss stepped back against the wall, gauging distances by instinct — the corridor ran eighteen meters, its effective width cut to barely two by the traffic — and that was when he felt it, before he saw it: his breath caught mid-inhale, one full second of air that never quite finished arriving, as if the hallway itself had thickened on the way to his lungs. On the admissions counter, a half-full glass of water trembled with no one touching it, a ring of concentric waves spreading out from the center for no visible physical reason.

"God," said the agent with the splinted arm, quietly, to no one in particular. "That's him, isn't it? That has to be him."

No one else needed to confirm it. The man came in through the north end of the hallway with no visible uniform, unhurried, and the whole space seemed to recalculate itself around him before he'd crossed half the corridor — agents stepping aside without consciously deciding to, conversations dropping in volume with no order given, two stretcher-bearers picking up their pace without needing to. Field scars left uncovered. A gaze that assessed before deciding whether anything else was worth doing.

Voss had heard the name through channels he shouldn't have had access to read. He'd never seen him in person, and nothing he'd read had prepared him for the difference between knowing something exists and feeling it compress the air of a whole room without its owner appearing to make any effort at all.

*Ji-ho.*

He crossed the entire hallway without stopping for anyone, without needing to, and disappeared through a side door Voss knew led to the maximum-discretion debrief rooms. The whole corridor took a full second, once the door closed, to move at normal speed again — the glass of water was still trembling, more slowly, by the time Voss finally stopped watching it.

Voss stood there thinking, longer than the scene objectively warranted, about how little he actually knew of how much weight AMAT's informal hierarchy really carried — the one that showed up on no organizational chart, the one he himself had spent thirty years helping build without noticing how much of it had slipped out of his hands.

---

"Viktor."

The voice came from behind him, in the same hallway, nearly empty again now, the contingent's urgency absorbed back into the building. Marcus Hale wore the same suit as always, the one that never seemed to wrinkle despite whatever transatlantic flights he must rack up, and the smile he used when he wanted something to look casual without quite managing it.

Voss knew him more by reputation than up close — regional director of AMAT-NA for eight years now, the kind of position that almost never showed up in Geneva in person because it didn't need to: everything he wanted arrived on its own, through channels he'd built himself with decades of patience. Cartwright answered to him, if only on paper; in practice, Cartwright managed a small corner of a territory Hale controlled whole, almost never needing to touch it directly.

"Marcus."

"Bad time to swing by, huh?" Hale said, with a vague gesture toward the hallway. "Geneva's gotten interesting lately. Your situation, the Reykjanes thing. Ji-ho paying a visit, no less."

"Did you come to see him?"

"I came for other reasons. Seeing him was a bonus." Hale fell into step beside Voss, matching his pace, without anyone quite inviting him to. "I heard about Furrer. Cartwright called me a few days ago, actually. Put in a good word for you — said you two were friends, or something close to it, though the word didn't seem to sit entirely comfortably with him."

"He didn't need to do that."

"No, he didn't," Hale said. "But he did, and that told me something anyway: that somewhere, someone cares enough about what happens to you to spend a favor on it without being asked. That's worth more than people think."

"Everyone's heard about Furrer."

"It's a shame. Your framework's the only genuinely useful thing to come out of Geneva in years." A pause, calculated, the kind Voss recognized because it was a tool he used himself. "The US could speak in your favor. Us, and a few others who tend to line up with us when it counts. Not free, of course. Nothing ever is, at this level."

"What would you ask for?"

"Nothing right now," Hale said, smiling in a way that made the word "nothing" sound like exactly the opposite. "I just want you to remember, when it's time to talk about real deployment for your star kid, who was willing to help when nobody else wanted to get their hands dirty."

Voss didn't give him the satisfaction of a visible reaction.

"Oliver isn't a chip on a table, Marcus."

"Never said he was." Hale stopped where the hallway opened onto the main lobby, making clear without saying so that the conversation was over. "I'm just saying we've all got things that matter to us more than we admit out loud. Think about it, Viktor. No rush. Not yet."

He left without waiting for an answer, with the same casual ease he'd arrived with, leaving Voss alone in a hallway that already sounded like any other office day again.

---

The phone rang just as he sat down. Hana, her voice more relaxed than usual, somewhere quiet in the background — still in Geneva, making the most of her three days.

"I put the report in my mother's hands myself," she said, no preamble, as always. "They're reading it together over dinner. That's all she's promising."

"That's more than I had this morning."

"Viktor." Hana paused, the first one Voss could remember in six years of purely operational conversations. "Are you okay?"

The question threw him more than anything Hale had said in the whole hallway.

"Not especially," he said, with the same economy as always, because lying to Hana after what she'd just offered him would have wasted the one real honesty he'd had all afternoon.

"Good," she said. "Send me whatever you need me to read. Good night."

She hung up with no formal close, the same habit Voss recognized in himself whenever someone looked at him too closely.

Voss laid out on the table — not on paper, only in his head, ordered the way he ordered any structure — the three pieces he had: Baumann, a verifiable fact, solid but insufficient. Hana, a channel to the council that might not arrive in time. Hale, the biggest of the three, and the only one with a price he still didn't fully know.

None of them, alone, gave him back the Valais case. Together, maybe. But for the first time in thirty years, Voss wasn't sure whether he was solving a problem or simply spreading it out among more people than he could control.

He thought of Oliver, in St. Gotthard, with no idea his name had already been used as currency that afternoon in a Geneva hallway.

He didn't call him. There was nothing yet to tell him that wouldn't scare him before it was time.
