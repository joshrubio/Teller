# Chapter 54 — The One Born of Blood

Casimira never brought up the half-lit cigar again, or the answer she hadn't gotten. She simply told Park, that same afternoon, to be ready to leave before midnight, and from there everything left of the two days of houses, lines, and dominoes tables closed behind them like a door.

They reached the foothills of Sorte a little after midnight, two nights after Roa's collection run, when the forest still held that damp cold that not even the hottest day ever fully drove out. Casimira insisted on walking the last stretch — no engine, no light, only Park's flashlight cupped in his hand until he no longer needed it.

Salcedo had stayed behind, the pickup hidden between two bends of the dirt road, and Ronald with him — the only time in the whole arc that Park asked him directly not to follow past that point. Ronald didn't argue. Something in the way Casimira had spoken that morning, when she'd finally agreed to take them, told everyone that this part wasn't for people without training, however much they wanted to come.

The climb took over an hour — terrain no motorcycle or pickup could have taken without announcing itself a kilometer off, roots thick as arms, the ground still soft from the previous night's damp. Casimira walked like she'd known every inch of that mountain since childhood, stopping before each clearing to listen for something Park couldn't always hear.

"Down there," she said, stopping behind a moss-covered boulder. "Where there used to be a coffee farm. Abandoned for years. Or that's what everyone who doesn't know what happens there at night believes."

From the cover of the boulder, the ground sloped down toward a clearing cut by force — trees felled, not fallen in a storm — and at the center, an unfinished block structure ringed by a makeshift wire fence and two pickup trucks with their lights off. Park counted seven men visible in that first sweep alone, uniforms mismatched again, three wearing the same patch he'd torn off two nights before — more people than he'd expected to find in a place that was supposed to go unnoticed. A generator hummed somewhere behind the structure, feeding a pair of floodlights aimed inward, not outward — no one down there was worried about something from the forest wanting in. What worried them, Park understood, watching how the men were positioned, was something inside wanting out.

The air, even at this distance, carried the same dense weight Park had been noticing since his first night at Casimira's house — but here, closer to the source, it wasn't a constant background hum. It rose and fell, slowly, in a rhythm Park struggled to name until Casimira said it first.

"It's breathing," she whispered. "Like a sleeping animal. Like a sleeping child, really, if you forget everything else."

---

He saw her before he fully understood what he was seeing.

A girl, sitting on a cut log near the entrance to the structure, her legs hanging without reaching the ground. Dressed in clothes someone had bought thinking of an ordinary girl — shorts, a T-shirt with a faded picture of something no longer recognizable — and a man standing beside her, not touching her, but close, guarding her the way you'd guard something valuable and dangerous at once.

"Casimira," Park said, without lowering the binoculars. "Tell me it isn't what I'm seeing."

Casimira didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice came out smaller than Park had heard it in two visits.

"It's exactly what you're seeing," she said. "Nine years old, maybe ten. They've watched her grow up here since she was a baby — some say they bought her, others that she was given, others that she was born already meant for this and no one asked whose she was. Doesn't matter which is true. The result's the same."

Her hair was tied back in two crooked braids, done by someone without much practice at it. A fresh scrape on her knee, the kind any girl gets from playing, that no one in that clearing seemed to have noticed or tended to. Park watched her swing her feet against the log, a rhythm with no music to it, and for one entire moment he wished, with an intensity that surprised even himself, that Casimira was wrong.

---

The man beside the girl said something Park didn't catch, and she nodded, slowly, with the kind of obedience a child learns when the alternative to obeying has, at some point, been something worse than obeying. She got up from the log. She walked toward the structure with the same short steps as any girl her age, and for one entire second, watching her walk, Park felt none of the dense weight he'd been noticing since his first day in Yaracuy.

Then she stopped, halfway there, and turned her head toward the boulder where they were hidden — not toward Park, not toward Casimira, toward something a little beyond them, as if she'd heard a sound no one else had made.

The weight came back all at once, denser than ever, and Park felt the air itself compress for an instant around her before it eased and she kept walking, as if whatever she'd sensed hadn't been worth looking into.

"That," Casimira whispered, "was her getting spooked by a shadow. Imagine what she does when she's really scared."

They didn't have to imagine long. One of the men near the structure dropped something metal — a tool, maybe an empty magazine — and the sound, amplified by the stillness of the early morning, reached where she was walking. The girl didn't scream or run. She went still for one entire second, and the ground under her feet cracked open in a circle half a meter wide, silent, with no shockwave to go with it — just the earth giving way, as if something beneath it had clenched a fist without the girl moving a single muscle to order it. The man who'd dropped the tool stood staring at the crack. He'd clearly spent months trying not to look at it directly.

No one in the clearing said anything about it. Everyone, Park understood, already knew this happened. It was part of working there, the same way heat or mosquitoes were part of working anywhere else.

"She's used to people staring when that happens," Casimira said, her voice barely a thread. "That's what scares me most of all. That it's normal for her too, by now."

---

They pulled back another hundred meters before Casimira spoke again, her voice still low even though there was no longer any way anyone in the clearing could hear them.

"I've spent days hoping you'd tell me there's a good version of this waiting underneath," she said. "A real little girl, frightened, who could be taken out of there and healed with time and love, the way any child who's seen things she shouldn't have seen gets healed."

"And there isn't?"

"Not the way you're picturing it," Casimira said. "Whatever was born of ten years of blood isn't a girl with something bad laid over her that can be peeled away. She was born this way. The sacrifice didn't damage something good that was there before. The sacrifice is what made her, point by point, from day one. There's nothing clean underneath waiting to get out."

Park stood in silence a moment, the image still fresh — the girl walking in short steps, her head turning toward a shadow that was almost nothing at all.

"That doesn't make her less of a girl," he said, finally.

"No," Casimira said. "That's why it's worse, not better. Both things are true at the same time, agent. You're going to have to learn to carry both if you want to stay sane this week."

AMAT had protocol for almost everything Park had faced so far — Levels, classifications, a whole language built so an agent would know, before acting, what kind of thing stood in front of him and what kind of response was correct. There was no entry in any manual for this. There was no Level that read *target with a child's body, moral weight separate from tactical*. He was going to have to decide this with whatever was in his own head, not with what the institution had trained him to look for there.

That didn't calm him. If anything, it was the one thing all that early morning left him truly unsettled about.

---

They saw Roa in person around one in the morning — a man past sixty, uniform with no visible insignia, gray hair cropped close to the scalp, who walked to the structure with the quiet confidence of someone used to no one contradicting him in any room he entered. He walked with a cane that didn't look like he actually needed it, more a symbol of authority than a real aid. He stopped for a moment by the door, said something to one of his men, and gestured toward the inside with a motion that carried no affection and no fear either — the kind of gesture a man makes toward an expensive machine that still hasn't finished justifying the investment.

One of his men answered him with something, and Roa shook his head, once, sharp, before going in himself. He didn't come back out in the half hour Park and Casimira stayed watching.

Two of the younger guards, posted near the fence, talked between themselves low enough that most of it was lost to the distance, but the wind carried a few words up to the boulder.

"...says it's almost ready," Park caught, one of their voices, nervous in a way that had nothing to do with the early morning cold. "Says after this moon they won't need anything more from us."

"They've been saying that for two years," the other answered, unconvinced. "What I want is a transfer somewhere else before they decide they don't need anything more from me either."

Casimira didn't need Park to translate the weight of that.

"Not even the people guarding her believe they're going to stay alive to see what she becomes," she said. "That should tell you something too, agent."

"He's the one deciding what to do with her," Casimira said. "Not the one who made her, exactly. The one who inherited the job when the others got too afraid to keep going."

"Do you know what he wants her for?"

"A weapon that obeys," Casimira said. "That's what he thinks he's raising. I don't think he's understood yet, not even after all these years, that what he has down there isn't going to obey anyone much longer."

---

Park's phone buzzed before he could answer her. He stepped a few meters away, his voice low, and recognized the prefix before he picked up.

"Agent," Cartwright said, without preamble, her voice different from the first call in Budapest, tighter, faster. "What I told you was coming in weeks or days. It's today. It started just a few minutes ago — two-oh-one, local time, if anyone ever asks you the exact hour."

"How much time do I have?"

"None I can give you with any certainty," Cartwright said. "What I can tell you is that whatever happens on that mountain in the next few hours is going to be buried under something much bigger before noon. That's the only window of discretion you're going to get. After today, anything out of the ordinary in this region draws eyes neither of us wants on it."

"What do I do with whatever I find, if I can't wait for a better plan?"

"What you've been doing all week, agent," Cartwright said. "Decide with what you have, not what you'd like to have. I can't send you anything more than this call. Everything else that happens on that mountain today is going to be entirely yours."

She stayed on the line a second, as if about to add something else, and didn't.

"Good luck," she said, finally, and hung up before Park could answer that either.

Park looked toward the clearing, toward the girl who'd already vanished inside the structure, toward Roa talking to his men as if he had all the time in the world.

Casimira was watching him when he turned around, something in her face already guessing part of what he'd just heard.

"Today?" she asked.

"Today," Park confirmed. "Before dawn. Before the sun's fully up."

They made their way down to where Salcedo and Ronald were waiting with the pickup, neither of them saying more than they had to. Salcedo read Park's face before he said a word.

"When?" he asked.

"Now," Park said. "In the next few hours, before it's fully light. I need everything you have on Roa's guard shifts, and I need it now."

Ronald jumped down from the hood where he'd been sitting, days of waiting turning, all at once, into something with a shape and a time.

"What about the others?" he asked. "Wilkins, Freddy, the rest."

"Not this time," Park said. "What's down there isn't like anything we've faced so far. I'm not asking them to walk into that with me."

Ronald didn't argue, but he didn't promise to stay behind either — and Park, looking at him, decided not to press for a promise he knew, either way, wasn't going to hold much past sunrise.

The sky, over the mountain, was only just starting to lose the closed black of night.
