# Chapter 74 — Decision

---

Sisi's room had the desk by the window, the bed unmade to one side, and a small backpack on the floor.

Sisi was sitting on the edge of the desk, not the chair. Coat on. She swept the room with her eyes — the walls, the window, the backpack — the gesture of someone taking a last inventory of what she's leaving behind.

When she finished, she looked at him.

Oliver closed the door.

---

"I talked to Voss," he said.

"I know."

She hadn't been in the corridor. But she knew.

"The protocol isn't going to change. Not right now." Oliver waited a second before continuing. "There's a working group in Geneva. He proposed it. Nine months without an approved methodology."

Sisi nodded. A small movement, no irony. She looked toward the window and let it land — the weight of it, the distance between what existed and what she'd need, all of it without separating one from the other.

"Nine months," she said, to the glass.

Oliver waited.

"Nine months waiting for a system to learn how to read something that already exists. Fourteen, too, since someone first proposed trying. A year, six months, and eleven days."

She didn't say it as an accusation. She said it as a tally — exactly what it was. Then she looked at him again.

"How long have you known what was going to happen tonight?"

"Since he reached the perimeter." The verifying look, direct. "I read it from a distance. Not his words — what's underneath."

"And you?"

Sisi didn't answer right away.

"I had doubts," she said finally. "That's why I wanted to ask you."

Oliver said nothing.

"If you were leaving," she said, "it would mean something different. You've been watching this longer than I have. So — you?"

He knew she already knew. Not the outcome — she knew which part of him was going to give, and toward what. She'd read it the same way she'd read Kaspar, the same way she'd read him the first time. Still, she asked. She needed to hear it.

"I'm staying," he said.

Sisi nodded. The same small movement.

"Then I already know what I needed to know."

She got down from the desk. Picked up the backpack from the floor. She didn't say goodbye.

Oliver didn't move from the doorway as she passed him.

---

Kaspar stood in the main hallway of the residential wing, hands in his pockets, with the calm of someone who's arrived early and doesn't mind waiting. Tomás stood two meters back, one shoulder against the wall, his breathing still ragged.

Sisi stopped in front of Kaspar.

It was the first time she'd seen him without the filter of what she read. She'd had him in her radius for days — she'd felt the architecture of what he thought, the way he'd spent forty years being the same thing with no internal contradictions that suggested imposture. Now she had him in front of her. He was old. More than the body let on.

"You have a question," Kaspar said. Not a question.

"One," Sisi said.

Kaspar waited.

"What's wrong with me?"

It wasn't a question about the plan or the exit route. It was the question AMAT hadn't been able to answer in nine months — the only one that mattered.

Kaspar looked at her for a second.

"I don't know," he said.

Sisi didn't answer.

"I know what you do. I know you've been doing it for years, and that the cost isn't in the body but somewhere else — in the weight of what you touch, in what happens after every time you use it. I know the system you're in has no name for what you are because it has nothing external to measure." He didn't say it as criticism or as a promise. "But what you actually are — no. Nobody knows that yet."

"Then you can't help me."

"I can help you find out without a protocol deciding first whether you've earned the right to try."

Sisi looked down. Not with doubt — with the gesture of someone letting something land before going on.

"You've been out forty years," she said.

"Forty-two."

"And it works?"

"Depends what you call working." Kaspar took a hand out of his pocket, did nothing with it. "I'm still here. I've seen what the system was like forty years ago and what it's like now. I've seen what happens to the people who stay — not all of them, not always — and I've seen what it's like to live without anyone telling you what threshold you have to clear to earn a review."

"And the ones who've left with you?"

Kaspar didn't look away.

"Some live better outside. Some don't. That's true too."

It was the honest answer. Sisi had known it before he said it — she'd read it before it existed in words — but hearing it changed something in its weight. A man who'd come to convince her would have found a way to soften it.

"And me?" she said.

"I think you're someone who's spent years waiting for a system to learn how to read something that already exists." The same flat cadence as always. "I could be wrong."

Sisi looked up.

"You're not wrong."

---

Oliver watched them go down the corridor.

He stayed at the top of it for as long as it took to do the count. Park in the north — the sustained impact of someone who'd spent longer than was reasonable containing something that wouldn't stop, with no one having formally asked him to. Riku, splint creaking against his palm before the channel would even open, three weeks from his discharge. Maren out in the courtyard with no protocol to justify it, keeping the secret that she, of all people, wasn't supposed to know. Voss in a corridor at nine at night giving an answer that cost him to give, and adding, without Oliver having asked, that he'd spent nine months pushing something from the inside.

Not AMAT. People inside AMAT.

That had always been different. He simply hadn't seen it clearly enough until now.

He decided he wasn't going to stand still.

He crossed the residential corridor toward the main wing with the Registry open — Kaspar's signature active in the overlapping layers, unmistakable, moving north. He followed them.

He passed Lev's room.

The door was ajar. The room, empty.
Oliver stopped for two steps.

Then he kept going.

---

Voss heard the alarm at eleven fifteen and took forty seconds to reach the center of the administrative wing.

Not the north access, not the control room. The building's geometric center — the point he'd calculated his first week at the Center and hadn't needed to use until tonight.

He pressed his hand against the stone.

The Domain opened.

It wasn't perception in the usual sense. It was knowledge — the whole building accessible as a system of catalogued components: every block in its position, every beam at its angle, every wall carrying the exact weight it had carried for four centuries. He'd learned it in his first weeks, walking the corridors with his hand on the old stone, piece by piece, the way someone learns the blueprint of something they intend to operate.

He saw three signatures moving toward the north access.

He started working.

He shifted the side wall of the north corridor — not bending it, not deforming it: moving it. Four blocks running toward the center, smooth, silent, sealing off the direct access.

Tomás reached the wall. He passed through it without stopping — inorganic matter didn't exist for him, had never existed. On the other side he turned, leaned back through the stone as if it were butter, and emerged in the hallway where Kaspar and Sisi were waiting.

Kaspar took one hand. Sisi took the other.

The Domain extended to all three. They crossed.

Voss pulled the ceiling beams of the next hallway apart — not to the floor, repositioned diagonally, sealing the exit corridor with two meters of ancient stone that hadn't been there twenty seconds earlier.

Tomás grabbed the other two by the arm, jumped. The three of them passed through.

Kaspar looked at Tomás — his breathing high, his body holding up something that wasn't just his own weight. The Domain extended over three bodies instead of one cost more every time.

"You need to rest," Kaspar said, without raising his voice.

Tomás didn't answer. They kept going.

---

In the east wing, three fronts were converging.

Pier saw them arrive from the crossing corridor — it was the colony, advancing at different angles with no visible coordination between them. Small groups, six or seven units, moving in parallel toward the same corridor by different routes.

They weren't going for containment. They were going for dispersal.

"Northeast," Pier said. "Nadia, south. Asha, the floor at the crossing."

Nadia was already watching the south corridor. Impulse shot out straight — a wall of force that caught the cluster at mid-distance and threw it against the wall with a dry impact. Three units fell. The rest split into four smaller fragments, disoriented for three seconds, and started to regroup.

"They're still coming," Nadia said.

Pier didn't answer. He opened the Domain over the northeast corridor and multiplied the local weight — five times normal gravity. The creatures in the cluster flattened against the floor, their movement reduced to a crawl.

Then the corridor changed.

Not like a crack, not like a fault line — the movement too controlled for anything those things could do. Four blocks from the north wall ran toward the center, smooth, silent, and the northeast corridor shrank to half its width. The creatures Pier had pinned to the floor were now in a different volume than the one he'd calculated. The field lost half the cluster.

Pier recognized the movement. Someone was operating the building from inside.

"Voss," he said. He'd only seen him do that a handful of times, and never at that scale.

Nobody answered.

Asha had her palm on the stone at the crossing. She tightened the tension of the material under the central corridor — the stone responded by compressing on itself, the ground hostile before anything even reached it. The fragments from the south trying to cross it met a resistance that wasn't physical. Some fell back.

Then she felt the stone move under her hand.

It wasn't her Domain. It was the building. Another block shifting two corridors away — the tension she'd applied to the floor losing coherence because the floor was no longer the same piece it had been ten seconds ago.

"The south corridor's going to change," Asha said.

It changed before she finished the sentence. An angular block turned at the east access and opened a gap where none had been. The fragments from the south found the new access before anyone could reposition.

"Dara," Pier said.

The Double appeared in the gap — moving toward the fragments with enough solidity to register as a priority target. The colony turned toward it. The real Dara fell back four meters.

Three seconds. The Double held three seconds before the first fragment tore through it.

It dissolved.

At that moment the colony lost orientation. Not because of the Double — because of something happening elsewhere in the building. The angles the clusters were arriving from no longer matched any corridor they'd registered. Three seconds of drift.

"Petra," Dara said.

Petra had the target in range. The reconstructed south cluster — eight units, mass comparable to the equipment crate in the storeroom by the east access. Swap. The cluster appeared on the other side of the sealed door. The crate appeared in the hallway.

The south corridor stood empty.

Pier recalibrated the gravity field to the northeast corridor's new volume. The creatures that had escaped when the hallway changed were coming back — uncoordinated, still disoriented, but coming back.

"The building's going to keep changing," Pier said. "We work with what's there."

Asha squeezed the floor one degree tighter without answering. And it shattered.

---

In the east wing, the colony lost orientation for several seconds — the angles it was arriving from no longer matched any known corridor. Park noticed the front shift without understanding why. He adjusted.

Voss didn't see Park or Riku. He saw the space they occupied — the pressure the colony was exerting against the architecture, the volume each unit claimed at its various points. He also saw Kaspar's signature in the north wing: ancient, without precedent in anything the Architecture Domain had ever encountered, occupying space in a way that matched no human body he knew.

He slid the angular block at the north corner — three tons of stone rotating on its axis until it sealed the access corridor from within. A piece that had sat at that exact angle for four hundred years, and now sat at another.

Tomás didn't wait. He grabbed both of them wherever he could and carried them to the other side.

The building had stood for four centuries. It was Voss's natural element — every component in its place for structural reasons he knew with the same clarity Oliver used to read Odem. And he could reorganize them. Not all at once, not indefinitely — but enough, in the right order, to make the path outside a different problem every time Tomás solved it.

The problem was Tomás. Voss could block Kaspar and Sisi. He couldn't block someone for whom inorganic matter didn't exist. But he could multiply the number of times Tomás needed to extend the Domain past himself — three bodies instead of one, at every crossing, every new wall.

All he could do was wear him down.

---

Oliver reached the north wing two minutes later.

Fingers protruded from the stone, no crack, no opening. On the other side: Kaspar, Sisi with the backpack, Tomás's breathing audible through the material.

The corridor behind him no longer had the geometry Oliver remembered. The proportions were different. Something had been working the building from inside.

Kaspar looked at him.

"Still time," he said.

Oliver looked at Sisi. Sisi looked back.

"Come back," Oliver said.

Sisi didn't answer. She held his gaze for a moment.

Then she closed her hand around Tomás's wrist. Kaspar gripped the forearm emerging from the stone. The Domain extended to all three. The stone gave way for them, and they were gone.
