# Chapter 83 — Terms

---

Dinner ended earlier than Oliver expected, or maybe he was the one who finished before the others, without real hunger, his head still on the conversation with Petra that hadn't gone anywhere. He excused himself with some vague line about needing air, and nobody pushed to come along — the whole group had already had enough of a day that nobody questioned wanting to be alone for a while.

He wasn't sure what he was looking for when he started walking through the west wing, past the mess hall, toward a part of the complex they hadn't had a reason to visit yet. It was there, from the far end of an unmarked corridor — the kind the instructor with the tablet had warned them about the first day — that he saw Dax step out through a half-open door, too far away to exchange a word even if he'd wanted to.

There was no greeting, not even the possibility of one. Dax walked off in the other direction without looking back, recognizable even at that distance by his uneven breathing and by the way his shirt clung to his back in a way ordinary physical effort didn't quite explain. Oliver waited until his footsteps faded completely before approaching the door he'd left ajar.

It was a secondary training room, smaller than the main pit — mats stacked against one wall, a long mirror covering the other, no windows, no visible camera, though Oliver wouldn't have bet that meant anything. Empty now. His, for the next few minutes, if he wanted it.

He shut the door behind him and sat down in the center, legs crossed, phone on the floor in front of him with the timer already set. Eight minutes was Park's number, the one he'd used every time since the first. Tonight he didn't send the warning text before he started. There was nobody on the other end waiting to tell him when to stop, and for the first time since he'd arrived at St. Gotthard, that felt less like recklessness than like deciding something for himself.

The buildup started where it always started, a weight gathering in the center of his chest that still had no proper name of his own — Park called it something else, in Korean, a word he'd never fully explained. Oliver let it rise, without rushing it, counting breaths instead of seconds. At eight minutes, the point where he always stopped, he went still for a moment, his hand already halfway to the phone, and didn't move it any farther than that.

He let it keep rising.

At nine minutes he felt the first real warning — not the soft threshold from other nights, but something closer to a seam pulling tight across his sternum, his body starting to protest a weight that no longer fit where he was storing it. He forced himself not to move, not to let go yet, counting every extra second as a number only he was ever going to know existed. At ten and a half minutes the shaking reached his hands — not the old kind, the effort of holding on, but a new one, finer, that he recognized, a second late, as real fear of what would happen if it got away from him with nobody there to help him release it clean.

He stopped there. Not all at once — he let the buildup drain slowly, the way he always did, but his hands were shaking for real by the time his chest finally emptied out.

He stayed sitting a moment longer, breathing, aware of the dull ache that had settled in behind his sternum, different from the exhaustion of other nights. It wasn't progress he could show anyone — no message to send Park this time, nothing to confirm — but he'd stayed two and a half minutes past where he'd ever dared go alone, and the fact that it hurt afterward, instead of scaring him, told him he'd come closer to a real limit than a borrowed one.

---

The notice was taped to the door of their room before breakfast, a printed sheet with no letterhead: seven names, a time, a room. Oliver recognized his own, Riku's, Dara's. No one else from the Valais group.

"Leveling," Riku said, reading over Oliver's shoulder, without much enthusiasm. "Great. Just what I needed to feel better this morning."

Nobody argued with the word, though nobody repeated it in front of Petra or Pier at breakfast.

The room turned out to be the same one Oliver had used the night before, the mats now spread out on the floor instead of stacked against the wall, forming a wide circle. When they arrived, others were already waiting — not many, seven or eight, none from Dax's group or Johanna's that Oliver recognized, faces he hadn't seen up close until now, all wearing the same wary look of someone who knows they've been singled out for something, even if nobody's said it in those words.

The instructor arrived last.

She wasn't in the gray uniform like the man from orientation, but training clothes with no insignia, and she walked to the center of the circle with a confidence that didn't match what her eyes revealed once they had her up close — a pale, uniform opacity, unfocused, that didn't track anyone in particular while she talked.

"Anka," she said, with no more preamble than the man in gray had used on the first day, and added nothing else to the name — no surname, no title, as if the word alone were enough for everything there was to know about her.

Nobody asked where the name came from. Someone behind Oliver murmured "AMAT, Anka," quiet enough not to be a comment aimed at anyone, chasing maybe the easy joke of the coincidence, and she registered it anyway — the smallest turn of her head in that direction, one nobody else caught.

"I'm going to ask you for something simple," she said, once the circle was complete. "Stay standing. That's all. There's no prize for lasting longer than the person next to you, and no penalty for buckling sooner than you'd like. I just want to see where each of your limits is today, so I know where to start."

Nobody asked exactly what that meant. Nobody had to ask for long.

She started with the candidate nearest the door, a thin boy Oliver didn't know, and did nothing visible — no raised hands, no change of expression — just turned her head toward him with the same unsettling confidence she'd shown walking to the center of the circle. Oliver, still at the far end of the semicircle, felt the echo of it anyway: a diffuse weight in his chest, no clear direction, like standing too close to a speaker nobody else seemed to notice. The boy lasted four seconds before his knees gave out — not a faint, something closer to a sudden weight his body hadn't had time to learn how to carry.

"Good," Anka said, without further comment, and moved to the next one.

Riku lasted longer — maybe from months of passive exposure to his own Domain, maybe just stubbornness — but he still ended up with a knee on the floor, his breath cut short like someone had laid an open palm on his chest and pushed, slow, unhurried, knowing there was no need to rush. Dara lasted less time, but got back up fast, her face angrier than embarrassed. With every candidate, the echo in Oliver's chest grew a little less diffuse, the source working its way around the circle like something he could almost point to.

When it was finally his turn, the sensation stopped being an echo and became a focal point. He felt the pressure arrive before Anka had finished turning her head — not as sound, not as image, but as an actual weight settling right below his sternum, with a clear direction, a point source that could, in theory, be located if you knew which way to turn. His breath cut off mid-inhale, as if the air itself had thickened on the way to his lungs. The hair on his forearms rose with no cold to explain it. He counted three short breaths before his legs started to give, and a fourth before they gave out completely, his knee hitting the mat harder than he'd planned.

Anka didn't say "good" this time. She stayed turned toward him a second longer — toward him, and nobody else — before moving on to the next name on the list.

When the last candidate got back to his feet, Anka didn't tell them to sit yet.

"What you just felt isn't mysterious at all," she said. "Anyone who knows how to extend their own Odem past the body can intensify it over an area instead of leaving it still. Against someone weak, tired, or just newer at this, that's enough to put them on the ground without touching them. No Domain required. No name required. All it takes is knowing how to push outward instead of inward."

Nobody asked what happened if the other person wasn't weak. Nobody needed to — the answer was already in the room, in seven bodies that had just learned it with their knees.

While they brushed themselves off and caught their breath, Dara was the first to say the other thing quietly, leaning toward Oliver and Riku as if the words weighed less if nobody else heard them.

"Have you noticed she never looks at our faces?" she said. "She looks toward wherever something just happened. When I walked in, she didn't look at me. She looked toward where I lit up my Domain by accident, half a second before."

Riku didn't answer right away.

"I thought it was just me," he said finally. "But yeah. She turns late. Like she hears before she sees."

None of the three said the word floating between them — *blind* — because saying it out loud, in front of someone who moved through the room like she wasn't, felt like an accusation none of them had the right to make yet.

---

While Anka finished taking the measure of the stragglers, on the other side of the complex, Pier and Petra had their own exercise that same morning — run by the same gray-uniformed man who'd given the orientation talk the first night. Someone called him by name out loud this time, loud enough for half the yard to hear. Reber. Nothing else, no title, as if the name alone were enough for everything there was to know about him.

Reber explained the exercise exactly once, no repeats: a single rope strung between two platforms, a gap of several meters underneath with a safety net at the bottom — not invisible, but not comfortable to look at either — and only two harnesses available for six people. Ten minutes for the whole team to cross. One rule, clearer than any they'd heard so far: any use of a Domain during the crossing, for any reason, disqualified the entire team, no exceptions, no matter the cause. "This isn't a Domain exercise," he said. "It's a rope exercise."

Pier and Petra ended up on the same team as a candidate neither of them had heard speak until that moment — absolute control in every movement, the kind of calm you don't learn, you either inherit it or pay a steep price for it. Nobody bothered with formal introductions; the exercise didn't leave time for that.

The disagreement started before the start signal even sounded — over who got the two harnesses — and escalated faster than the situation warranted. Pier proposed giving them to whoever had the least real experience with heights, starting there and letting the more confident ones cross last, with no net to catch them if something went wrong. The candidate cut him off mid-sentence.

"That's backward," she said. "Give the harnesses to whoever crosses fastest, first, so the rest can see it done right before they try it without a net."

"That leaves the shakiest ones crossing last, tired, with nobody left to show them anything," Pier said, without changing his tone. "I'd rather protect the ones who need it most, first."

"Preferring isn't the same as being faster," she said. "And the clock doesn't wait for anyone to feel ready."

Petra, who'd stayed out of it until then, stepped forward — her hands still slightly stiff from the day before, with no patience to spare on anyone that morning.

"He's right," she said, gesturing at Pier without looking at the candidate directly. "We've been here less than a week. You've been here longer. That doesn't automatically make you the ones who decide who risks what."

"Nobody said we decide by rank," the candidate said, her voice still flat, never once rising in volume. "I'm saying it because it works. If you'd rather lose the exercise protecting everyone equally, it's your team too."

It was Johanna, already wearing a harness from an earlier exercise, who stepped between the two of them before it went any further. Petra recognized her right away; they'd already exchanged a few words the first day, just enough to know her name and little else.

"You're both half right, and the clock isn't going to let you finish the argument," Johanna said, without raising her voice. "Give the harnesses to the two in the middle — not the shakiest, not the steadiest. That way nobody loses anything that matters if something goes wrong."

Nobody had time to decide whether to accept the proposal or argue it.

Reber didn't wait for them to finish deciding. A whistle sounded once, sharp, and the clock started running whether they liked it or not — and it was exactly that, the lack of time to keep arguing, that made the team end up doing the last thing they'd heard out loud: harnesses to the two of similar level, neither the shakiest nor the steadiest, without anyone ever confirming it out loud. The first three crossed without trouble, one with a harness setting the pace. Petra crossed fourth, with no net, focused only on not looking down more than she had to.

It was the fifth, one of the two left without a harness, who lost his footing halfway across the rope — a clean slip, nothing dramatic, the kind of thing that happens to anyone after several minutes of tension built up in the forearms. He fell backward, arms still reaching for a rope he no longer had.

Petra, already on the other side, felt Swap light up before she decided to — the same reflex as in Chapter 44, her body outrunning any thought. She got as far as raising her hand.

"Domain and you're out!" Reber shouted, from somewhere Petra couldn't place, his voice cutting the air with the same dryness he'd used to explain the rule at the start.

Petra closed her fist and didn't activate it. The candidate fell the rest of the way, and the net caught him with a hard bounce, nothing elegant about it, a short shout more surprise than real pain.

Nobody else moved for a full second, everyone looking at the net, at Petra, at the hand that had been halfway to rising and now hung, empty, at her side.

"I'm fine," the candidate said from the net, his voice a little broken but alert, moving his arms to prove it. "I'm fine."

Reber said nothing about what had just happened when he came over to check the time. The team didn't finish crossing before the alarm sounded — two people still on the rope when the clock hit zero — but he wrote something on his tablet with no expression to read, and moved on to the next team without mentioning the Domain that hadn't been used.

Pier waited until they'd stepped a little away from the group to say it quietly, without reproach.

"You didn't have to defend her," he said. "I've known how to take care of myself since before you were born."

"I know," Petra said. "I didn't do it for you."

She didn't say who she'd done it for, and Pier didn't ask. Both of them knew, without saying so, that what had just happened wasn't an isolated incident. It was the first time something had grazed against something else. It wouldn't be the last.

---

Anka didn't let them leave when the test ended. She asked them to gather up the mats themselves and set out the folding chairs stacked against the back wall, arranged in a semicircle — the first time since they'd arrived at St. Gotthard that anyone explained something to them without urgency attached.

"I just measured where each of you stands," Anka said, standing in front of the semicircle, no platform, no screen. "Before I give you the vocabulary, I want you to feel it first. Close your eyes."

She closed her own eyes too — the gesture changed nothing about her, but it felt ritual all the same — and something in the room loosened: an ambient weight Oliver hadn't registered as weight until it stopped being there. He'd spent the whole session feeling, in the background, a kind of low hum around her, almost indistinguishable from the rest of the room's noise. Now there was nothing. She wasn't absent — she was still standing, three meters away — but the instrument everyone had, without knowing it, been using to track her had lost its signal.

"This is Void," she said, without opening her eyes. "Shutting down your own emission until you're unreadable. The simplest of the four to explain, the hardest to master well. Eyes still closed — point to where you think I am."

There was the sound of fingers moving, hesitating. Riku pointed left, two meters off from her real spot. Dara didn't point anywhere. Oliver, who'd spent the whole morning feeling her presence as a fixed point in space, found he had nothing to point at — no direction felt any more right than another.

"Open your eyes."

She was exactly where she'd been before.

"Mantle is the opposite," she went on, without pausing. "Not shutting down — containing. Keeping what's yours pressed close to the body instead of letting it leak out on its own." She turned her head toward Riku without looking at him directly. "You. Activate your Domain for one second, then shut it off again."

Riku obeyed, uncomfortable with the attention on him. The static raised the hair on his forearm for half a second before he cut the contact himself.

"Did the rest of you feel that?" Anka asked.

Several nodded, Oliver among them.

"What you just felt for that half second is what leaks out without Mantle. Most of you already practice a crude version without knowing it, every time you try to keep your Domain from tipping your hand early. Now it has a name." She paused briefly, as if weighing whether it was worth adding. "Trained well, it does more than hide you. Containing your own Odem against a wound or against exhaustion speeds up what the body already does on its own — no real magic behind it, nothing that heals what wouldn't heal anyway, just faster. Most people take months to notice the difference. Don't expect miracles the first week. Contained over your own body at the right moment, instead of held in reserve, it also hardens what you hit with and what takes a hit — how much depends entirely on the control of whoever's using it, not on the Domain they're wearing. That's what separates someone who's had Mantle for a year from someone who's had it for ten."

Pressure didn't need demonstrating again — they'd all felt it firsthand half an hour earlier, on their knees on the same mats they were sitting on now. Anka covered it in a single sentence and moved on, without stopping to repeat what their own bodies had already learned.

"The last one is Focus," she said. "Concentrating everything on a single point instead of leaving it spread out. Most named Domains are born from this one." She turned her head toward Oliver, not toward his voice. "You. What do you see when someone activates a Domain near you?"

Oliver hesitated, not because he didn't know the answer but out of habit — he never said it out loud in front of strangers.

"A pattern," he said, finally. "Layers, direction, an anchor point. Like a file loading."

"That anchor point is Focus, crystallized into something with a name of its own," Anka said. "Those without a Domain use the same principle without ever naming it, every time they concentrate force into a fist or resistance into a rib. It's the same hardening we talked about with Mantle, just concentrated into one point instead of spread across the whole body — more attack or more defense exactly where it's needed, at the cost of leaving everything else uncovered. The difference between having a Domain and not having one is smaller than this institution's Board wants you to believe."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Something in the other seven faces told Oliver he wasn't the only one who'd just had a piece click into place that was bigger than himself — not just his Registry, not just Riku's or Dara's loose thread. There was a common language underneath all of it, and nobody had taught it to them until today.

He asked, before Anka called the session over, whether the Registry fell under any of the four categories, or whether it stood entirely outside the system.

Anka turned her head toward him before answering — toward the Odem, not toward the voice, Oliver noticed now with the same uncomfortable clarity Dara had named that morning.

"Everything fits into the system," she said. "Some things simply take longer to find where they fit." A pause, almost imperceptible. "Keep practicing. That's all for today."

It wasn't a complete answer, and Oliver knew it as he watched her leave the room with the same impossible confidence she'd walked in with.
