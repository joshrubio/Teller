# Chapter 41 — The Other Family

Inside, the house smelled of woodsmoke and old paper, the kind of smell that builds up in a building where nobody throws anything away. In the kitchen, a very old woman was cutting vegetables by the window, not quite looking up, though Park noticed her eyes track them out of the corner as they crossed the room. The woman's hands, he noticed, shook with a tremor that didn't look like age — fine, constant, the kind that comes from a body that's spent a long time holding something it no longer wants to hold.

"My great-aunt," Katalin said, not stopping, her voice dropping a degree. "Guardian before Zoltán. She can't make it down to the river alone anymore. That's why it fell to him, and that's why, when he said no, no one else was ready."

Katalin led him straight through the kitchen, without a formal introduction, toward a dark wooden door at the end of a hallway that creaked under both their feet.

"My office, if you can call it that," she said, opening the door. "This is where we keep what we haven't told AMAT in a hundred and eighty years."

The room was smaller than Park expected, shelves to the ceiling on three of the four walls, a desk buried under folders sorted by decade, the years written by hand on the spines. On the fourth wall, with no glass and no elegant frame, hung a row of portraits — more recent photographs, older oil paintings further back, each one a different man or woman, none of them quite smiling. Park didn't need it explained to understand what they had in common: the same tired stillness in the eyes, generation after generation, as if the weight got inherited in the face too.

Katalin went straight to a low shelf, pulled out a wooden box darkened with age, and set it on the table before opening it.

---

Inside was a hand-drawn family tree, whole generations in ink that had changed color from century to century, and beside some of the names — not all — a small symbol, a repeated mark that took Park a second to recognize as a hand with one finger too many.

"Táltos," Katalin said, before Park could ask. "That's what real Hungarian folklore calls it. A shaman born marked, recognized from childhood. My family didn't invent anything by using that word. We just learned, generation after generation, to industrialize what used to be a rare, respected gift."

"Industrialize how?"

"Not the way Zoltán does it now, if that's what you're thinking," Katalin said. "For a century and a half, 'industrialize' meant keeping careful records, for us. Knowing who married whom, which generation was more likely to carry the mark, where to place the ones who did so they'd grow up near the river instead of far from it. None of that sounds pretty said out loud, Agent, and I'm not pretending it does. But it was management, not exploitation. The difference between tending a garden and setting it on fire to see what grows faster out of the ashes."

Katalin traced a finger along the tree to a name near the top, the ink gone nearly black with age.

"March 1838," she said. "Do you know what happened that month in Pest?"

"The flood," Park said. He'd read about it on the plane, in passing, in the file Filipa had sent him.

"The flood everyone knows about," Katalin said. "The one nobody outside this house knows about is the one that happened underneath it. That night, while the water rose, hundreds of people across Pest started manifesting things that had no name yet. Ordinary people, terrified, suddenly able to break down doors with their bare hands, breathe underwater, see in total darkness. Nobody who survived that night told it that way afterward. It turned into panic legend, stories of people doing impossible things under fear. My family knows it wasn't fear. It was the Lidérc, waking half the city at once because something, that night, broke whatever had been keeping it calm until then."

She took a brittle sheet out of the box, protected between two panes of glass, the ink faded but legible — cramped, nervous handwriting, in old Hungarian.

"He wrote this the same night, before dawn," she said, not translating it word for word. "It says, more or less, that the water entered his own body before it entered his house. That he felt something choosing him before it chose the whole city. That he didn't know if what he did afterward was an act of faith or the only instinct he had left. He never wrote anything again after that night. He lived forty more years. No more letters from him in this box."

---

Park sat down, uninvited, in the office's one free chair.

"What is the Lidérc?"

"In folklore it's a night spirit. It's born from an egg hatched under someone's arm, and at night it comes to you disguised as a lover — but it isn't what it looks like. It steals the shape of what you love to get close to you." Katalin closed the box slowly. "What's under this river is that idea, real, at the scale of a Ruler. It doesn't want love. It wants a body. And if it has no channel to release what it builds up, it takes one from whatever weak point it finds."

"An ancestor of yours stopped it that night," Park said, putting it together.

"He contained it," Katalin corrected. "He didn't defeat it. It doesn't get defeated. He found a way to channel what was overflowing into one body, his own, instead of hundreds. It cost him something even this family still can't fully name, generations later. But it worked, and since that night someone from my line has held that channel. A Guardian. Always one. Never two at the same time."

---

"And how is the Guardian chosen?"

"It isn't chosen," Katalin said, something like exhaustion in her voice. "It's raised. It gets identified early, usually by the mark, and trained a whole lifetime to hold the channel when the turn comes. It's a job, Agent. One that ages the person doing it faster than normal, one that demands discipline, ritual, patience." She nodded toward a closed door at the end of the hallway they'd come through. "Once a month, the Guardian goes down to the riverbank alone, at night, and lets the Lidérc rise through them just long enough to empty out a little before the Guardian cuts the contact themselves. It's not a spectacle. There are no witnesses. It's work done alone, in silence, paid for in years of life that don't come back. In exchange, the same relief the Guardian releases carefully is what, filtered, eventually settles as a Domain in some of us over time. We don't choose it. It happens to us, the way it happens to anyone anywhere in the world who wakes something up they didn't ask for."

Park thought of Mariana, of Mantle, of her hand on Bruno's chest teaching him to hold something back. Same logic, different language, different cost. He thought too of his own father, of Ji-ho, of the reserve he'd been building since childhood without anyone ever explaining it to him in these terms — how much of what he himself carried had, somewhere down the chain, a similar cost nobody had shown him yet.

"In Lisbon there's a family that does something similar," he said. "They don't channel inward. They feed what they protect with grief, and they get back what's left over."

"I know," Katalin said. "My family's correspondents for generations, until Zoltán decided the correspondence was excess too." Something crossed her face as she said it, quick, put away before Park could ask about it. "The difference is they feed their Ruler. We drain ours. Each line found its own way of keeping still whatever fell to it. Neither way is comfortable. Neither looks anything like what Zoltán is doing now."

"Zoltán was going to be next," Katalin said, before Park could ask. "He was raised for it. Trained for it. And at some point none of us saw coming in time, he decided holding the channel with discipline, slowly, over his own body, was slower and less interesting than forcing it straight into other people. He's not competing with the Guardian, Agent. He is the Guardian. The one who was supposed to protect everyone else from this is the one breaking it from the inside."

---

Something creaked in the far wall, a dry sound, old wood settling — or that was what Park wanted to think, until he saw Katalin's face. It wasn't fear that crossed her expression. It was something closer to resignation, the kind that belongs to someone who's been waiting a long time for a specific sound and finally hears it.

"Is that normal?" he asked.

"No," she said, already on her feet. "It isn't."

She went to a small window looking out over the slope of the garden, toward the line of trees and the river beyond. The glass, on the inside, had a thin film of condensation that had no business being there on a cold, dry November afternoon.

"Without a Guardian holding the channel properly, this is going to keep happening," Katalin said, not looking away from the glass. "Small. Quiet. Until it isn't." She turned to Park. "Zoltán can't hold it fully anymore. He hasn't been able to for months. Every time he forces it into a stranger instead of letting it pass through his own body, whatever doesn't get out that way keeps piling up in the only channel he's got left. Him."

"And if we don't stop him?"

"Then at some point, I don't know when, the Lidérc is going to finish taking the one thing it still has close at hand," Katalin said. "His body. And whatever comes out of that won't be my cousin anymore."

"And the crisis?" Park asked. "The uncontrolled awakenings across the city. Ilona talked about it like it was a separate problem."

"It's not separate. It's the same problem, seen from further away," Katalin said. "Every time Zoltán forces the gift into a stranger instead of letting it pass through the channel, some of it spills where it shouldn't — like water leaking through a crack before it reaches the drain. That's what Ilona is measuring without knowing where it comes from: small awakenings with no pattern, all over the city, the Lidérc's pressure hunting for improvised outlets because the one real outlet is broken. The longer it takes Zoltán to fall completely, or the longer it takes someone to stop him, the more new cracks are going to appear, and none of them are going to stay small forever."

---

Park looked at her a moment, something finishing its click into place.

"And if that happens to him?" he asked, slowly. "Who holds the channel after?"

Katalin didn't answer right away. She pushed up her left coat sleeve without a word, showing, on the back of her wrist, a birthmark in the exact shape of the extra-fingered hand Park had seen drawn on the family tree.

"Me," she said. "Since I was born. No one else in my generation has it." She pulled the sleeve back down, slowly, her hand lingering a moment over the covered wrist, like someone making sure something is still where it should be. "So no, Agent. I'm not here just to clean up my family's name. I'm here because if Zoltán falls all the way, the next person who has to learn to hold this in their own body, from scratch, with no one to train her in time, is me."

Park said nothing for a moment. There was something in the way she'd said it — no drama, no asking for pity — that reminded him of Mariana explaining why she protected A Saudade: not as a heroic choice, but as a fact so heavy it wasn't worth arguing about out loud anymore.

"Are you afraid?" he asked, finally.

"Every day," Katalin said. "That doesn't change anything I have to do if the moment comes."

Katalin's phone buzzed on the desk before Park could answer that. She read it, and what little calm was left on her face vanished completely.

"It's from my contact downtown," she said. "Two men armed with something that isn't an ordinary weapon, spotted twenty minutes ago entering a closed pumping station by the Danube, right in the middle of Pest. Description of one of them: European, young, accent that isn't Hungarian."

"Lisbon," Park said.

"And the other had his right arm bandaged to the shoulder." Katalin was already on her feet, putting the box back on the shelf with a care that didn't match the urgency in the rest of her body. "Zoltán isn't preparing an escape, Agent. He's preparing something in a pumping station that connects straight to the river."

Park was already on his feet too when the phone buzzed in his hand — not the message he was expecting. A call. Cartwright.

"The window closed," Cartwright said, no greeting. "Eleven minutes ago. Someone with more rank than me decided eight hours of courtesy was more than enough."

"Then I have to give you something worth reopening it for," Park said, already walking toward the door, Katalin two steps behind. "A closed pumping station by the Danube, in Pest. Two men armed with something that isn't an ordinary weapon, spotted twenty minutes ago. And a much bigger reason than the one I gave you this morning for why this can't wait on anyone with more rank than you."

There was a silence on the other end, longer than the last one, the kind Park already knew meant Cartwright was deciding something, not just writing it down.

"I'm not going to reopen the same window," Cartwright said finally. "I'm bumping this to Level 2, confirmed regional threat, with your name as field contact. That means real resources behind you from now on — and it also means if this goes wrong, it doesn't go wrong quietly for anyone. Not for you. Not for me, since I'm the one signing off to escalate it."

"Why are you doing this?" Park asked, though he already suspected part of the answer.

"Because the boy died anyway, whatever I do with the paperwork," Cartwright said. "At least this way, if something goes wrong again tonight, it won't be because nobody gave you what you needed in time."

She didn't say the word trust. She didn't have to. Park heard it anyway, in the exact weight Cartwright had just put on her own shoulders without asking for anything back.

"I'll try to make sure you don't need it," Park said.

"Try hard, then," Cartwright said. "Move."

The call cut off. Park pocketed the phone, and for the first time since landing in Budapest he felt the clock wasn't just running against him and no one else anymore.

"Then we're out of time for the rest of the file," he said, turning to Katalin.

"No," she said, closing the office door behind them both. "We're not."
