# Chapter 110 — What Waits in the Mist

---

Kaspar had left that afternoon without saying where, something that no longer surprised anyone in town, and the woman who brought them dinner — Sisi had never learned her full name, only that everyone called her Doña Zulay — stayed sitting with them on the porch longer than dinner required.

"Have you two gone up?" she asked, not looking at either of them in particular, her eyes on the high slope, where the night mist had already closed completely over the candles someone kept burning. "To the mountain. At night."

"Kaspar said there was no need," Tomás said. "That the mist up there doesn't forgive anyone who doesn't know the way."

Doña Zulay smiled, not mockingly, with the patience of someone who's heard that same caution repeated a hundred times from a hundred different mouths.

"He's right, though not for the reason you think." She settled the shawl over her shoulders, though it wasn't cold. "Up there is something that's been around longer than anyone in town can count. Before us, the old folks say. Before there was a town. It has a hunger that doesn't go away with eating, so it takes other things instead — an animal, a night's sleep, the calm of someone who never feels safe again. Nobody knows how much it needs or how much is enough."

"And the candles?" Sisi asked.

"That's what people offer it, hoping it's enough." Doña Zulay got to her feet, gathering the plates with the same tired motion as always. "My grandmother used to say it was different before — that it had guardians, people who knew how to ask it for things without it hurting. Not anymore. Now it's just alone, with no one to teach it manners." She stopped at the door. "Don Martín goes up more than anyone I know, for a man who says he doesn't believe in stories."

She left without waiting for an answer to that. Sisi stayed watching the mist a moment longer, and didn't say what she was thinking: that for weeks now she'd watched Kaspar look toward that exact slope with the same attention a man gives something he still hasn't decided whether to save or to fear.

---

Yusuf had been eleven hours inside the town's radius, moving with the same professional care as always, when he understood that months of financial trail — Marcel Aubry, the estate, the Meridian Fund, each name a door leading to the next — had finally brought him to something real.

It wasn't what he'd imagined.

He'd expected a bunker, a hidden operation, something that needed to hide. Instead he found a small clinic with freshly painted walls, a new generator humming under an unpaved street, and a plaza where three old men played dominoes in the shade of a roof that, according to the conversation he caught without getting too close, "Don Martín" had paid for himself the year before. No one spoke of him with fear. They spoke of him the way people speak of someone who's done more for a place than the very institution that should have.

That was the real fragment, and it cost him something to admit it to himself: whatever Kaspar was, forty years running from AMAT, decades of patient recruitment and disappearances no one investigated, he wasn't just building a cover. He was building something the people here genuinely needed, and sustaining it with a consistency no front operation required.

What he still couldn't see was the rest. Why. To what end. What happened to the people Kaspar actually recruited, beyond Aubry, beyond the accountant in Geneva and the lab technician in Fribourg. That gap was still as dark as the day he'd flown to Venezuela.

It was in the third hour of discreet surveillance, posted at the edge of town where the hillside began to reclaim ground from the crops, when he noticed the change.

He'd been in Void since before crossing the first field — signature fully shut off, the same discipline he'd trained for years without ever needing it as much as now. No Domain should be able to read him. That was exactly what he'd been taught to trust.

A young man — twentysomething, eyes closed even while walking, something Yusuf had never seen in a body that moved with that kind of certainty — crossed the trail fifty meters away and stopped dead, his head turning slowly toward the exact spot where Yusuf stood.

Yusuf didn't move. Years of training had taught him that absolute stillness was, almost always, more invisible than flight. He counted the seconds: the young man wasn't looking at him, not directly — he was reading the ground around him, the underbrush, the exact arrangement of fallen leaves on the damp earth, with the same attention as someone reading a page in a language he knew by heart. It wasn't the Domain. Yusuf, fully shut off, had nothing that Domain could sense. It was the terrain itself — a trace that Yusuf, trained to leave nothing an instrument could pick up, had never learned to hide from someone who'd known every inch of that hillside since childhood: a branch bent at the wrong angle, earth turned where it shouldn't be, the exact tread of a man who walked like someone who came from somewhere else.

The young man never quite looked in the right direction. He stayed a moment longer, head tilted, something close to certainty crossing his face without turning into action, and then he turned back toward the center of town, unhurried.

Yusuf let his breath out slowly, convinced he'd held. He had no way of knowing that what had just failed wasn't his concealment — only half of it.

---

Before that, much farther up than any marked trail, Kaspar stood on the water itself, in the same clearing as the first night — where a stream dammed itself against a stone drop, forming a dark pool the size of a small room, and the usual candles marked the stone edge. The mist around him was thick enough that he'd stopped being able to tell where the air ended and the other thing began.

He wasn't speaking out loud. There was no one there to hear him if he had. His arms were spread to either side, palms up, and something answered — a slow gathering in the air around his hands, a growing weight with no clear shape or boundary, only a direction: toward him, from the stone bank, from the water itself, from the mist that drifted down between the trees and hung over the pool as if it didn't want to touch it.

She was sitting on the bank, among the roots of the same old tree from the first night, her feet almost touching the water without getting wet. She hadn't walked there — Kaspar hadn't seen her arrive, and he'd been standing in the same spot for half an hour with nothing moving before she appeared, simply, in the mist, as though she'd always been there and had only just decided to let herself be seen.

"You came back," Kaspar said, not as a question.

The girl didn't answer with words. She tilted her head, a gesture too slow to be entirely human, and the few candles someone had left on the stones of that bank all went out at once, with no wind, while the water around Kaspar's feet moved with no ripples, no visible displacement — the kind of movement you only notice by what's no longer where it was.

Kaspar didn't step back. He lowered one hand, slowly, palm open toward the exact point where the water had just gone still in a way water doesn't go still on its own.

Something took it.

It wasn't a grip. It was more a sustained pressure, cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature — the same weight he'd spent weeks learning to recognize, now closed around his hand as if it had fingers of its own — and Kaspar closed his eyes for an instant, like someone recognizing another person by touch before sight.

"I'm not here to take you anywhere," he said, the same words he'd used the first night, weeks back, because they seemed to be the only ones that hadn't made her pull away. "Still not here for that."

Something passed through the contact that wasn't quite a word, but that Kaspar understood anyway — a hunger with no shape, no clear direction, something that didn't yet know how to ask without frightening whoever came close. Behind that, further down, fragments that weren't hers: a voice that wasn't the girl's, repeating a name Kaspar couldn't hold on to, as if it wasn't built to stay in any memory but her own.

"Not yet," Kaspar said, quieter now. "But soon. I've owed you this since the first night, and I haven't stopped paying it since."

The contact eased, not all the way, and the mist thickened for a moment before starting to fold back toward the tree, taking her with it before Kaspar could see the exact moment she stopped being there.

The candles that had gone out lit themselves again, one by one, with no one touching them.

---

Kaspar came down from the mountain shortly after full dark, and he did it in a way no AMAT report would have known how to classify.

He wasn't quite walking — his feet grazed the ground without fully bearing his weight, as if something besides him decided how much he actually had to step down. He moved wrapped in the same thick mist that hadn't let anyone see anything from the square all night, the underbrush and roots giving way a step before he reached them. Both hands open at his sides, at the exact pace of someone walking, unhurried, down a familiar path, except there was no path.

The air felt denser before he reached the clearing where they waited. Sisi felt it in her chest, a weight that matched nothing visible, and Tomás put a hand to his sternum without realizing he'd done it.

"Doña Zulay told you the story," Kaspar said, his feet finally touching ground, not needing to ask. "She tells it to everyone who arrives. She's a good woman. Believes every word of it."

"You don't?" Sisi asked.

Kaspar looked at her for a moment, the weight in the air easing off slowly, like something folding back instead of switching off.

"I think something that alone doesn't know how to ask for company without it looking like a threat anymore." He shook the water off his sleeves, an almost domestic gesture. "I don't know if that makes it less dangerous than what Doña Zulay tells, or more."

Neither of them asked him anything else. They'd learned, in the weeks since Switzerland, that Kaspar answered questions when he decided they were worth answering, not when they were asked.

Camilo arrived a few minutes later, the same young man who'd crossed near Yusuf without fully opening his eyes, and waited for Kaspar to finish drying off before speaking.

"There's someone at the north edge," he said, no preamble. "Been there since afternoon. Hasn't moved enough for me to lose him, but not enough to get closer either."

"I know," Kaspar said, no surprise in his voice, almost something like relief. "Or I sense it, which for our purposes is the same thing."

"Want me to pull him out?"

"Not yet." Kaspar looked back toward the mountain, toward the mist that barely let the candles show anymore. "Give him a while longer. I want to know what he decides to do with what he's found before I decide what to do with him."

---

Yusuf had spent the previous hour arguing with himself over whether even approaching was worth the risk.

He'd seen Sisi from a distance that afternoon, crossing the plaza beside Tomás, and the recognition had cost him a full second — not because of the time that had passed, but because of how different someone looked outside the perimeter where he'd known her. At the Center she'd been a closed presence, nearly opaque even to people with no Registry to read her; here she walked with her shoulders down, without the constant tension of someone waiting for someone else to decide something for her. Yusuf had trained with her alone twice, near the end of her time in Valais — sessions no one else had asked to supervise, because no one else volunteered to do it with someone whose Domain left nothing to read. Enough that she knew his name, and he knew the exact weight of what her Domain refused to show.

That, he decided, was more than he had with anyone else within three kilometers. It would have to be enough.

He found her past midnight, by the well behind the house where she slept, and didn't give her time to ask who he was before he spoke.

"Combat Instructor," Sisi said, before he could introduce himself, the surprise barely audible in her voice. "From the Center. From Valais."

"So you remember. That's a relief."

"You were one of the few who didn't pretend my Domain didn't bother him," she said. "That's the kind of thing you remember."

"And Oliver?" Sisi asked, before he could say anything else, the question coming out with an urgency that didn't fit the rest of her calm.

"At St. Gotthard," Yusuf said. "Getting ready to become a real agent. He's doing well, from what reaches the ears of anyone who still pays attention to that kind of thing."

Sisi said nothing for a long moment. Something crossed her face that never quite turned into words — the same kind of doubt she'd spent weeks not fully naming, the question of whether the door she'd closed behind her was still the one she remembered, or had already become something else entirely without anyone telling her.

"There's no time for nostalgia," Yusuf said finally, his voice low, urgent without being a shout. "I know you trust him more than you trust anyone who comes from outside. I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to use whatever's left of your own head, which I know is more than most people have."

Sisi didn't step back, but she didn't move toward him either.

"I've already sent word to AMAT," Yusuf went on. "What I've found, where to find it. People are coming, with me or without me. That gives you a window, right now, to decide for yourself what to do with it, before someone else decides for you again."

Sisi opened her mouth to answer, and that was when Yusuf heard, behind him, a footstep that was neither his nor hers.
