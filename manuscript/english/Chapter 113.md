# Chapter 113 — Pattern Revealed

---

Reber said it without raising his voice, the way he said everything, which was exactly why it took them a moment to understand he'd just named something serious.

"There's a pattern I don't like," he said, pointing at the map Anka always kept spread across the warehouse table. "I've been watching it for days, waiting for it to correct itself. It hasn't."

"The fight club," Anka said, not needing him to finish.

"Without Iker, since the first night. But the ones who are left haven't stopped moving as a group, and they're not hunting whoever has the most badges. They're hunting whoever can defend themselves least. I've counted six separate encounters in three days, every one against candidates way below them on the board."

"That doesn't add points," Anka said.

"Not for them. For everyone else, it does." Reber crossed his arms, his voice still flat. "If they shrink the number of people competing for the bottom spots, the margin rises for everyone still above. Including them."

Father Conti, who up to then had listened without saying anything, leaning against the edge of the table as if he'd been there the whole conversation, finally spoke.

"Collective sabotage, then. Not hunting."

"That's exactly it," Reber said. "And I don't plan on intervening yet. The rules don't forbid it, however dirty it feels."

Conti nodded slowly, his gaze already back on the map — not on the zone Reber was pointing to, but a little farther north, where one point had spent the whole week moving differently from the rest.

"And that one?" he asked, not looking away from it. "Separated from his group since last night."

"The Webb boy," Anka said. "The rest of Valais is still moving together, without him."

"When he's ready, I'd like to be the one who finds him."

No one asked why. Reber went back to the map without further comment, and Anka wrote something down, without saying it aloud, that had nothing to do with what they'd just discussed.

---

Several kilometers away, Johanna's group moved in silence down an already-closed commercial street, five people spread through a pattern no one had taught them and that they executed flawlessly anyway — two in front, two on the flanks, Johanna at the center, coordinating with gestures that needed no words.

They'd spent four days hunting like this, with the same discipline Johanna applied to everything else, and the results showed: thirty-four badges among the five of them, no losses of their own, the same methodical pace that put them, alongside the fight club, among the most feared groups in the entire exam.

Noa led her own group through another part of the city, smaller, more aggressive — three people, including her, moving fast and with far less patience than Johanna's. Her edge wasn't coordination. It was her — every direct confrontation ended the same way, no Domain required, just the same inherited technique that had made her champion of her own group weeks earlier at St. Gotthard. Twenty-two badges in four days, fewer than Johanna, but many more than the seventeen Valais, as a whole, not counting Oliver, had gathered among its five in the same time. A hundred twenty-eight candidates remained that afternoon, and the distance between one group and another was starting to show as much as the distance between them and the ones who'd already fallen out completely.

Neither route knew it yet, but both were about to cross paths that same night with something neither had planned on finding.

---

Oliver had spent since dawn following a trail that wasn't the one he'd started out looking for.

He had five badges of his own, the ones from the night before, the first since he'd left. He'd gone out to hunt alone again that same morning, barely hours later, and by mid-morning he'd already ambushed two candidates fleeing from something — the panic still on both their faces when he caught them, more worried about what they were leaving behind than what was in front of them — and took their badges before they finished understanding there was now a new problem. The pattern behind their flight — weak candidates, hurt more than necessary, left behind with no one bothering to finish the job — carried a signature he already recognized. Milan, leading the rest of the fight club, hunting in a pack.

He got a third badge that same afternoon, ambushing another candidate running in the same direction as the first two, with the same marks of having narrowly escaped something. Three, without having crossed paths with the fight club directly yet.

He let a full day pass after that without going anywhere near the fight club — moving alone through the rest of the city, learning the rhythm of other candidates before deciding he already knew enough about this one. That new day, the fifth since the exam began, brought him nine more badges, none the same way: two from a candidate sleeping in the open with no watch set, three from a small group that scattered the moment they saw him coming and never managed to regroup in time, one from someone who practically offered it up on his own once he found himself cornered with no way out, and three more from a couple arguing too loudly in an alley who never heard him coming until it was too late. Seventeen, counting everything before.

He followed them the rest of the day, letting them do the work of finding targets and limiting himself to collecting what they dropped — until, at dusk, the trail led him toward Scalo Farini, the old rail yard Milan had spent years promising to redevelop without ever finishing the job. By day, a half-done construction site. By night, a real void inside the city itself — abandoned freight sheds, rusted dead-end tracks, not a single civilian in sight once you crossed the perimeter fence, half collapsed in several stretches.

That was where he understood who the fight club was hunting that night.

He recognized Noa's group entering from the opposite end of the yard, three shapes moving in the same disciplined formation as always between two rows of rusted freight cars, following two of Milan's people who'd gone ahead as decoys, unaware that Milan and the rest of his group had already been positioned among the sheds for some time, waiting for the exact angle to cut off their path.

The decoys showed themselves just long enough for Noa to quicken her pace, following them toward the exact gap between two sheds where the rest of the fight club waited. Oliver, crouched on an overturned freight car fifteen meters up, high enough to see the whole yard at once, calculated the moment — a little closer, and he could do to Milan what he'd already done to the other three: steal the work before they finished it.

The two groups met at the exact center of the yard, between the two empty sheds, the distance closing from both sides at once.

And then, with nothing to announce it, Father Conti appeared between them.

No one finished anything. The blow, halfway thrown. The step that never closed the circle. Eight bodies frozen at once, the air thickening a full degree around a man in a cassock who shouldn't have been there — the same pressure Oliver already knew from the night at the warehouse, now felt by eight people with no Registry and nothing to call it by.

"Don't stop on my account," Conti said, hands crossed behind his back, something amused in his voice. "This was just starting to get interesting."

No one thought twice.

Eight against one. Asphalt cracking under a fist that connected with nothing. A freight car's siding torn open two meters wide by a kick that swept past empty air. Conti moved through all of it unhurried, one step, a half turn — every miss too precise to be coincidence, and none of the eight had breath left yet to wonder why.

Milan changed the rhythm first.

Threads. Impossibly thin, nearly invisible even to the Registry, strung taut between his fingers and a rusted container ten meters off — Oliver didn't need anyone to explain it to understand what he was watching.

A piece of rail came flying at him. A minimal flick of the hand. The metal split in two, clean, no spark. Cut before it arrived, Oliver understood, by something no ordinary eye would have seen tighten in the air. An instant later the same threads closed around the whole container, tearing it off the ground with the screech of fatigued metal, and dropped it right where Conti had been a second earlier — already empty, the crater opening in the asphalt alone.

Noa didn't need a name for hers. A half-derailed freight car rolling toward one of her people, her hands and her whole body loaded with raw Odem, metal screeching against metal — the same technique any Awakened could train without ever naming it, holding a weight no body her size should be able to move, until the whole car changed direction and slammed into the nearest shed.

A blow aimed at her. The curly-haired kid intercepted it first, and didn't fall — something went dark in the air between the fist and his skin, the impact arriving at half strength, as if filtered through water, absorbing whatever touched it without giving anything back. On the other side, tattoos climbing up a neck, hands and feet stuck to the rusted metal of a shed with no real handhold, scaling it as if the rust itself were holding him up, hunting for an angle from above.

The first switch of sides came without warning.

The stocky one saw Noa busy with the freight car and caught the braided woman across the face — a hit that had nothing to do with Conti. She dropped sideways. The curly-haired kid, still close by, didn't hesitate: he broke off from Conti himself mid-step and came down on the stocky one with all his weight concentrated in an elbow, taking advantage of the fact that the stocky one, already off-guard from his own hit, had nothing left to answer with. Not out of loyalty to anyone on his own side. Because the gap was there and no one else had seen it in time.

The stocky one went down, and the curly-haired kid didn't get to enjoy it — the thin one, seeing his own side fall, threw himself at him before he could recover. Payback, though not from the one who'd taken the first hit.

It happened again minutes later, on the other side. The thin one, still on top of the curly-haired kid, never saw the braided woman coming — recovered by then, limping but upright — until her hit knocked him clean off the body underneath him. The tattooed one, from the wall, took advantage of her having spent her whole balance on that hit to let go and drop onto her back, the weight of the fall landing on the exact spot she'd already been hit before.

Conti moved through the middle of all of it.

Hand closing around the thin one's shirt, still reeling from the braided woman's hit. Both of them, gone — no sound, no flash, as if they'd simply stopped being there.

No one stopped to check where. With Conti gone from the fight, the seven who remained kept finding gaps in each other, the same game with no fixed sides repeating itself without him. Milan, seeing the braided woman still pinned under the tattooed one's weight, pulled on a thread already strung around a loose rail fragment and sent it at her to finish what his own teammate had already started — not out of cruelty, just because she was there, down, and easier than any target still standing.

The curly-haired kid got in the way in time, the same blackout as always absorbing most of the blow, though not all of it this time — he went down too, dragging the braided woman with him out of the rail's reach.

It was then, in the middle of that new chaos, that Conti reappeared on the other side of the yard, without the thin one anymore, and the rhythm changed all over again.

He deflected the tattooed one's hit against the braided woman with his forearm. A half turn. A second hit from Milan, blocked. A third, from someone Oliver never fully identified, blocked too, in almost the same second. Before any of the three could try again, Conti's hand was already closed around the curly-haired kid's shoulder.

Both of them, gone again.

Eight, now six. Two gaps in the air where a moment ago there had been people, and no one left in the yard was entirely sure anymore who they were fighting.

That was exactly the moment Oliver had been waiting for.

Swap carried him to an empty cable spool ten meters off, just as the tallest of the remaining fight club members — gray jacket, broad shoulders — landed a clean hook on the braided woman, already back on her knees. Oliver didn't go for her. He went for him.

A punch, blocked. The tall one didn't back off like the others — he read Oliver's angle almost as fast as Oliver read his, and the second hit didn't go for the body, it went for the wrist, closing his fingers around it before Oliver could pull it clear. He pulled with real force, trying to throw him off balance against the cable spool itself, and it nearly worked — Oliver had to release Momentum all at once, with nothing built up, just to break the grip before it locked, the whole sleeve of his shirt tearing along the line where the tall one's fingers had dug in.

The tall one answered with an elbow that Oliver dodged by twisting his torso, and in the gap that left, he saw the badge — hanging loose, nearly within reach, though the tall one was already resetting for a third attempt, tighter than the two before it.

Behind the tall one, someone was closing fast to help him. The stocky one, still recovering from his own fall but back on his feet. Oliver triggered Swap against him at exactly the right instant, the world trading places without a sound, and reappeared already behind the tall one, every ounce of built-up Momentum releasing at once in a single blow to the base of his skull — the only one the tall man never saw coming in the whole fight.

Both of them — the tall one, and the stocky one, who a second earlier had been two meters away — flew off in opposite directions, sent further by a burst of Impulse Oliver released after them to make sure neither one touched the ground anywhere near the other, into two different sheds.

First badge. Oliver already had it pocketed before the tall one finished falling, his wrist still aching from how close he'd come to losing that exchange.

He didn't get to celebrate it. The hit came from his side with no warning at all, something he never saw coming in time, and he went flying through two sheets of rusted siding before stopping against a third, every bit of air knocked out of his lungs at once.

Mantle and Focus, almost by reflex, containing the damage before his mind finished processing what had hit him.

Noa. Coming straight at him, something close to rage crossing her face — not aimed at him specifically, Oliver understood, but at everything that yard had become that night.

The rusted siding had torn his shirt clean off his back going through it, a line of shallow but long scrapes marking him from shoulder blade to waist. There was no time to explain himself. He loaded Momentum into the debris right behind him — the broken siding, fragments of the shed his own body had just punched through — and threw them all at once, an entire rain of twisted metal flying at Noa at a speed no fragment should carry on its own.

Noa didn't back off. She tore a loose length of beam off the ground and used it to split the first big fragment in two, dodged the second by twisting her whole body, and reached Oliver with the beam still in her hands.

First exchange: Focus concentrated at the tip of the beam, a hit Oliver blocked by crossing his arms, the impact traveling straight up through the bone. Second exchange: a feint to the side, real this time, and Oliver barely managed to deflect it enough that it didn't break anything.

He wasn't going to win that exchange clean, and he knew it before Noa finished her third move. Swap pulled him out of there before it landed.

He reappeared in the middle of an entirely different exchange — the tattooed one, still tangled up with the braided woman, spinning toward the new threat the instant he felt Oliver appear at his side, with no time to finish what he'd started. Oliver blocked the first hit. The tattooed one didn't stop there: he chained a second before Oliver finished recovering his guard, and that one landed full on his shoulder — a dull crack, not quite broken bone but close, a bruise that was going to take days to fade already forming right there. The third came already loaded with more force than the first two, reading that Oliver hadn't fully recovered — and it was on that exact attempt, arm fully extended, that Oliver fixed him with Anchor.

That gave him the room he needed. Three hits, four, all loaded with Momentum and released in a burst, faster than the tattooed one could process with only one free arm to cover himself — though he took the first two without going down, something in him as stubborn as Milan himself about giving up.

Second badge.

He moved before the dust settled, crossing the yard toward where the stocky one, back on his feet again, waited with his fists closed instead of running — the earlier flight hadn't taken his will to fight, only his breath. He feinted to the side, real this time, good enough that Oliver had to adjust the angle mid-motion; the real hit only grazed his jaw instead of landing full, a thin cut opening just under his cheekbone, hot blood running down before he felt the real pain of it. Oliver answered with point-blank Impulse, the force direct with no contact needed, and the stocky one flew back into the same shed that already bore his mark from before, this time without getting up.

Third.

The braided woman was last — found halfway between two freight cars, still recovering from the tall one's hit but far from finished: she threw the remains of a rail fragment at him, Focus concentrated the moment she saw him coming, and Oliver had to break it mid-flight with a well-aimed Momentum strike before it reached his chest — a loose sliver still opened a short line across his forearm on its way past. She used that second of distraction to try a hit of her own, fast, almost clean — Oliver read the feint an instant before it fully committed, and closed the distance just as she finished committing to the wrong move.

Fourth.

That was when Conti reappeared, at the far end of the yard, and Milan reacted before anyone else — another thread strung out, this time around the entire frame of a rusted loading crane, tearing it off its base and hurling it at the priest the way someone throws something far smaller. Conti stepped aside with no apparent effort, and the crane kept going on its original path, its edge clipping Oliver just enough to knock him off where he stood.

He ended up on his feet, three meters from Milan, the two of them alone for the first time that night.

"You too," Milan said, no real surprise, only exhaustion. "Of course."

There was no feint this time, from either side. Milan pulled a thread already strung to a rail fragment at Oliver's feet, trying to sweep it out from under him, and Oliver jumped in time, using the thread's own tension when it snapped against his boot as extra lift in the air. He came down already on top of Milan, a short hit to the ribs that connected clean.

Milan answered with another thread, closer, faster, cutting straight through the air between them — Oliver felt it before he saw it, and dropped under the invisible line at the last possible instant. From the ground, he swept Milan's legs, and for the first time that night Milan really lost his balance.

Oliver already had his fist loaded, the punch that would have closed the whole exchange, when a hand closed around his arm.

Conti. Standing right beside him, as if he'd been there the whole time.

"That's enough," he said, and the two of them vanished before Oliver's fist ever fell.
