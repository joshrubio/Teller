# Chapter 64 — No Signal

Seven weeks. Tomás had kept count up to that point — in the notebook that wasn't for writing down anything useful, because nothing that had happened in seven weeks was especially useful to write down.

The apartment sat above a hardware store that opened Thursdays and Saturdays. The rest of the week, the village had the specific silence of places that used to be something else — skiing in winter, hiking in summer, and now February, with just enough snow that nobody wanted to be here. The shops downtown closed with the off-season sign up. The bar at the end of the main street with three men who always drank the same coffee at the same hour and looked at the door whenever someone they didn't know walked in, which was almost always Tomás.

Kaspar had been here before. The building's owner, a man in his seventies who came up to collect on the first Friday of every month, called him Mr. Hofer with the familiarity of someone who's received rent in cash and on time long enough to stop asking questions. Kaspar returned the greeting with the cordiality of someone who doesn't need anybody to know his real name.

Tomás had spent seven weeks learning things like that about Kaspar. The total sum wasn't much.

The apartment door opened at a quarter past four. Kaspar came in with the outside cold still on his coat and went straight to the kitchen. Tomás heard him from the couch, book open on his knees, the page unturned for half an hour now.

What he noticed — what he always noticed when Kaspar came back from outside — was the vitality. It wasn't the energy of someone who'd exercised, or the good mood of someone who'd had an interesting conversation. It was something more physical, harder to name: Kaspar came back from outside always slightly younger than he'd left. Not in the features — the features stayed the same, the wrinkles around the eyes, the fully white hair. But in something about his posture, in the way he moved around the kitchen.

The first time, Tomás had put it down to the light. The second, to fatigue from the days before compared to the rest of an afternoon out. By the seventh week he'd filed it in the category of things about Kaspar with no satisfying explanation, and probably never would have one.

"There's soup," Kaspar said from the kitchen.

"I already ate."

"I made it, so it's better than whatever you ate."

Tomás didn't argue that because it was probably true.

He got up and went to the kitchen. Kaspar had his back turned, coat still on, stirring something in the pot, and Tomás had the same split second he always had: Kaspar's silhouette from behind didn't look like a man in his sixties. It looked like something else. Different shoulders, a different height, a different way of occupying space. Then Kaspar turned around and it was Kaspar, and the split second had passed.

Tomás picked up a bowl.

He'd learned not to say anything about those split seconds.

---

That night, after the soup, Kaspar asked about the Center.

It wasn't the first time. The questions arrived spaced out, unhurried, scattered among conversations about other things — the building's history, next week's weather, what Tomás had read that afternoon. Kaspar carried no visible agenda. What he had was patience, and patience made the questions look natural.

"The man with the Weight Domain," Kaspar said, filling his glass. "How long has he been there?"

"Pier." Tomás thought about it. "A long time. Longer than anyone else I met there."

"What was he like?"

"Quiet. Always quiet." Tomás looked at his glass. "He'd been there so long the Center had become something like a calling for him. I don't remember ever hearing him complain about anything."

"Did that seem admirable to you?"

Tomás took a while to answer.

"It seemed like he'd been there so long he'd forgotten he could want something else."

Kaspar said nothing. He just listened, which was what he always did, and that silence was the kind that invites you to keep going.

"Riku was different." Tomás picked up his glass. "His Domain runs all the time, even when he doesn't activate it. Electromagnetic field. Being near him was like having something switched on in the room that never turns off." He paused. "It wasn't hostile. It was just always there. I don't know if that's worse."

"Did you ever talk to him?"

"A little. Not much. I had the impression he was reading me even when I didn't know it."

"And Dara?"

Tomás sat a moment with the glass in his hand, not drinking.

"Dara's hard to explain."

"Try."

"At first I thought she was one of those people who don't get attached to anyone. Operational distance, they call it. Then there was a moment — I don't remember exactly when — when it seemed like she did get attached, but very selectively. That she decided who." A pause. "With me she decided no."

Kaspar didn't comment on that. He just nodded slightly, the way someone notes something down without offering an opinion on it, and that gesture was clearer than any answer would have been.

"Asha was the easy one," Tomás went on. "Always willing, always in a good mood. The kind of person who makes hard spaces feel more bearable." He said it without warmth. "And that irritated me too, because you end up wondering whether the ease is real or just another way of not looking at what's actually there."

"What's there?"

"A program that makes decisions about your life with the same efficiency it uses to manage any other resource." Tomás said it without drama, like someone citing a fact. "Asha knew that. We all knew it. Some of us learned to call it something else."

They kept going. Lev — who'd been at the Center as long as Pier, but about whom Tomás could say much less, because he was the kind of person who made a space feel emptier when he was in it, not out of hostility but from a presence that folded in on itself. Sabine, reasonably honest, with the clarity of someone who's decided not to have illusions about where she is. Petra, who had firm opinions about almost everything and expressed them with a precision that could feel like judgment even if it probably wasn't. Nadia.

"Nadia?" Kaspar said.

"That's all I've got. Nadia was Nadia. I don't know how to explain it better than that."

Kaspar accepted that without pushing.

"And the staff? Hana, Maren—"

Tomás stopped.

He hadn't mentioned those names. Not tonight, and not in any of the earlier conversations. He ran back through it quickly in his head — Hana, Maren — and knew for certain he hadn't said them. Which meant Kaspar knew them from somewhere else.

Kaspar waited without changing his expression, with his usual patience.

Tomás decided not to mention that he'd noticed anything.

"Hana runs the evaluation protocols," he said. "Efficient. Sometimes it looks like kindness, sometimes the opposite. Depends on the day." He paused. "Maren's probably the most honest person in the building. The kind of honesty that doesn't judge because it doesn't need to. And Yusuf does his job, does it well, and doesn't give more than the job requires."

"None of them questioned anything?"

"Maren did." Tomás thought about it. "But from inside the system. She questioned the methods, not the premise."

Kaspar refilled his glass. Slowly.

"And Voss?"

There it was. Tomás had been expecting the question since the conversation started.

"Voss is the Center," he said. "The building, the protocols, the evaluations, the extraction decisions. There's no difference between one and the other."

"Did he explain why they pulled you out?"

"No."

"Did he ask if you wanted to stay?"

"No."

"Did he say anything to you?"

Tomás thought back to the day of the extraction. The protocol Hana had explained, the transfer, the form. Voss at the end of the hallway, looking at something on a tablet, glancing up for a moment, nodding.

"He told me the process was documented and that I could request a review through institutional channels."

Kaspar set his glass down on the table.

"Sounds like you never had the sense they chose you," he said. "Only that you were available."

Tomás didn't answer right away. Because that was exactly it, and nobody had ever put it to him that way, and hearing it out loud was different from having only thought it.

"Yeah," he said.

There was a silence that wasn't uncomfortable.

"And the new one?" Kaspar said, eventually. With the same tone he'd used to ask about Pier.

"Which new one?"

"The one who arrived shortly before they let you go. Young. Reading Domain."

Tomás didn't ask how he knew that. One more thing he'd stopped asking about.

"I didn't know him. We crossed paths in a group session or two but never talked. They were evaluating him when they pulled me."

"What was your impression of him?"

Tomás thought about the days before the extraction. The attention Voss had started paying to that corridor, that room, that particular subject. The sessions getting reorganized. The resources getting redistributed.

"He was new," he said. "Everyone's new at first."

He didn't say the rest. But the rest was there.

"And do you know how the mechanism works?"

"He reads Odem signatures. Can reproduce what he reads, in theory. That's what was going around. That's all I've got."

It was true. It was everything he had. It had been enough for Kaspar to lean slightly forward — just a centimeter, just an instant — before settling back into his earlier position.

He refilled Tomás's glass though Tomás hadn't asked for it, and changed the subject with the same fluency he always used once he'd gotten what he wanted. He asked about before the Center — where Tomás had grown up, what he'd studied, whether he had family. Ordinary questions that in anyone else's mouth would have passed for conversation, and that in Kaspar's mouth always carried that slightly different weight Tomás had learned to notice and to ignore.

He answered anyway. It was easier than not answering.

In bed, an hour later, what stayed with him wasn't the debriefing but the second part — the question about his family, the way Kaspar had listened to the answer, the brief, uncomfortable feeling of having been seen in a way the Center had never once attempted. The Center evaluated. Kaspar listened. It wasn't the same thing.

He didn't think about the centimeter Kaspar had leaned forward when he mentioned the kid who could read Domains, and not before.

He didn't think about the rest.

He fell asleep.

---

The second week of February, Kaspar wasn't there when Tomás got up.

In itself that wasn't unusual. Kaspar left without warning and came back without explanation, and Tomás had learned that asking *where did you go?* produced answers that were technically answers but didn't answer anything. What was unusual was the hour. It was six-fifteen in the morning, still dark, and Kaspar's coat wasn't on the hook.

Tomás waited until ten. Then he went out.

The village in February at that hour was basically a stage set — the bar with the three coffee men, the hardware store closed (it was Tuesday), a dog that was always in the same doorway with nobody sure whose it was. Tomás walked without a specific direction, which was how he usually walked since he'd been here. He passed the edge of the village and kept going up the trail that climbed toward the eastern ridge.

The snow was hard, packed down by the last few weeks' cold. Tomás had no specific destination. He had the feeling that if he walked far enough in the right direction he'd find something that explained the empty hook at six in the morning.

About two hundred meters from the ridge, he stopped.

There was something there. Not a figure — or maybe it was, depending on how he looked at it. The February midday light on the snow produced shadows that weren't shadows, outlines that weren't outlines. Tomás spent thirty seconds looking at the spot and couldn't say whether he'd seen something or seen the absence of something he'd expected to see.

When he reached the ridge there was no one there. Just the eastern slope, the line of the valley below, and in the distance — if you knew where to look — the shape of the stone building at fourteen hundred meters that Tomás no longer had any reason to look at and looked at anyway, for a few seconds, before heading back down.

Kaspar was in the kitchen when Tomás opened the door. He was making coffee with his coat still on — either just back, or back long enough that it made no difference.

"Where were you?" Tomás said.

"Walking."

"Since six in the morning."

"I like the morning." Kaspar offered him a cup. "The silence is different before dawn."

Tomás took the cup. He looked at Kaspar's coat, snow on the sleeves but none on the shoulders. Someone who'd walked among trees, or under cover, or somewhere the snow fell sideways instead of straight down.

He said nothing.

He'd stopped saying that kind of thing out loud.

---

The errand came in the first week of March, with the snow starting to give way at the edges of the main road — not disappearing, just retreating toward the margins with the reluctance of something that knows it's going to lose but hasn't decided when yet.

"I need you to go to Riddes," Kaspar said.

Riddes was the next village over, twenty minutes on foot or twelve on the eleven-fifteen bus.

"To do what?"

"There's an envelope at the pharmacy. Under the name Hofer. They pick it up for me when I can't go."

"How long since you've been able to go?"

"Two weeks." Kaspar said it with no further detail, in the tone of someone who's answered exactly what he was asked.

Tomás didn't ask what was in the documents. Didn't ask why a man who wasn't named Hofer received documents under the name Hofer at a pharmacy in a village that wasn't where he lived. Didn't ask what the two weeks had been for.

He grabbed his coat.

The pharmacist was a woman in her fifties who handed him the envelope without a second look, with the manner of someone who's done that exchange before and has instructions not to ask questions. Tomás signed the register with the name Kaspar had given him — a name that wasn't his either.

On the bus back, the envelope in his coat's inner pocket, he looked at the valley through the window. The snow on the lower slopes had that end-of-season texture: gray at the edges, more porous, less definitive than in January. Somewhere in the valley was a stone building with people he'd known who probably didn't think about him, because he didn't think about them either — or at least that was what he told himself.

He handed over the envelope at the apartment. Kaspar opened it unhurriedly, read something Tomás couldn't make out, and closed it.

"Thank you," he said. "Tomorrow I'll explain something I've been meaning to tell you for a while."

Tomás waited for the explanation the next day. It turned out to be about the village's history, something Kaspar had found in a local archive about land use in the canton before the Second World War. It was an interesting story, with specific people in it and an ending that wasn't sad but wasn't happy either, the kind of ending real stories have. Kaspar told it well.

It wasn't about the documents.

Tomás slept well that night. He thought he'd had a normal day. That he'd gone out, talked to someone who wasn't Kaspar, come back. That things were, in some way he still didn't have a name for, getting better.

Outside, the snow at the edge of the road kept retreating.
