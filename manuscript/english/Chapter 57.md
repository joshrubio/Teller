# Chapter 57 — Assignment

---

Voss's call came nine days after Cartwright's, while Park was still in Caracas. It was brief, economical, without the tone of someone who needs the other person comfortable before saying what needs saying. The mission was resolved, Voss said; formal follow-up would fall to the local branch. What he wanted was something else: an instructor position at Valais Center, combat tactics and Domain physical integration, a cross-branch assignment already approved — initiated by Geneva, not by AMAT-NA, which inverted the usual order of the protocol in a way Park noted without comment. Nine subjects in active evaluation. Ten, counting the case that had arrived in October.

Park didn't ask the name. He didn't need to.

He could have asked to stay — the transition in Caracas still needed people, and Cartwright wouldn't have refused him. He didn't ask. He'd spent four months not asking about Oliver, leaving the follow-up reports unopened in the folder, telling himself there was nothing to do with the answer until someone put it in front of him without his having to go looking for it. Now someone had. Twenty-four minutes in Grayhaven had been enough to leave Oliver marked in a way no report fully captured, and Park had spent every day since carrying the part of those minutes that was his without having found a way to give it back yet. He didn't owe it to AMAT. He owed it to Oliver, and an instructor position at the same center where Oliver had spent the last four months was the first real chance, in five missions, to come close to paying it without having to ask for the chance directly.

He hung up, found the route to the airport, and didn't think about Caracas again for a long while.

---

The flight from Caracas to Madrid took eight hours. Madrid to Geneva, two. The car ride from Geneva to the canton of Valais, an hour and a half.

Park slept through the first flight. On the layover in Madrid, with two hours ahead of him and the kind of artificial light that didn't distinguish between three in the afternoon and three in the morning, he took out his phone and finally opened the report that had been waiting in his inbox for days.

*Follow-up report 1952-HFD-002 — access granted.*

It wasn't any one person's testimony. It was the document Geneva had built over decades to understand what, in general, Hartford had left behind in the north sector — not an incident closed in 1952, but a pattern the office had spent seventy years tracking under different names, in different countries, each one filed separately until someone with enough access set them side by side. Hartford didn't appear as the origin. It appeared as one node among several, the first AMAT had had the luck — or the misfortune — to document with its own instruments.

The report didn't say where the other nodes were. That part was still redacted, or classified at a level Park's access still didn't cover.

What it did say, in the dry language of someone who'd spent years avoiding claims they couldn't back up, was that a Ruler didn't get resolved. It withdrew, reformed, changed shape and name, and waited. Seventy-two years of Odem organizing itself toward something in Hartford's north sector hadn't been seventy-two years of nothing happening. They'd been seventy-two years of something happening too slowly for any field instrument to classify it as an active threat.

Park thought about the mountain at Sorte. About the girl. About what Casimira had said, that there was no good version waiting underneath.

He closed the file before finishing the last page, not because he didn't want to know the rest but because part of him, the same part that had spent five missions learning to read its own mechanisms, knew he needed the rest of the trip to process what he'd already read before adding more.

On the second flight, he looked out the window.

Three missions since Hartford. Seoul, Lisbon, Budapest — and now Caracas. Each with its own file, its own evaluation, its own resolution. Clean work. The kind of work Park was trained for and did well, with no variables he couldn't calculate.

There was a mountain, inland, that he hadn't spoken to anyone about since turning in his report. No one had asked. Park hadn't offered.

There had been variables he couldn't calculate in Grayhaven. There'd been variables from the very first moment — from the power infrastructure company, and Oliver's terminal, and the way Oliver had looked at the screen with the specific curiosity of someone who doesn't know he's about to change everything.

It wasn't that he didn't want to know. He'd spent enough time in this work to understand his own mechanisms, and he knew that if he asked, he'd have to do something with the answer. And the answer, whatever it turned out to be, required something from him he hadn't yet worked out how to give.

So he hadn't asked.

---

Valais in January was exactly what the name promised: snow on the peaks, stone in the valley, the kind of high-altitude silence that wasn't the absence of sound but the presence of something larger. The car wound up a road that narrowed by degrees, and when the Center appeared around the next bend Park recognized it from the file photos and also saw it differently from the photos — more solid, more settled, with the look of something that had been in the same place long enough to have decided that place belonged to it.

Hana Ibáñez was waiting at the entrance.

"Agent Park." The tone of someone who's coordinated too many arrivals to give any one of them more weight than it needed. "The instructors' quarters are in the east wing. I'll walk you through the protocol on the way."

Park followed.

Hana talked as they walked — schedules, access zones, protocol for joint sessions, the signal system for Domain activations in indoor spaces. Park listened with the part of his attention that processed operational information and used the rest to read the building: the stone floors, the layout of the corridors, the kind of windows that looked onto the inner courtyard. The building carried its years in the way the corners of the doorframes had worn down, and in the more recent additions that didn't try to blend with the original stone.

"Four subjects currently in active evaluation," Hana was saying. "Tomás Reyes was transferred two days ago, so the group's down to three, plus the ones in integration and formal collaboration. I'll send you the full roster this afternoon, but the names you'll see most often in conditioning sessions are the front-line cases."

They turned down a corridor.

"Riku Hayashi, Static, seventeen," Hana went on. "Dara Mihai, Double, twenty-three. And the third is the case that arrived in October. Oliver Webb, Registry. Week sixteen."

Park didn't break stride.

"Webb?" he said.

"Yes." Hana checked the tablet without noticing anything in Park's tone, because Park's tone hadn't changed. "He's the most advanced case in active evaluation right now. Maren classified him thirteen weeks ahead of projection after Wednesday's session. First deliberate copy with partial modulation, radius exceeding the estimated area." Hana swiped to the next screen. "There's a note from Voss about him specifically. He wants him included in tactics sessions starting week one."

Park looked down the corridor ahead of him.

The windows at the end looked onto the east courtyard — a rectangle of stone with three benches and a steel roof. Empty at this hour. The alpine cold was doing its usual work on the air, and the January light in Valais had the specific quality of something that illuminates without warming.

"All right," Park said.

Hana kept talking. Park listened, and let the other half of his attention do what it had spent twenty years doing on its own: reading the building, not what Hana was saying about it.

It was that other half that stopped him, half a step, at the next crossing of corridors.

Below, in a field of packed dirt ringed by a low stone wall, six figures ran the perimeter in ragged formation, their breath rising in short columns against the cold morning air. Yusuf stood at the center, motionless, arms crossed, with the kind of patience Park recognized immediately — not the patience of someone waiting for something to end, but of someone counting reps he already knew were going to fail before the group made it halfway.

"Again," Yusuf said, without raising his voice. He didn't need to.

The group stopped, caught their breath, and started again without anyone arguing the order.

Park identified them one by one with the same economy he used to memorize any file. Riku, the youngest, ran like someone making up for technique with sheer stubbornness. A dark-haired girl — Asha, he guessed — setting the group's pace without anyone having asked her to. And at the back of the line, a full second behind everyone else, the boy Park had spent four months not letting fully into any of his thoughts.

Oliver ran badly. Not for lack of effort — for lack of time. It showed in the way he placed his foot, in the arm swing that hadn't gone automatic yet, in the extra second he needed to react whenever Yusuf changed the pace without warning. A body that had gone twenty-two years without needing to do this, learning it now the hard way, out in the open, in January.

Park knew that field. Not this one, but one like it — Korea, sixteen years back, an instructor with the same unhurried patience as Yusuf and the same certainty that the body had to learn before the Domain did, because a Domain activated on top of a body with no foundation was, at best, an accident, and at worst, a sentence. Park remembered his own hands, open and bleeding from the cold, his father's voice in the distance — never on the field itself, always watching from the building, which was, in its own way, a form of being present — and the cold certainty that no one was coming to save him from the next lap.

No one was saving Oliver from this one either. That, Park decided, was correct.

What wasn't correct — what took him a full second to recognize, standing in that corridor with his hand still in the pocket that held the unopened file — was the part of him that wanted to go down there right then, cross the field, and run the next lap beside the boy, not to make anything easier for him, but so that the extra second, this time, wouldn't be his to carry alone.

He didn't go down.

"Agent Park," Hana said, several steps ahead already, not yet impatient. "The east wing."

Park stepped away from the window.
