# Chapter 84 — First Day

---

St. Gotthard at night was a different building. The same windowless corridors, but fewer people crossing them; the mess hall's background noise replaced by something closer to the silence of a guard shift. Oliver already knew the schedule well enough to know that at this hour nobody would ask where he was if he didn't show up for dinner — the group had only been there a few days, but they'd already learned to read his absences without needing an explanation.

He went back to the secondary training room, the same one Dax had unknowingly lent him the night before, and this time he didn't sit down to accumulate anything. He stood still, trying the other thing Anka had mentioned almost in passing — Mantle, containing your own Odem against exhaustion instead of leaving it scattered — over the dull ache still lingering behind his sternum. He felt nothing he could swear was real and not suggestion. But the attempt gave his body something to do while his head went somewhere else.

He thought about the night of the Incursion, about the offer Kaspar had left him without ever once saying the word "offer," and about the decision he'd made without fully thinking it through — staying, continuing to be someone the system could measure instead of someone who'd dropped off the map by choice. He hadn't had a clean reason then. He still didn't have one.

He thought about Gary. They'd spent months talking on the phone every few days, the research-grant cover story holding up almost everything Oliver couldn't tell him without his father noticing the seams. The last call had been in the car, on the way to St. Gotthard, the signal cutting in and out between mountain tunnels. Gary hadn't asked questions Oliver couldn't answer — he never did; he was the kind of man who fixed things with his hands before he fixed them with words — but at the end, right before the signal died completely, there'd been a "take care of yourself" that sounded heavier than usual, as if he'd sensed, with no real data to go on, that this time there was something even he wouldn't know how to fix.

He thought about Petra, about the conversation with no ending from the day before. He thought about Nadia, about the sentence that had slipped out of her in the infirmary hallway without her planning it — *I don't have anywhere else to go* — and about how that sentence wasn't only hers, even though she was the only one who'd said it out loud.

Nobody in the group knew he was carrying the complete versions of all these stories at once, because nobody had asked him directly and Oliver hadn't offered the answer without being asked. Contain before you emit. Mantle before Pressure.

He still didn't know whether that made him stronger, or just more alone.

---

They called everyone in, no exceptions, to a hangar none of the Valais group had seen until that morning — high ceiling, polished concrete floor, and an entire wall covered in screens nobody had turned on yet. Oliver counted heads as they filed in, not getting far: the space filled faster than he could track, row after row of candidates he didn't recognize, different uniforms, different accents, until counting stopped making sense.

"Two hundred fourteen," Riku said, reading something off a sign by the door that Oliver hadn't noticed. "There are two hundred fourteen of us in the whole complex. I didn't know there were this many."

The screens lit up all at once, no ceremony, and the whole hangar went quiet before anyone had to ask for it.

It was a list. Names, in order, no photos, each one with a number beside it that didn't bother explaining what it meant yet. It took Oliver a moment to find the point where it started to matter to him, scanning the column top to bottom with the same feeling of hunting for his own name on a list of who'd passed, already knowing it wasn't going to be where he wanted it.

Higher up than he'd expected, well above any of the others, a name he recognized immediately — **Asha, #45**. It didn't entirely surprise him: he'd watched her listen to every conversation in the group with an attention that never seemed to run out, the kind of person who absorbs information without the effort ever showing. Several spots lower, **Pier, #58** — lower than Oliver would have guessed, given what he already knew about his years of experience, though maybe experience and the raw power the Board measured weren't, after all, the same thing. Lower still, **Petra, #103**. His own name came after that — **Oliver, #109** — and behind his, **Dara, #167**, and **Riku, #172**, and, almost brushing the very end of the list, **Nadia, #211** — barely three spots ahead of being the last person out of two hundred fourteen.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Nobody needed it explained, what it meant to see your own name a hair from the bottom of two hundred fourteen.

"This is the general Board," Riku said quietly, more to himself than anyone. "Great. Now it's official."

Nadia said nothing. Oliver, out of the corner of his eye, watched her read her own name twice, as if the second reading might change something, and then look away toward the hangar ceiling with the same quick, false smile he'd already seen her use on the clearing, three days earlier, with her collarbone still fractured. Nobody asked her if she was okay. She didn't offer the answer either.

Oliver kept reading, without quite meaning to, past the names he already knew. Just below the top spot, a name he'd been seeing without a label for days: **Iker Zabala, #2**, the Spanish-accented man from Dax's group, the one whose fingers sparked. Second on the whole list — close enough to the top that he barely needed looking for. Farther down, near the middle, he found the other name he'd been missing: **Noa Shapira, #104**, from Johanna's group — the candidate with the inherited calm, the one who'd ended the rope argument without raising her voice. Seeing them written there, with surname, number, and all, felt like closing a gap that had been open for days without anyone mentioning it.

"This doesn't change what you do tomorrow," said the same gray-uniformed man as always, Reber, standing in front of the hangar with no need for a microphone. "It changes exactly one thing: starting today, everything you do in Combat, Knowledge, and Field Evaluation shows up here, in real time. There's no single exam. There's this" — he gestured at the wall of screens without looking at it — "updating every day you're here."

Nobody asked what happened to the ones at the bottom of the list. Nobody had to.

---

That afternoon they went back to the pit.

Oliver recognized it before anyone said the name of the place — the same thick-glass gallery, the same white overhead light that left no shadow to hide in, the same bare concrete with its lines nearly worn away. The last time he'd been up there was his first night at St. Gotthard, watching a stranger on his knees and another standing over him, without having exchanged a word with either one.

This time there were several fights in a row, one after another, with no pause between them beyond the time it took to clear the pit and call the next pair of names. Oliver didn't manage to focus on most of them — names that still meant nothing, techniques passing too fast for the Registry to catch anything useful.

The one that stuck was Dax against Johanna — #1 and #3 on the entire list, respectively, a fact Oliver hadn't needed to read off any screen because the hangar itself, that morning, had gone silent one second too long when those two names appeared.

There was no special announcement, no instructor raising his voice to declare that this one mattered more than the others — but something in the way the rest of the instructors drifted toward the pit's railing, dropping the pretense of looking at their tablets, told Oliver they'd all known ahead of time this one would be different.

Dax walked in unhurried, the same almost animal stillness Oliver already knew from a distance. Johanna entered from the opposite side empty-handed, nothing to activate, no Domain Oliver could expect to see light up — and yet, the moment the instructor gave the start signal, the Registry registered something anyway: not a pattern, not a layered structure with an anchor point, but a kind of background, dense and constant, as if Johanna herself were, all on her own, a room full of people breathing at once.

Dax moved first — fast, closing distance with the kind of efficiency that left nothing loose, and Oliver, watching from above, caught the edge of a mode shift, brief, barely a flicker in the way the air around Dax seemed, for a second, to hold something that hadn't been there an instant before. It wasn't Focus crystallized into a Domain with readable layers, like Nadia's or Riku's. It was closer to a gate slamming shut and open very fast, a mode switching in and out before the Registry could finish fixing on it.

Johanna didn't dodge the first hit. She took it, her whole body absorbing the impact in a way that shouldn't have been possible with nothing active behind it, and returned the second with a force that had no pattern behind it to read — just reserve, raw, formless, the kind of thing Anka had described the day before without giving it a Domain's name, because it didn't need one.

Dax switched modes again, faster this time — the tell barely a flicker, one second of different air around his shoulders — and closed distance with something that was no longer just speed. Johanna turned her torso just enough that the blow grazed her instead of connecting clean, and answered with a direct shove to the chest that sent him back two full steps, his feet sliding a little on the concrete. Neither of them looked surprised. It was, Oliver thought, the kind of exchange two people can only sustain if each already knows, ahead of time, how much the other has to give without needing to test it to the limit.

They traded off like that for several more minutes, neither one letting go of what they really had — Oliver noticed it in Dax by the way he never finished completing a mode switch, cutting it off halfway every time, as if some part of him didn't trust letting it run all the way through, and in Johanna by something harder to name, a stillness between hits that felt less like fatigue than decision. Neither of them seemed willing to be the first to show everything they had, and at some point Oliver stopped being sure whether he was watching a fight or two people sizing each other up with far more care than the pit seemed to deserve.

The instructor cut the fight before either fell. Nobody protested. Both stepped back with the same cold courtesy they'd started with, and the scoreboard, somewhere Oliver couldn't see from the gallery, must have logged what everyone in the room already knew without needing a number: a draw.

---

The next fight was much shorter.

The candidate who stepped into the pit after Dax and Johanna was someone Oliver recognized right away, though it took him a second to place where from — the same man he'd seen on his knees his first night at St. Gotthard, something in the way he moved still not fully healed. He lost fast, nothing dramatic to tell afterward, barely staying on his feet long enough that the instructor didn't have to step in.

Oliver didn't see where he went after the pit. He found out that same night, at dinner, from Dara, who'd seen him crossing the access-level corridor with a bag over his shoulder, nobody with him, headed toward the door that nobody guarded.

"He didn't say goodbye to anyone," Dara said, pushing her food around the plate without really eating it. "Not even his own group. He was just walking."

"How do you know it was him?" Riku asked.

"Because he was still limping a little from the first night," Dara said. "The one from the pit. The one who lost to Dax before we'd even arrived."

Nobody at the table said anything for a moment. Petra finally broke it, her voice lower than usual.

"He lost again today, didn't he? Right after the big fight."

"He barely stayed on his feet," Dara said. "I don't think he made it to two minutes."

Nobody asked whether there was any connection between the two things, the loss and the bag over his shoulder, though everyone at the table did the math in silence at the same moment. There was no official announcement. No goodbye, not even the awkward kind that sometimes passes between strangers who've shared a mess hall for a few days. Just someone walking toward an unguarded exit, and the rest of the building behaving exactly as Reber had promised the first day — nobody followed him, nobody said his name again, and by the time they finished dinner, nobody at the table was mentioning him either, as if the whole building had infected its guests with the same discipline of forgetting.

Oliver thought, long after the hallway lights went out for the night, about the number he'd seen next to his own name that morning, and about how much real margin he had left before someone — himself, maybe, nobody else would have to decide it — decided he'd reached the end of the list too.
