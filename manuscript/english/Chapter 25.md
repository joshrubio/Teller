# Chapter 25 — Edge

What was coming from the north wasn't coming for the team.

Park saw it the moment the first gwisin crossed the clearing visible through the trees — not straight at the perimeter, but on a diagonal, toward the chimera. Behind it, two more. Then four. The same coherence of movement he'd seen in the clearing hours earlier, when the entities weren't fleeing, they were going back — except this time the point of convergence wasn't the island's interior. It was the chimera itself.

The chimera let them come.

It didn't move to intercept them. It stayed where it was, the unstable faces on its surface turning toward what approached the way something turns toward food rather than a threat. The first gwisin reached contact range and the chimera absorbed it — didn't strike it, didn't push it back. The gwisin's body dissolved against the chimera's surface, and the chimera held it there, the Odem of one passing into the other without resistance.

"It's growing," Ji-ho said, and it wasn't a warning. It was information.

One after another, the gwisin arrived and vanished inside it. With each one, something in the chimera's shape changed. The multiple faces that had spent the whole night appearing and disappearing across different points of its body began converging into one, at mid-height, and that face grew more defined with every absorption — not a human face, but something starting to organize itself as if it were becoming one. The torso straightened. The arms, disproportionate until now, found a length that matched the rest of the body.

By the time the last gwisin disappeared inside it, the chimera no longer looked like a chimera.

It had a humanoid shape now, skin white as paper. Taller than Park, broader in the shoulders, the Odem on its surface stabilized into a single pattern instead of the chaotic signature from before. Park's reader, which had spent the whole night unable to lock a number to it, started oscillating again: Level 3, Level 2, then 3 again.

It stood still a moment. Then it turned its head — the first time Park had seen it do something as precise as turning its head — and looked at him.

The four agents with the units had stopped aiming. They stayed at the edge of the perimeter, weapons lowered, watching — not because anyone had ordered it, but because there was no containment angle to apply to something the protocol had never classified. They were spectators now, not an active team.

Ji-ho stood among them, still, arms crossed. He hadn't drawn a weapon all night. He hadn't needed to — and that absence weighed more than any instruction he could have given. He could end this in one move if he wanted to. He didn't move.

Ji-ho was already talking, voice low, aimed at Kiki.

"The Elder."

He didn't explain why. Kiki didn't ask. She looked at Park for a second — the same wordless second as before — and crossed toward the Elder, still coming from the trail, slower now but not stopping, its surface still marked by the fracture from the first strike.

Park understood the logic without anyone spelling it out: the Elder burned on contact, and Kiki's hands had already paid that price twice tonight. Whatever she carried in the case wouldn't need skin to touch anything.

The case lay open on the ground. Kiki pulled out what was inside in a single motion, the sheath falling to one side — a dark-bladed katana, the edge textured like nothing quite metal, denser, as if the material had been compressed rather than forged. Her fingers, still red, the skin still tight from the Odem burns in the clearing, closed around the grip with something close to a wince — the material against damaged skin, a small, familiar pain compared to what was coming. The instant her fingers wrapped the grip, the blade shuddered once, and Park understood it was the same kind of reaction as the Class-R: her own Odem integrating with someone else's.

"Zanma," Kiki said, not to anyone in particular, the name coming out like something she'd been waiting a long time to say out loud.

She went toward the Elder without running.

In the same instant, the evolved chimera took its first step toward Park.

It didn't run. It walked, the speed held in reserve by something that no longer needed to prove it was fast — it had already proved that by absorbing the gwisin, by becoming this. Park waited, planted, reading its first move.

The chimera stopped four meters off and tilted its head, the same precise movement as before, as if it were deciding the angle of approach. Park didn't give it time to decide — he closed the distance first, a short jab to the center to read the density before committing to anything more.

The chimera let him come and at the last instant twisted its torso, letting Park's fist pass grazing its side. The counter came immediately: an arm opening toward Park's throat, faster than any gwisin all night.

Park turned and got clear of its range in one motion, two meters, driving the inertia into his right leg and launching himself with just enough speed to dodge the strike — barely. That thing was dangerous, even for him.

That was where it really began.

---

At the same time, the Elder dokkaebi read the distance and closed first — the same impossible speed as always, fifteen meters in under a second, the long arm tracing the same arc it had used all night against the agents.

Kiki didn't retreat. She let the arm reach almost to range and at the last instant cut sideways — not at the arm, at the shoulder, the joint where the arm met the torso. It was a test, not the real strike: she wanted to see whether the Elder protected the joint or only the point of impact it expected.

The Elder protected nothing. It changed vector mid-motion — the arm that should have finished the arc pulled back, and the whole shoulder rammed toward Kiki, its full weight behind it, a move no dokkaebi had shown all night.

Kiki didn't fully dodge it. The shoulder clipped her side and lifted her off the ground, the katana losing the angle of the cut she'd planned, and she landed rolling, a full second out of position while the Elder was already closing distance again.

She hadn't expected that.

She got up with the katana out front, on the defensive for the first time in the fight, and blocked the next blow with the flat of the blade instead of cutting — there was no time for anything more precise. The impact shook her whole arm up to the shoulder, the tremor running straight down into her hand — the burned skin of her palm pressing against the grip harder than the wound could take without protest. Kiki didn't loosen her hold. She couldn't afford to.

The Elder pressed on, the same long arm tracing a second arc before Kiki had fully regained her balance.

Kiki spun under the arc, more instinct than reading, and in the turn found the angle she'd lost before — the blade crossed the forearm in a clean line, mass concentrated at the edge, severing the limb above the elbow. The wound's dense Odem sparked at the cut without the heat ever reaching the katana.

The Elder didn't scream — it had nothing to scream with. It stepped back, the closest thing to surprise Park had seen it show all night.

It didn't stay there.

The stump where the forearm had been didn't bleed — the wound's dense Odem gathered at the cut, compressing, and when the Elder turned back toward Kiki it led with the stump, not its remaining arm. Kiki, still recovering her balance from the last exchange, couldn't get clear in time — the stump struck the edge of her guard and released the whole compression in a wave that wasn't contact at all, pure pressure, spreading out from the cut as if the wound itself had become the mouth of a weapon.

The wave caught her fully. The Class-R on her forearm lit to maximum trying to absorb what hit her, but it wasn't enough to cancel it out — the impact lifted her off the ground and threw her three meters back, the katana nearly slipping from her hand on landing. She rolled, got up late, a thin ringing in her left ear that hadn't been there before.

It hadn't done that before. None of the three entities from the clearing had done that, and it took Kiki a full second to bring the katana back up to position.

The perimeter no longer looked like the clearing where Kiki had opened her case. The Elder's pressure wave had cut a new furrow in the ground, different from the bowl's fractures — wider, shallower, the earth pushed outward instead of in. Two of the trees still standing at the edge had bark torn away in parallel strips, marks of the fight. The dust kicked up by the successive blows never finished settling — every new impact sent it back into the air, and for the agents at the edge the fight was no longer fully visible, only silhouettes moving inside a cloud that wouldn't clear.

The Elder didn't give her time to find her rhythm again. It came again, its remaining arm leading this time, low, at leg height — a different attack from before, adapting without anyone feeding it the data. Kiki jumped the sweep late, her ear still ringing, and the jump came up shorter than she'd planned — she cleared the Elder's arm grazing instead of clean. In the air, with less control than she wanted, she twisted her body so the descent would still carry the blade toward the dense core of its chest.

The Elder pulled back half a meter — not enough. The diagonal cut caught the edge of the core, leaving the entity's edges flickering between definition and collapse, but didn't split it clean. Kiki landed badly, one knee hitting the ground before she could control the fall, her free hand cushioning the impact against churned earth — already-burned skin scraping against the surface, a thin new line of blood surfacing through the old red. She got up reading the damage on both sides: the Elder wounded, slower, down to one arm and a wound that could now fire pressure without warning. Not down. And she wasn't whole either — the arm under the Class-R ached from inside the material, her ear still ringing, her supporting hand dripping slowly onto Zanma's grip every time she closed it, her breath shorter than she'd have liked to admit.

The Elder read something in that moment neither of them expected: that it was losing. It broke the axis of the fight — instead of turning back on Kiki, it pivoted and charged on a diagonal, crossing the space between the two fights, straight at Park.

It was after the one already wounded, not the blade cutting it apart.

Park caught it coming at the edge of his vision, mid-exchange with the chimera, no time to turn fully or absorb another impact — his left arm already at its limit, his right busy reading the chimera's pattern. There was no margin to take on the Elder too.

He didn't take it.

In the half second he had, Park didn't absorb the Elder's vector — he barely touched it, a graze with his left forearm, just enough to read the direction and add a single degree of deflection without spending anything he had left. He used inertia. The Elder, coming straight at Park, went off-axis three degrees to the right. Redirected.

Three degrees were enough.

Kiki didn't need anyone to tell her where it would land. She was already turning, katana in position, when the Elder crossed the space Park had just opened for her. The blade went into the core, the same point the earlier cut had marked, deeper this time — the dense core split into two uneven halves, the edges of both losing definition at the same moment.

The Elder fell. It didn't fully dissolve.

Both halves stayed there, on the ground, edges flickering between collapse and something that wasn't quite stillness yet — the same state the first dokkaebi Kiki had closed in the clearing had been in, minutes before it got up again. Kiki didn't drop her guard. She held two meters back, katana still ready, reading whether the pattern would repeat.

She didn't fully lower the katana, didn't look at Park, didn't say anything — her attention split between what lay on the ground and the ringing that hadn't left her ear. Park didn't look at her either — he was already back with the chimera, the earlier exchange never having fully paused. They hadn't spoken. They hadn't needed to.

---

The evolved chimera was already on him again, probing once more — another feint, its right arm faking toward Park's face and veering halfway to his side. Park read the feint late and absorbed the real impact on his left forearm, pain climbing to his shoulder.

*It's reading my patterns,* Park thought.

He had no time to think anything else. The evolved chimera tried again, this time a gesture toward Park's support leg — another feint, another read on his reaction. Park didn't bite. He stayed planted, absorbing the real strike when it landed on his shoulder a second later, and in that moment he understood something: the entity was testing him with the same logic he used to test his enemies. Read before committing.

Two could play that game.

Park faked a direct strike at the center of the figure — not real, a gesture to see how it reacted. The chimera moved to block the center.

There was the pattern: it still responded to the visible gesture, not the real vector, same as the gwisin all night.

Park changed the angle mid-motion — the real fist went to the side, not the center — and connected. The strike landed clean, but the chimera was already turning with the impact at the instant of contact, absorbing part of the damage in the spin instead of taking it whole. It didn't fall. It stepped back three paces, straightened, and crouched to pick up a rock fragment from ground Park's own strike had fractured back in the bowl.

The same trick. His trick.

The fragment wasn't aimed at Park's head. He understood that half a second too late — the throw was clumsy enough that Park tracked its trajectory with his eyes a fraction of a second longer than he should have, and in that fraction the chimera had already closed the four meters between them. Its fist caught Park's side before he could read the real vector, a clean hit that knocked the air out of him and sent him stumbling back.

First time it had really connected.

It hadn't been clumsiness. It had been bait.

Park had no time to process how humiliating that was — the chimera was already coming behind the strike, no pause, a hook to the same side meant to finish what the first blow had opened. Park absorbed what he could on his left forearm, not much, the arm nearly empty, and used what little he took in to throw himself backward instead of standing there for the rest.

That was where the real exchange began.

The chimera advanced and Park retreated at the same pace, both reading each other in real time — a feint at the face Park didn't bite, a low fake at the legs the chimera didn't buy either when Park tried it back. Neither of them could land a strike clean: every real attempt came fractured by the other's read, deflected, half-absorbed, dodged by centimeters.

The chimera threw a straight punch, no feint this time, full weight behind it. Park didn't dodge it fully — he had no margin left — but used the last inertia in his support leg to shift his body three centimeters at the last instant, enough for the fist to graze his jaw instead of connecting full. The air the punch displaced still cost him a second of disorientation.

The chimera's fist, finding nothing where Park had been, carried on to a tree at the edge. The impact wasn't just the sound of splitting wood — it was a shockwave, visible in the dust that blasted out in a ring from the point of contact, the whole trunk vanishing into splinters instead of breaking.

That could have been his head.

Park counterattacked the instant the chimera still had its arm extended from the miss — a short hook to the ribs, real this time, no feint. It connected. The chimera folded for half a second, and that half second was enough for Park to land a second strike, weaker, at the same point.

The chimera answered with an elbow Park didn't see coming until it was already on him — he dodged it on pure reflex, snapping his head back, the elbow passing a centimeter from his nose. Behind the elbow came the other hand, open, reaching to grab the way it had grabbed before. Back in the bowl.

Park didn't let it close its fingers. He dropped a fraction, the inertia of his own weight carrying him under the reach of the hand, and from there threw a rising strike to the jaw that connected clean.

The chimera stepped back, touching its jaw with a gesture that looked almost human.

The two of them stood three meters apart for a second, breathing — if the chimera breathed at all, something on its surface rose and fell as if it did — each assessing what was left of the other.

Park stepped back twice, reading what stood in front of him with a clarity that hurt: this wasn't a big, slow dokkaebi learning to hit harder. It wasn't the Elder either, with a burst of speed that formed a clear, readable pattern. This was something starting to think the way he thought, and every exchange gave it more data on how to do it better.

In the retreat, for the first time since the exchange had started, he had a second to look past the chimera.

The whole perimeter was marked — not just the Elder's furrow, but the trail of his own footwork, three circles of churned earth where he'd planted his foot to strike, the tree the chimera's fist had just turned to splinters, another split in half that he didn't remember knocking down. Ji-ho stood in the same spot, arms crossed, not having moved a centimeter through the whole exchange. Watching.

Park had no time to wonder what that stillness meant.

What reserve he had left wasn't much. His left arm barely responded. His right carried the accumulated cost of the whole night, plus what he'd just spent in that exchange. But he had one thing the chimera hadn't seen him use twice: the full strike, both phases of his Domain at once, the entire reserve converted into a single instant.

He'd already used it once tonight, against the Elder, in the bowl. He didn't know if his body would hold up for a second.

The evolved chimera came at him, direct this time, no feints — as if it too knew this would be decided in one exchange, not ten.

Park went to meet it.

At three meters he began concentrating everything he had left into his right fist — not just the Chukgi reserve, nearly spent, but the whole weight of the night, the clearing, the bowl, the destroyed trail, everything his body had absorbed and never released. No Class-R on that arm, nothing holding the structure from inside — only bone and tendon carrying what had been building for weeks.

He never finished loading it.

The chimera read the shift before Park could complete it — something in his breathing, in the rhythm of his steps, in the way his right arm had gone a beat too still — and it didn't wait for the concentration to finish. It closed the entire distance at once, faster than it had been all fight, straight at the point where Park was still halfway through loading.

Park had a fraction of a second to decide: finish loading and risk taking the chimera's impact with nothing to answer it, or release what he had now, incomplete, with the chimera almost on him.

He didn't decide. Both things happened at once.

The chimera reached range in the exact instant Park released his reserve — not a calculated, clean strike, but the whole accumulated force let go at once against a target already in motion, colliding with the full momentum of the chimera's own charge. The two forces met at the same point in space in the same fraction of a second, and what should have been an exchange became, for an instant, something closer to an explosion — a pressure wave that burst from the point of contact in every direction, the ground beneath them both pulverizing into a crater unlike any impact that night.

Both of them were thrown clear.

Park flew backward, no control over the motion, his whole body shaken by the same wave he'd helped create. He landed rolling, out of air, unable for a second to tell which part of him hurt and which had simply stopped answering.

The chimera flew the other way. Park's fist, nothing stopping it halfway, had caught it fully in the instant of collision — not across the whole chest, at one point, the full force concentrated where the fist connected before the two waves crossed. The impact tore a clean hole through its torso, side to side, the wound's edges unraveling inward instead of out, as if something had torn the dense core straight out of the figure's center.

It fell ten meters away, and didn't move.

Park took a while to get up. When he did, it was slow, one arm first to push off the ground, the other hanging, no help at all — both arms at his sides once he was standing, the right one numb, the left barely responding since the hit in the bowl. Battered, not out of the fight: his legs held him, unsteady, his head still working. But both arms were, for now, more weight than weapon. His right fist hadn't come out of the collision intact — knuckles split open, the skin beneath exposed and bleeding, something inside the hand that didn't answer quite right. Park couldn't say what. With no Class-R to have absorbed part of the impact, the whole arm felt wrong on the inside, something past fatigue.

Then the ground shook.

Not from Park's strike. Not from the Elder, still on the ground, not yet fully dissolved, or from the chimera. Something deeper, farther off, a tremor that climbed through the soles of everyone on the perimeter at the same moment — Kiki, katana still trained on the Elder's two halves, turned toward the island's interior without fully dropping her guard. Ji-ho was already looking that way. The four agents with the units went still without anyone ordering it.

Park's reader, still in his pocket, buzzed with an intensity that matched nothing they'd faced that night.

Something much bigger than the Elder and the chimera combined had just noticed they were there.
