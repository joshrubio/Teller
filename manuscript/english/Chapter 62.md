# Chapter 62 — Warmth

---

The notice arrived that morning on paper, folded into quarters, one per door.

*South courtyard. Four in the afternoon. Attendance recommended.*

It was Maren's handwriting. The signature too. No protocol stamp, no form number. The paper was the same they used for the laundry lists.

---

At ten past four, the south courtyard had three long tables pushed against the west wall, four stacked plastic chairs nobody had set out yet, and Pier in an apron over his blue sweater, tending something that smelled of burnt wood and lamb fat. Pier didn't ask for help. Pier didn't need help — Pier had the posture of someone who's spent decades being the first to arrive at things and the last to explain why.

Sabine sat on the edge of one of the tables with a drink nobody had given her — she'd found it or brought it, which in Sabine amounted to the same thing. She watched the courtyard with the quality of attention of someone who already knows what's going to happen and is waiting for everyone else to find out too.

Petra arrived with cups. She set them on the first table with a precision that didn't need to be showy to be precision.

Riku came in after that, Dara beside him. They sat on the low stone lip that separated the courtyard floor from the garden outside it, and stayed quiet a moment, watching Pier as if the fire he was building looked like a technical operation worth observing.

Nadia arrived with something wrapped in kitchen paper. Nobody asked what it was. She set it on the table and went to find Asha.

Yusuf arrived on time, greeted Pier with a gesture that didn't require an answer, and found a spot along the wall where the sun still reached. He leaned there, arms crossed. It was his field posture, but looser — as if the field were this courtyard and the mission were to stay still.

Lev was the last of the early arrivals. He sat on the ground, back against the wall, knees drawn up. He said nothing. Nobody expected him to. There was something about his presence that filled the space differently than silence did.

Park came in through the side access with Hana. He looked at the courtyard, saw Pier, saw the fire, saw the tables, and the expression that crossed his face was hard to classify — something between recognition and recalibration. He took up a spot near the center, neither close to nor far from anyone in particular.

Maren arrived with no apron, no list, no tablet. She came with her hands in her pockets and an expression that, on her, meant something was going according to plan.

---

Oliver and Sisi arrived at the same time.

They hadn't come together — Oliver from the east wing, Sisi from the north residential corridor — but they reached the south courtyard access in the same second, and both stopped for an instant before either one said anything.

Sisi looked at him. Oliver looked at her.

"Hi," Oliver said.

"Hi," Sisi said.

Neither of them moved, for a second exactly as long as it needed to be. Then Sisi went in first and Oliver followed, and the courtyard absorbed them both with the efficient indifference of a space that already had enough people in it.

---

The afternoon ran longer than anyone would have predicted.

Asha found Sisi before Sisi had even finished getting her bearings — called her name without raising her voice, as if she already knew exactly where she was, and Sisi went to her with the cadence of someone who's received an invitation she wasn't expecting and decides to accept it before she can think too hard about it. They sat on the low stone next to Riku and Dara. Asha said something nobody else heard. Sisi nodded slowly — the kind of nod that closes one thing without opening another — and then, slowly, said something back.

Oliver watched them a moment from across the courtyard. Then Pier put a cup in his hand without asking, and he followed the cup to the table where Sabine and Petra were sitting.

"What is this?" Oliver asked, looking at the contents.

"Wine," Sabine said.

"The good stuff," Petra added. "The kind Pier saves for occasions."

Oliver looked at Pier, still turned toward the fire, adjusting something with a pair of tongs.

"What occasion?"

Sabine and Petra glanced at each other. Not surprised — with the expression of two people who share a code and are weighing how much to explain.

"It's Voss's birthday," Sabine said.

Oliver processed that.

"Voss has a birthday?"

"Everyone has a birthday, Oliver," Petra said, with the cadence of someone who doesn't add more context because the rest is understood.

"We've celebrated it since the first year," Sabine said. She looked at the fire. "It started because Pier suggested it and nobody said no, and now it's just a thing."

"Does Voss come?"

"He shows up," Sabine said. "For a while. Long enough for us to tell him we came, and long enough for him to leave without it looking like an escape."

Oliver looked toward the side access. He pictured Voss crossing that threshold with the precision of someone who's already calculated exactly how long he can stay at a celebration before it turns into something else.

"Good," he said, finally.

Petra looked at him with something close to a smile.

---

Voss arrived at a quarter past five.

He crossed the courtyard with the same walk he used in the corridors — measured, unhurried, occupying exactly the space needed — and stopped a moment at the threshold, taking in how the group had distributed itself with an attention that wasn't cold, more the attention of someone who knows exactly what's in front of him and is choosing where to start.

He chose Yusuf.

He went straight to the wall where Yusuf still stood, arms crossed, cup in one hand. It wasn't a visible decision — it was the kind of movement that looks casual because it's calculated so well. Pier intercepted him with a cup before he got there, and Voss took it without breaking stride.

What followed lasted fifteen minutes, and nobody caught it from a distance.

But the group saw it. It was impossible not to: at some point in those fifteen minutes, Voss said something — short, no gesturing — and Yusuf answered with something just as short, and then both men laughed. It wasn't a big laugh. It was the kind that belongs to something specific and old, to a story that needs no context because the context lives in both of them at once.

Pier, from the fire, watched them a second. He said nothing.

Nobody said anything.

It was the first time anyone at the Center had seen Yusuf laugh like that. It was, almost certainly, the first time anyone at the Center had seen Voss do it.

After that the two men went back to talking normally, and the group returned to its own conversations, and the moment sat unnamed in the courtyard the way things sit that don't need naming to matter.

By six, Voss had made something like a quiet circuit. Thirty seconds with Park by the side access — operational exchange, both of them nodding — then a while with Maren at the second table. Maren had pulled out her notebook at some point. Voss looked at it. Maren put it away without anyone saying a word. It was the first time anyone at the Center had watched him wave off work in a social space, and nobody pointed it out loud, which was the only way it could be pointed out at all.

He ended up by the fire, standing a meter from Pier. The two of them watched the embers without finding it awkward. Pier picked up the tongs, adjusted something. Voss said two or three sentences quietly. Pier answered at the same volume. It wasn't a conversation that needed anyone else to hear it.

Close to seven — conversations overlapping, the wine going around, the fire built up high — Voss stepped away from it and took up a position near the center of the courtyard without asking for one. The silence arrived before anyone organized it.

"Thank you for coming." No preamble. He looked around the courtyard, at how everyone had settled, at the fire. "This year's been difficult in ways not all of them were in the protocols. We'll keep working. That doesn't change."

Nobody answered. There was no need to — it was one of those statements that doesn't ask for agreement, it assumes it. Voss nodded once, the same way he closed out his sessions.

He didn't leave right away. He stayed a while longer by the north wall, cup in hand, with no visible conversation. Twenty minutes, maybe less. Then he crossed back through the access door with the same walk he'd arrived on.

The courtyard carried on without him as if it had always been built to do exactly that.

---

Night fell without anyone announcing it.

The tables filled, emptied, filled again. Pier's fire went from a grill to a point of heat conversations gravitated around. Someone — Nadia, probably — found more wine. Maren and Hana ended up sitting together at the end of the second table, papers between them they'd long since stopped reading, talking about something that had nothing to do with any subject present.

That was when the game started.

Sabine and Nadia did it standing, facing each other, palms held out toward one another. Everyone else noticed it differently: Riku looked up, Dara narrowed her eyes, Pier kept drinking without turning around, though his posture went slightly more alert.

From the low stone, Sisi saw it too. She was half-listening to a conversation between Asha and Dara when Sabine's Anchor opened up in the space — not visible, but present, like the moment before pressure changes. Sisi turned her head. Looked at Sabine. Looked at Nadia. Then looked at Oliver, sitting on the stone bench across the courtyard.

What Sabine and Nadia were doing was this: bringing their hands closer. Slowly. And somewhere along the path between distance and contact, the Domains woke up — Sabine's first, the Anchor spreading outward in a radius Oliver, in another context, would have described as a mesh of stable fixed points; Nadia's answering with concentrated Impulse, contactless force looking to push before the Anchor could set. It wasn't combat. It was closer to a conversation in a language with no words.

Sabine won. Nadia laughed, shook out her hand as if something had stuck to it, and sat back down.

Oliver had been watching from the stone bench.

"What is that?" he asked.

Nadia looked at him.

"A game," she said. "Want to try?"

Oliver looked at Sabine. Sabine looked back at him. She didn't exactly invite him — but she didn't close the question either.

"Yes," Oliver said.

---

He lost the first round before he knew it had started.

Sabine's hands were half a meter from his when the Anchor hit — no warning, no transition, just the moment his feet stopped being an option and the ground became the only surface in the world. It wasn't pain. It was the absence of movement presenting itself as an unappealable physical fact.

The group laughed. Not cruelly — with the recognition of something they'd all seen before.

"Reading the signature doesn't count," Nadia said, still laughing. "I already tried."

Sabine released the Anchor. Oliver moved his feet to check that he could.

On the low stone, Sisi didn't laugh. Her head was tilted slightly, eyes on Oliver — not on what had just happened, but on what was coming next.

"What was I doing wrong?" Oliver asked.

Pier set his cup on the table and came over. He had the manner of someone who'd waited to be asked before explaining.

"You weren't about to do anything with your hands. You were going with your hands." Pier held out his own, palms up. "The Domain isn't in the fingers. It's in the Odem, and Odem doesn't start at the skin—it starts further in. Once you know where yours is, you can feel someone else's reach you. Before you notice it in the body."

Oliver listened to that. Filed it. He remembered the training courtyard, a fight he'd watched Park observe in silence a few weeks back — Yusuf and another subject, bodies working from the inside out, as if movement were consequence, not cause. The question Pier had just asked without knowing he'd asked it.

"Again," he said.

This time it was with Nadia.

The Impulse hit the same way the first one had — no warning, no apology. Oliver went flying a meter backward, feet dragging against the flagstones before his own momentum decided it had done enough. Asha was there when he steadied. She put a hand on his arm without saying anything, which from Asha was the equivalent of quite a lot.

Sisi kept watching. She hadn't changed position since the first round — back straight, hands in her lap, attention held with the concentration of someone saving something for later. She wasn't watching the result. She was watching the instant before the result.

"Again," Oliver said.

Sabine raised an eyebrow. Not a no — an evaluation.

"With Sabine this time," he said.

Sabine set down her cup. Stood. Held out her palms.

Oliver looked at them. He thought about what Pier had said. Thought about the Odem as something that comes before the skin — distributed, no identifiable frequency, simply present. The same thing he'd spent nights trying to locate from his bed without knowing exactly what he was looking for.

Sabine's hands drew closer.

Oliver didn't think about the hands. He thought about the point three fingers below his sternum where he'd found something that morning. And waited.

The Anchor came the same way it had the two times before. But this time Oliver felt it half a second early — not in his feet, but somewhere more internal, in the way Sabine's Odem expanded toward him before it locked. And in the space of that half second, without quite deciding to, the Anchor's structure stopped being something that pinned him and became something he was reading.

Then it pinned Sabine.

The silence lasted two seconds.

Sabine looked down at her own feet. Tested them. They didn't move.

"Huh," she said.

Oliver released the Anchor immediately. Sabine shook one leg, then the other, checking.

Lev, who hadn't moved from the ground all night, lifted his eyes toward Sabine. Not toward Oliver, not toward the group — toward Sabine. Sabine saw it. Neither of them said anything. There was something in that silence that didn't feel like the first time they'd had it.

Riku glanced toward the corner where Maren and Hana were still talking. Both women had their eyes on the papers. Hana was pointing at something with her pen.

The group let out a breath.

"Well," Pier said, with the cadence of someone who's just confirmed a hypothesis he's been waiting a long time to confirm. "That's different."

Nadia was looking at Oliver with an expression that was half irritation and half something harder to name.

"Next time," she said, "I'm not letting you try twice."

Oliver didn't answer. He had the specific feeling of someone who's touched something without knowing yet whether he's broken it or fixed it.

From the low stone, Sisi was watching him. Not the way she had for the two earlier rounds — something different, stiller. When Oliver turned his head that direction, without quite meaning to, her eyes were already there. She didn't look away. She nodded once, barely, and went back to her conversation with Asha as if nothing had interrupted anything.

It took Oliver a moment longer to do the same.

Pier hadn't been watching when he said what he had to say. But by the time he turned back to the embers, he'd already caught Sisi's nod — and caught Oliver taking one second too long to look away. He picked up the tongs. Said nothing.

---

Toward eleven, the courtyard began emptying naturally — no announcement, just the mechanics native to spaces that have already served their purpose. Pier gathered the tongs. Dara folded napkins nobody had asked her to fold. The fire kept burning unattended, smaller, quieter.

Oliver walked back toward the residential wing with Riku on his left and Sisi on his right, without any of the three of them having quite planned it.

The central wing's corridor was empty at that hour. Their footsteps on the stone, the low hum of the heating, a lamp still not quite bright enough at the far end.

Riku said something about Pier's Domain — the way he'd watched him adjust the fire without seeming to touch it — and the conversation carried on from there, effortless, with the quality of an eleven o'clock conversation that doesn't need to arrive anywhere. Oliver talked. Sisi answered. Riku asked something about Sisi's first months at the Center that wasn't really a tactical question so much as genuine curiosity.

Sisi answered.

What she said wasn't long, but it had the texture of something not usually said out loud — something about learning to tell apart what you feel from what other people feel when they're close, and how for months she hadn't been able to tell whether what she felt was hers or borrowed. Oliver listened and said nothing, which was the right answer. Riku said nothing either, but his silence was different — more active, like someone taking note of something that mattered to him.

They reached the fork in the corridor. Riku's wing went left. Oliver and Sisi's, right.

Riku looked at the two of them. He looked at them in a way that didn't need to be explicit to be exactly that. Oliver looked back at him with an expression trying to say *don't* as clearly as possible without saying it.

Riku was already turning left.

"Good night," he said, without looking back.

Oliver and Sisi continued down the right-hand wing.

Sisi's room was three doors before Oliver's. They walked it in silence — not the awkward silence from before the courtyard, but the other kind, the kind that doesn't need filling.

Sisi stopped at her door.

So did Oliver.

"It's been a strange day," Sisi said.

"Yeah," Oliver said.

Sisi nodded. Slowly, like someone confirming something she'd only half worked out. Then she opened the door and went in without adding anything else, and the door closed with the specific sound of the Center's doors, which were old and heavy and never made noise without reason.

Oliver continued down the corridor to his own door.

Inside, with the light off and the window showing the black cutout of the Alps against a cloudless sky, he sat on the edge of the bed and thought about what Pier had said about the Odem. Thought about the moment before he'd pinned Sabine. Thought about Sisi saying *I didn't know if what I felt was mine or borrowed.*

He didn't arrive at any particular conclusion.

But he slept better than he had in weeks.
