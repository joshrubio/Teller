# Chapter 120 — What the Water Took

---

Before Valais, before Sabine, before anyone with an AMAT badge knew his name, winter was measured in tasks, not dates.

Lev broke the well ice every morning with the same splintered-handled mallet, and every morning, before the mallet touched the surface, he pulled the cold out of his own fingers and pushed it into the frozen crust — not to break it faster, just to keep holding the handle for another twenty minutes without his hands stopping on him. Nobody had taught him to do it that way. He'd worked it out alone, at fourteen winters old, the afternoon he realized the ache in his fingertips wasn't the same as the other kids' in the valley: it moved when he wanted it to move.

He cut the firewood without the Domain's help — that he saved for things that actually needed it. The roof, for instance. Every two or three days he'd climb up in spiked boots and pull the heat gathered under the south-facing shingles, the heat that half-melted the snow and let it turn into a sheet of ice heavy enough to snap a beam under its own weight if nobody did anything. He'd concentrate it at one point in the gutter, release it all at once, and twenty meters of soft snow would come down in a single dull sigh. Halvard's reindeer, in the neighboring pen, had stopped spooking at the noise. They'd gotten used to him before any person had.

He knew exactly how much he could take from a thing without ruining it — how much cold to pull from a pipe before the metal itself started to give, how much heat to steal from a river stone before it cracked from the inside without warning. That calculation, more than the strength, was the part nobody in the valley would have known how to name if they'd seen it. They didn't need to know. It was enough that the well kept giving water and the roof stayed standing.

He ate alone, radio off most of the day because the news from beyond the valley never changed what he had to do that afternoon. At midday he'd sit for a while on the front step, no coat, letting the weak February sun touch his face while the fog of his own breath dissolved without him having to do anything about it — the one moment of the day he wasn't touching anything, fixing anything, carrying the weight of any body besides his own.

When he finished — the well open, the wood stacked, the roof clear, the water in the bucket without a single skin of ice — he'd sit in front of the fire he actually lit with ordinary fire, ordinary wood, the one fire all day that cost him nothing to pull from anywhere, and he'd stay there until the cold outside stopped mattering.

That particular evening he smiled, with no one there to see it. Not because anything had happened. Because nothing had, and that, that winter, still counted as enough.

---

Park closed the last stretch of the perimeter himself, without handing it to Marc.

The channel opened into a wide backwater there, after three kilometers of narrow canal where anyone with half of Lev and Sabine's strength should have gone under before reaching it. Elena had marked the bend on the map as the most likely exit point — the current lost force, the bank became walkable, there was a service bridge two hundred meters off with sightlines both ways. Everything pointed to it: if they were still alive, they'd surface there or nowhere.

"They might have no intention of coming out where we're waiting for them," Marc said, checking the fastening on a vest that needed no checking. "A whole month proving they don't do the expected thing."

"I know," Park said.

He didn't add anything else, and Marc didn't push, though he lingered a second too long looking at him before heading back to his post. There was something different in the way Park held the silence that night — not the usual calculated calm, but something closer to certainty, a certainty with no new data behind it. Elena had noticed too, without saying so. Sofia, the scar still pulling under her sleeve, was the only one who held his gaze a moment before taking position, as if she expected Park to explain where that confidence came from and already knew, in advance, that he wouldn't.

The real odds of them showing up exactly there, Park calculated without telling anyone, didn't reach one in four. He took his post anyway, on the stretch of bank closest to the water, and waited with the kind of patience that doesn't invite discussion.

The river carried more noise there than it let on from the bridge — water against stone, against root, against the team's own silence spread through the dark. Elena checked the map by a half-covered light, jaw tight the way it gets after three days of reading an ever-staler trail. Sofia hadn't moved from her spot since they arrived, hand near the scar without touching it, watching the black water as if she expected it to hand back something different from what it had taken from her on that loading dock. Nobody mentioned the smoking gap in the canal wall, or the two shapes swimming for the far bank faster than exhaustion should have allowed. No one needed to. They were all carrying the same tally without saying it out loud.

---

He found him before dawn, and it wasn't the perimeter that did it. It was chance — Park walking the edge of a bend that wasn't his to cover, following an instinct he didn't bother naming even to himself.

Lev came out of the water alone, on all fours in the mud, clothes plastered to his body and hair hanging over his face. He didn't run at the sight of Park. He had nothing left to run with. He stayed there, half risen, turning his head to both sides of the bank with a panic that looked nothing like a fleeing man's — the panic of someone searching for something and unable to find even the right spot to start looking.

"Where is she?" he said, not looking at Park, as if the question were for the river and not for him. "Can you see her? Tell me you can see her."

"You're alone," Park said.

That was enough to make Lev truly see him, and what crossed his face wasn't recognition of danger. It was something older, something he'd already been carrying before he ever left the water.

He lunged with no plan behind it, a closed-fist swing Park blocked without real effort, and felt, in the same instant of contact, Lev trying to pull something out of the air between them — a half-formed tug of heat, clumsy, incomplete, that came apart before it turned into anything. There was no reserve left to draw from. A month of running had taken that first, before the strength in his arms, before his voice.

Up close, in the gray light starting to filter through the trees, Lev looked nothing like his file. Knuckles split and not fully healed. Lips cracked in two separate places, one of them bleeding again with the effort. Under the soaked shirt, the outline of ribs more visible than they'd ever been in the Valais photographs. He smelled of river mud and something older, sour — the smell of a body that had spent weeks burning through whatever it no longer had to spare.

Lev swung again, no heat behind it this time, just body against body, and Park let the weight of the impact land exactly where his Domain had been quietly accumulating for half an hour without anyone noticing. The impact sounded wrong, a dry crack that should have dropped Park and instead bent Lev's wrist back against his own chest with a wet snap, and the sound he made wasn't rage.

There was no elegance in what followed. Lev bounced off the mud like he'd hit something weighing three times what it looked like, got up once more on pure reflex, the wrist already useless at his side, and didn't try a third time. He stayed down, face half-sunk in the mud, swallowing more of it than air, shoulders shaking with a tremor that wasn't cold.

"You don't understand," he said, voice scraped raw, barely any air behind it. "You could never understand."

Park didn't come closer yet.

"She's the only thing that kept me sane in that hole in Valais," Lev went on, rolling slowly in the mud, no strength left even to lift his chest off the ground. "And the last time I saw her, her mouth was open, screaming my name, and the water swallowed her before I could reach her."

He lay there a moment, breathing mud more than air, and then said it again, quieter, as if repeating it could change the order things had happened in.

"Screaming my name. That's the last thing I have of her."

---

Park thought of Zoltán Báthory, on the bridge, the exact second he'd let the Grillete's strike through on purpose so he could send it back multiplied — the cleanest decision he'd ever made, because there was nothing in Zoltán that deserved anything else. And he thought of Oliver, long before any of this, a collapse on an eighth floor and a decision not to treat him as a case file when every protocol said he should. The other cleanest decision he'd ever made, for the exact opposite reason.

Two ways of looking at someone on the ground in front of him. Never the same, and yet every time he had to choose between them he caught himself asking the same question: what did this particular person deserve, not what the file said they deserved. Lev wasn't Zoltán. He wasn't Oliver either. He was something without a name yet, halfway between the two decisions, and Park was going to have to invent a third with no real precedent to hold it up.

"Do you really think you can do anything for her, the state you're in?" Park said, not moving closer, his voice carrying no heat Lev could use against him.

"You have no idea what it is to be free," Lev said, not lifting his head from the mud. "Really free. I had it, before all this. Before Valais, before this country, before someone like you decided I was a case to solve."

"Being strong sometimes also means carrying the consequences of something you can't control," Park said.

Lev let out something that didn't quite become a laugh.

"That sounds like something said by someone who's never actually lost anything."

Park didn't correct him. There was a version of that sentence he could have told him — a bridge, a body that fell in a way it shouldn't have, a name that still showed up in reports Park filed without rereading — but it wasn't the moment, and Lev wouldn't have wanted it anyway.

"Maybe I could never understand you," Park said, finally, "but I don't need that to do something about it."

Lev lifted his head for the first time since he'd gone down, something like hope crossing his face before reason caught up with it.

"Are you going to—"

"I can swim faster than you can swim right now," Park said. "And I know exactly where this current ends before it opens into the lake. You don't."

It wasn't entirely true, and Park knew it as he said it. What he knew with the same certainty, and didn't say, was that if Sabine really had gone under where Lev believed, there was no one — not him, not Lev, not the whole team — who could change the outcome now. He said it anyway, in exactly the tone it needed to sound like a promise and not something else.

Lev pulled himself up however he could, staggering, and turned back toward the water without Park making any move to stop him. He stood on the bank a moment, watching him wade back into the current with arms that barely answered him anymore, and didn't move again until Lev's head disappeared past the first bend.

---

Four hours later, in Elena's earpiece, Park's silence no longer read as focus.

"Two hours with no response," Marc said, voice tighter than it had been all month. "I'm going to check the vehicle."

He came back sooner than expected, something on his face Elena had never seen him wear before.

"One of the cars is missing," he said. "And his things are on the bank, where he said he'd cover the bend. Phone, backup earpiece, his jacket and ID. There are marks in the mud that aren't one person walking."

"Marks of what?" Sofia asked.

"Of something that wasn't quiet," Marc said.

Nobody said out loud what all three were thinking at the same time, because saying it would have meant admitting how long they'd already been, without admitting it, doubting the same person: that Park had spent weeks making decisions no one else would have made, that the last time he'd had the whole angle to end this cleanly he'd chosen protocol and lost anyway, that maybe tonight he'd decided not to lose the same way twice.

"If something's happened to the two of them on their own," Elena said, slowly, choosing every word like it carried weight, "we're going to have to explain why we didn't see it coming."

"Or explain what's happened to him," Sofia said.

Neither of them finished the sentence that came after that. Marc bagged Park's phone without anyone asking him to, the exact gesture of someone who's already seen how something without a name yet gets documented. Elena stayed looking at the footprints in the mud, Park's clear, the others dragged, as if trying to read in them the same kind of irregular texture she'd spent three days reading in Lev and Sabine's trail, without finding anything that looked like an answer.

---

The call came in before they could decide who'd take the first step toward the one car they had left.

It was Park.

The hospital smelled of disinfectant and the damp of three people who'd spent the night in the water. Park stood by the doorway of the room, mud still cracking dry on his shirt, looking toward the gurney where Lev lay unconscious, an IV in his arm, chest rising and falling with a machine's forced rhythm.

In the chair by the window, eyes swollen from crying and an emergency blanket over shoulders that had stopped shaking, Sabine watched him without saying anything. She had a makeshift bandage on her forearm, superficial, nothing that would have needed a hospital on its own. Someone had given her dry clothes from somewhere. No one, yet, had asked her what she remembered from the hours before Park found her, lying on the ground and covered in mud.

Sabine stayed there, watching him with something Park, in that moment, couldn't tell was gratitude or the exact start of a question that would take its time arriving — one that, when it did, he would already have decided in advance how much of the answer it deserved to hear.
