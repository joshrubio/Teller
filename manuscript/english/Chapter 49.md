# Chapter 49 — Transit

The meeting had started at seven.

Maren arrived last, which was unusual, and sat down without explaining the delay, which was not. Hana already had the documents arranged on the table. Yusuf stood by the back wall with his arms crossed, in the posture he used when he'd rather not participate but felt he should be present. Voss occupied the head of the table, and to her right, with a thin folder and the tone of someone who has made a decision and come to announce it rather than discuss it, sat Brenner.

Oliver didn't know Brenner yet. But his name was in the email Hana had sent that morning to call the meeting, and Pier had read it over Hana's shoulder while she looked for something else, and that was enough for Pier to know what the meeting meant before it began.

What followed was what Pier had reconstructed in pieces, that afternoon, from what Maren hadn't said in the three o'clock session, from the way Hana had closed her tablet when Pier walked into the administrative corridor, from the specific way Yusuf had looked at the floor during morning training.

Brenner was Coordinator of Operational Assignments for the Geneva branch. He didn't evaluate. He didn't train. He arrived when a subject's file had crossed the threshold between *still in development* and *ready for use*, and he took what the agency needed.

What the agency needed this week was someone who could walk through walls.

---

The news reached the dining hall before breakfast.

Not all at once — that wasn't how things arrived at the Center. It arrived the way almost everything did: in the way someone was sitting, in the silences between sentences that normally had sentences, in the specific absence of Tomás at the table where Tomás always ate breakfast alone, because Tomás ate breakfast alone by choice, and that morning the choice was no longer his.

Oliver arrived at eight-twenty, read the dining hall in the first ten seconds, and didn't pour himself coffee.

Pier was waiting for him in the corridor.

"Come," he said, without the tone of someone asking. Without the tone of someone ordering. With the tone of someone who has something to say and has calculated that this is the right moment to say it.

Oliver went.

---

The room was one Oliver hadn't used before — not a session room, not the library, not the dining hall. A room in the west wing with four chairs, a sealed window, and the look of a space that existed for purposes no one had needed to define precisely until now. Sabine was already there when they arrived. Petra came in a minute later. Her calm looked deliberate — earned from enough hard conversations to know that calm is a tool before it's a state. Asha arrived last, closed the door without a sound, and stayed standing beside it.

Lev wasn't there.

"We invited Lev," Sabine said, before Oliver had asked. "He said he'd think about it."

No one elaborated.

Pier sat with his back straight and his hands on his knees, in the posture he used when he was about to talk about something he'd thought about longer than he would have liked.

"Brenner came for Tomás," he said. "Operational assignment. Formal collaboration under deployment protocol, not integration protocol. That means he doesn't choose when he's used, or for what."

"Does he know where he's going?" Oliver asked.

"No. That's part of the protocol too."

The silence that followed had the weight of something no one in the room was processing for the first time.

"When did you find out this was going to happen?" Oliver said.

Sabine answered. She had the voice of someone who has learned to deliver bad news without wrapping, because the wrapping doesn't change the contents.

"Three weeks ago. When Maren's monthly report changed the language it used to describe Tomás's Domain." She looked at her hands for a moment. "In evaluation reports there are words that mean *still in development* and words that mean *operationally viable*. When the language changes, Brenner gets a copy."

"How do you know what language Maren uses in the reports?" Oliver asked.

Sabine looked at him.

"Because I've been here four years and I've learned to read what's available."

Oliver took it in, then looked at Pier.

"And you?"

"Eleven years," Pier said simply. "I know which doors are open when. I know which conversations happen in which spaces. It isn't spying. It's institutional survival. You learn to read the system around you when the system surrounds you long enough."

Petra spoke then. She had a way of entering conversations at the exact moment she added something the others hadn't said yet.

"The formal collaboration protocol has two variants. One is what Sabine and I have — we stay here, we work with the Center, we have a degree of continuity. The other is external assignment." She looked at the sealed window. "Tomás has the second. What that means in practice is that AMAT is going to use him for whatever AMAT needs, and it's going to tell him exactly as much as it decides he needs to know about it."

"Did he choose it?"

"No."

Petra's answer carried no judgment. It was just the answer.

Oliver looked at Asha, who had been listening from the door with her arms crossed and the expression of someone waiting for the conversation to reach the point that mattered to her.

"This has happened before," Oliver said. It wasn't a question.

"Twice, as far as I know," Sabine said. "Before I arrived. Pier knows more."

"Three times," Pier said. "In eleven years. Every time someone's Domain crossed a threshold of tactical usefulness before the person had reached the point where they could make decisions about how to use it." He said it without heat. He'd processed the emotion behind it long ago; what was left now was only the data. "The second time it was someone who'd been here two years. Nineteen when he arrived. He left at twenty-one."

The silence that followed was the kind that fills a space without pressure.

"What happened to him?" Oliver said.

"I don't know. That's part of the protocol too."

Oliver looked at the stone floor of the room. The same stone floors as the rest of the building. The Center had the floors of something that had been something else before.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked.

No one answered right away. Pier looked at Sabine. Sabine looked at Petra. It was the kind of pause that exists when several people have discussed something beforehand and decided who says it.

It was Petra who said it.

"Because what happened in the courtyard yesterday reached everyone before noon." She looked at Oliver with the direct attention she used when she wanted no room for misunderstanding. "Maren walked into the courtyard. She doesn't do that. The radius exceeded the safe area. And you talked to everyone afterward as if what had just happened was something between the people who were in that courtyard, not between a subject and her evaluator."

"And what does that mean to you?"

"It means," Pier said, "that you have something none of the previous Registry cases had. Not just the copy. The way you use it. The way you relate to what you see."

Oliver waited. He'd learned to wait when the conversation hadn't yet reached the real point.

"Some of us have gone years without really understanding what we have," Sabine said, and it was the first time Oliver had heard something from her that wasn't pragmatism. "What the Domain does in the abstract, yes. What it does with Odem, how it looks from outside, what structure it has when it's active... that we don't know. Maren documents it. We don't see it."

"Riku knows now," Asha said, from the door. "Not everything. But more than he knew two weeks ago. And that changed something in how he works with him."

Another silence. This one had a different shape than the last.

"Lev doesn't want to," Pier said. "We're making that clear so there's no confusion. His reluctance isn't about you — he doesn't want anyone, AMAT included, to have visibility into what his Domain does. He's managed alone for too long to hand it over easily."

"I understand," Oliver said.

Pier continued.

"The three of us are in formal collaboration within the Center. Petra two years, Sabine four, me eleven." Pier rested his elbows on his knees. "What that means in practice is that at some point each of us reached an agreement with AMAT about the terms. We know what's asked of us. We know where we stand. That's what Tomás doesn't have right now."

Petra picked up the thread.

"The formal collaboration protocol has two variants." Her calm wasn't borrowed from a file. She knew this firsthand. "The one Sabine and I have is continuous residence: we stay at the Center, we work with AMAT on terms we've negotiated, we have a degree of continuity. The other variant is external assignment. You don't choose to go. You don't know where. You don't negotiate the terms."

"That's what happened to Tomás."

"Yes."

"Sabine has her own reservations about this," Petra added, with the same calm. "Not about the process. About what AMAT would do with the information if it had it."

Sabine didn't deny it.

"If you can see what my Domain does," she said, "Maren can also read whatever you report. I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm telling you the mechanics."

"I know," Oliver said.

"And?" Sabine said.

It was a real question, not a rhetorical one. The kind asked by someone who has spent enough time in institutions to know that the answer to that specific mechanic says more about a person than almost anything else.

"I'm not going to report anything you haven't given me permission to report," Oliver said.

"Can you do that?"

"I don't know yet. I've never been in this situation. But that's my intention."

Sabine filed the answer away in some provisional category — not trust, not distrust. Information pending more data.

Pier spoke.

"We're not asking you to turn against the Center. We're not asking you to cover anything up." He leaned slightly forward. "We're asking that, if you see something useful, you tell us before AMAT decides what to do with it."

"Why?"

"So we can make informed decisions about what we are," Petra said. "About what we're capable of. About when the threshold Tomás crossed is getting close for each of us."

"So we're prepared," Asha said, more direct than the others. "So we don't end up where Tomás ended up without having chosen it."

The room went still.

Oliver looked at the four of them — Pier with his back straight, Sabine with her hands in her lap, Petra calm now that she'd said what she meant to say, Asha by the door. Four people who had spent between two and eleven years navigating a system that made decisions about their lives with the same efficiency it used to make decisions about any other resource. Who had built, in that time, the ability to read the system from the inside without the system knowing it. And who were now offering Oliver something they hadn't offered anyone before.

It wasn't a pact. It wasn't a conspiracy. It was, simply, the question of whether they shared a vision of what this place should be for them.

Oliver didn't know yet if he had a better one.

But he knew theirs didn't strike him as wrong.

"All right," he said.

Tomás had been in transport three hours when he lost count of the time.

It wasn't the blindfold — he'd already learned to calculate time without sight during the months of transfers to the Center. It was the combination of the blindfold with the restraints and with the knowledge that this transfer was different from all the others. The others had a known destination. They had an estimated duration. They had Maren waiting on the other side with a tablet and the question of how he'd slept.

This one had none of that.

The restraints were organic — pressed wood, treated plant-fiber composite, the kind of material that blocked Transit because the Domain couldn't pass through something carrying living Odem, even processed wood. Someone had researched this. Someone had designed these restraints specifically for him. The idea of that — of being a problem specific enough to deserve a specific solution — should have produced something like satisfaction. It didn't.

It produced fear. Tomás called it by another name, as he always did, but it was fear.

His family had tried to suppress the incident. Forty-eight pages of material fatigue documentation, three lawyers, two non-disclosure agreements. They had tried to buy it, the way his family bought the problems that could be bought. AMAT wasn't the kind of problem that got bought. Tomás had understood that too late, when the initial transport was already underway and his parents had already signed something he hadn't been able to read.

Months later, the anger was still easier to sustain than the rest.

The vehicle stopped.

It wasn't the usual stop — not the gradual deceleration of arriving somewhere, but the abrupt stop of something unexpected. Tomás heard voices outside, brief, the specific exchange of people resolving a problem with urgency. Then silence.

Then footsteps.

Not the footsteps of the two guards who'd been in the vehicle since the start. Fewer footsteps. One. Maybe two.

Tomás didn't move. The restraints wouldn't have allowed it anyway.

The voice came from close — too close to be outside the vehicle, too low to be part of the protocol.

"Easy."

One word. No context. No name. No instruction of any kind.

Tomás waited, his heart in a rhythm that no longer pretended to be calm, for whatever came next.
