# Chapter 98 — My Father's File

---

The Classification Room always smelled the same, no matter the hour — old paper, something metallic underneath that nobody had ever fully identified, and the air conditioning set a degree colder than it needed to be. Nadia had grown as used to that smell as to the rest of the routine, to the point of no longer noticing it, until this morning.

The instructor — an older man, the kind who'd been at St. Gotthard so long nobody remembered whether he had a Domain or not — projected a flowchart on the screen, the kind of diagram any office in the world could have produced to explain how a vacation request gets approved.

"This is how a new case gets classified," he said. "Intake of the initial report, cross-check against existing files, preliminary level assessment, regional branch assignment, formal file opened. Five steps. None of them require the person carrying them out to understand what they're classifying. Just that they know how to follow the procedure."

Nadia knew that diagram. Not from class. From her father's table, spread out over the tablecloth some Sunday when he'd brought work home without thinking about who else might see it — the same five boxes, the same arrows, the same small handwriting in the margins he used to jot down doubts he never quite got around to resolving.

"Cross-check," the instructor said, advancing the slide, "means, in practice, comparing the new report against a database of similar cases. Most of the time the system finds something. When it doesn't, that's when a case gets interesting."

Nadia raised her hand before fully deciding to.

"Who does the cross-check?" she said. "Someone specialized, or is it standard administrative work?"

"Administrative, in most cases," the instructor said, finding nothing strange in the question. "Branch staff, not field agents. It's office work. Boring, most of the time."

Boring. Her father had described it exactly that way, more than once, complaining about the volume of files without ever raising his voice, without ever once naming a single case. Nadia had grown up thinking her father managed generic paperwork for a generic agency. Now, sitting in this room, she understood, with a clarity she hadn't asked for, that her father's generic paperwork was, probably, the first filter people like her had passed through — like Oliver, like Riku, like any of the names she now knew in person — before anyone with more authority decided anything about them.

It wasn't a monster that had made all of this possible. It was her father, on some ordinary Monday, bored, following five steps.

That was worse.

The instructor kept going, unaware of what he'd just stirred up in a corner of the room. He projected a second diagram, denser, with more boxes and more arrows crossing each other.

"When the cross-check finds no match, the case moves automatically to preliminary level assessment," he said. "That's where someone with real judgment gets involved, not just procedure. But before it gets there, it's already passed through at least two people who never saw more than a case number and a box to check."

Two people. Nadia thought about her father checking boxes for twenty-five years, never knowing — or knowing, and never telling her — that one of those boxes had decided, somewhere down a much longer chain, that a teenager with a new Domain would end up exactly where she had. There was no way to ask him directly. Not without admitting how much she now knew about the side of the process where she herself had been, in some file, a checked box.

She stayed fixed on the diagram long after the rest of the class had moved to the next slide, trying to decide whether she'd rather her father had known exactly what he was marking, or never known at all. Neither answer left her any better off.

---

She found Riku that same afternoon in the hallway by the secondary training rooms, not looking for her — looking for Pier, with a clumsy determination that wasn't his usual register.

"Can you help me with something?" Riku said, stopping Pier before he reached the door. "Not for class. For me."

Pier looked at him for a moment, weighing the request with the same calm he applied to everything.

"Depends what 'something' is," he said.

"I don't know how to explain it yet," Riku said. "It's something I saw someone do. I want to try it myself, but I need someone who knows how to watch closely, in case it gets away from me."

He didn't say Iker's name. He didn't need to — Pier had been at St. Gotthard long enough to recognize, in the way Riku avoided saying it, exactly who he meant.

"Is it dangerous?" Pier said, not as an objection, as a real question that needed an answer before he'd agree.

"I don't know," Riku said, with an honesty that seemed to cost him. "That's why I'm asking you. I'd rather it go wrong in front of someone with judgment than just in front of me."

Pier nodded, once, and opened the door to the empty room without saying anything more about it — the kind of silent acceptance that, evidently, Riku needed more than any word of encouragement. Nadia, who'd come down the same hallway for a different reason, lingered a moment in the doorway before continuing on, thinking that somewhere in the last few weeks, without anyone announcing it, everyone in the group had started seeking each other out for the things they couldn't do alone.

---

Noa Shapira was in the same Classification Room when Nadia came back that afternoon to return a manual she'd taken by mistake, sitting alone in the back row, reviewing the same flowchart with an attention that didn't look like it needed it to memorize anything.

"Your face, this morning," Noa said, without looking up, as Nadia passed near her. "When the instructor mentioned the administrative work. You recognized something."

Nadia stopped. She hadn't thought anyone else had noticed.

"My father," she said, finally, without fully deciding to share it. "He works for AMAT. Administrative. I'd never really thought about what he actually does until today."

"I don't have that problem," Noa said, closing the manual. "In my family, everyone's passed through those same five boxes on the diagram, in one post or another, for generations. There's no single day where it hits me all at once. I've always known."

"And does that feel better or worse to you?" Nadia said.

Noa thought about the question longer than Nadia expected.

"Neither," she said, finally. "It's just what there is. I don't have the luxury of asking myself whether I could feel something else. Any crack in me would be a crack in something much bigger than me, and I can't afford that."

Nadia didn't know how to answer that. She stood there a moment longer, the manual still in her hand, feeling the distance between them more clearly than ever — Noa, who'd never had the option to doubt; herself, who'd just discovered that doubting hurt too.

"Have you never wondered what would've happened if your family hadn't been inside it?" Nadia said, finally, with more care than she'd put into any other question that afternoon.

"All the time," Noa said, without hesitation, which surprised Nadia more than anything she'd said until then. "But wondering it and acting differently because of it are two separate things. I can do the first every day. The second would cost me everything I have, and not just me."

She said it with no drama, with the same economy she brought to everything else, and that was what stuck with Nadia most — that Noa didn't need to underline the weight of the sentence for it to feel heavy.

"I think you're lucky," Nadia said, without fully thinking it through before saying it. "Not having to do what I'm doing right now."

Noa didn't answer right away. She picked up the manual, stood, and for a moment it looked like she might leave the sentence unanswered.

"I know you don't mean it badly," she said, finally, without real coldness, but without giving ground either. "And I know you helped me a few days ago without asking anything in return. I don't forget that. But I can't afford to think about luck, not mine or anyone else's. The day I start wondering what it would feel like to have room to doubt, I'll have already lost something I can't afford to lose."

They didn't get any further than that. Noa left first, and Nadia stayed alone in the room, the flowchart still projected on the screen, the five boxes and their arrows looking back at her as if nothing she'd just felt mattered at all to the system that had drawn them.

---

Anka found Oliver that same night, in the same outdoor courtyard as before, already seated when he arrived, as if she'd known he'd be passing through.

"Sit," she said, without turning.

Oliver sat, the stone already familiar and cold.

"I heard you used something new at Val Tremola," Anka said, no preamble. "Momentum, for real, not as an exercise. Someone got hurt."

"How do you know that?" Oliver said, the same question as always, the one that never got a full answer.

"I don't know it from the outside," she said. "I know it because whatever you've been storing up changed shape the night you used it. It's not just dense anymore. Now it has edges, like something that's been used for real for the first time and can't go back to being what it was before."

Oliver said nothing for a moment, letting the sentence settle.

"Is that bad?"

"I don't know," Anka said. "But it's different, and different things deserve someone looking at them carefully before deciding whether they're a problem."

They sat in silence a while, the same silence Anka never seemed in a hurry to fill.

"Someday," Oliver said, finally, with more care than he'd put into a sentence in a long time, "I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to know I've been thinking about it for a while before deciding to."

Anka turned her head toward him, the same gesture as always that followed no active Odem.

"I know," she said. "I've been waiting for it since you arrived."

She didn't say anything else. Neither did Oliver. They parted that night with the certainty, new and heavy, that the question he'd been putting off for weeks was no longer a distant possibility — it was something the two of them, without fully saying so, had started counting down the days toward.

---

She found Petra later, near the quarters, and sat beside her without asking permission, the same quiet trust they'd given each other since the hallway outside the infirmary.

"My father," Nadia said, no introduction, because with Petra none was needed either. "Today I really understood what he does every day."

"And?" Petra said.

"And there's nothing to hate," Nadia said. "That's what I wasn't expecting. I wanted there to be something to hate, a monster, a cruel decision somewhere down the chain. But it's just... procedure. Boring people following five steps."

Petra said nothing for a moment, letting Nadia finish saying what she needed to say.

"I don't have anywhere else to go, since he brought me here," Nadia said, finally, the part that cost her most. "And now I know exactly how ordinary the day he decided to bring me was."

"That doesn't make the decision to stay any less yours," Petra said. "The origin of a door doesn't decide what's on the other side of it."

Nadia didn't answer right away. She sat looking at the empty hallway, thinking about five boxes, five arrows, and how many other lives had passed through the same diagram without anyone, ever, stopping to wonder if it hurt on the other side.
