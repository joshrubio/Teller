# Chapter 101 — The Big Exercise

---

They set out before dawn, in two unmarked vans, along a mountain road that kept narrowing until it dropped off every map Oliver had ever seen. When they climbed out, the air cut differently than it did at St. Gotthard — thinner, colder, with that particular silence of altitude where even a switched-off engine sounds out of place. Nobody talked much through the last hour of the drive.

On Reber's maps, the wall was called the North Face of Pizzo Centrale. In everyone else's mouth, it was just "the wall." Seen from the starting esplanade, before the sun finished climbing, it was a gray mass eating half the horizon, fog still tangled in the first fifty meters and loose shreds unraveling higher up, where the wind had already taken hold.

Two hundred twenty meters of gray rock, three descent lines marked with reflective tape — one for Valais, one for Johanna's group, a third closed off, held in reserve, that nobody mentioned out loud but everybody's eyes found anyway. Reber explained it once, without repeating himself, his voice barely clearing the wind: simultaneous descent, two teams, one stopwatch. The goal was to reach the evacuation ledge at the foot of the wall with the full gear complement. Everything else — how each team organized itself, who went down first, how they covered the blind spots — was left to each group to decide.

"This is what real training looks like," Pier said, checking Nadia's harness a second time without her asking him to. "No one goes down alone. No one waits their turn with their arms crossed."

Oliver looked at the rock. Clean Contours where nobody stood yet, no active signature except his own, dormant. Nothing anomalous. Not yet. Across the esplanade, Johanna's group was already warming up in formation, everyone's breath rising together into one small cloud over their heads.

Eight days. Ever since Noa had put a hard number on the cycle — one full month, three attempts, nothing more — Oliver hadn't been able to stop counting. Eight days until a month passed since they'd arrived at St. Gotthard. No one in Valais had said it out loud yet, but today's exercise carried the specific weight of something done when there's no more room left to practice without it counting.

---

The first match wasn't on the wall. It came earlier, on the starting esplanade, where Reber announced that each team would put forward a "champion" to decide the descent order — whoever won got to choose the starting point, and the northernmost point meant less wind, less exposure, more margin. Valais chose without discussion. Riku stepped forward before anyone said his name.

On the other side, Johanna's group put forward Noa Shapira. No Domain, none needed — her feet already set, her weight already distributed before anyone gave the signal to start.

"Ready?" Riku said, no arrogance in it, more a real question than a challenge.

"Always," Noa said.

They stepped off to a square of packed dirt marked out in lime. Oliver stayed at the north edge, next to Pier. He saw Riku's field before Riku moved: a closed node over his left forearm, dense, still — not the usual diffuse interference, something concentrated, almost fist-sized.

Noa opened the match with no field to read, only body, measuring distance with the same economy she used when she spoke. Riku tested her first — a feint to the torso, not the real strike. She didn't bite. She just turned her shoulder two centimeters, just enough to clear the line of the real punch if it came, and waited.

"I've seen that one before," Noa said, still moving. "Whole generations spent training people to fake a hit just to see what the other guy does."

Riku didn't answer. He changed the angle mid-step and threw the real punch, his right fist loaded from the inside with the same Static he'd only ever used to interfere before, never to hit — straight to her side, where the feint had left her just barely open.

It landed. It didn't look like much of a hit, but Noa felt it differently — she doubled over for half a second, the air cut off in her chest, and stumbled back two steps instead of one.

"What was that?" she said, already straightening up, looking down at her side.

"I don't know how to fight with my head either," Riku said. First time Oliver had heard him quote someone on purpose.

Noa didn't give him time to enjoy it. She closed the distance before Riku could reset his guard, caught the wrist of his loaded arm with both hands — a move Oliver had never seen from Iker, or from anyone at the fight club, something learned somewhere else, out of a different tradition — and used Riku's own forward momentum to take him down with a clean armlock, no extra strength needed.

Riku couldn't break free. He slapped the ground twice with his free palm, the signal to submit, and Noa let go the instant he did.

He didn't win the match. But when Riku got up, brushing the dirt off, he didn't wear the face of his other defeats. He wore the face of someone who'd just confirmed the path existed, even if he didn't know how to walk the whole length of it yet — and who'd learned, along the way, that a loaded punch wasn't enough on its own either, not against someone who knew exactly what to do with the wrist of the person throwing it.

"The starting point's still yours," Riku said, holding out a hand to Noa, who shook it without ceremony.

"Try it again with your low guard fixed," she said. "That's where I tied you up."

Pier, beside him, said nothing. But when Riku passed him on the way back to the line, he tapped his shoulder twice, brief, and Riku ducked his head so no one else would see him smile.

---

They went down in three simultaneous rope teams: Pier with Riku on the center line, Asha with Dara and Petra on the south line, Nadia and Oliver covering the middle point, radio open across all three groups. On the other side of the wall, thirty meters north, Johanna's group descended in a different formation — two teams of three, no one working solo, faster through the first forty meters.

"They're sixty seconds ahead," Asha said over the radio, no urgency in her voice.

"Let them be," Pier said.

Oliver read the exchange without needing more context: Asha measured time, Pier measured margin. It wasn't the first time they'd disagreed like this, quietly, neither one giving ground. Across the wall, Johanna was calling short instructions with no pauses between them — every candidate knew exactly when to move before she finished the sentence. Command by anticipation, not by reaction.

By the first forty meters, the gap between the two walls was already less than sixty seconds. Nadia found the reason before Oliver finished working it out himself: at every redirect point, unclipping one carabiner and clipping the next cost half a second too many — half a second multiplied across six points, across five people. She solved it her own way — a short pulse of Impulse pushed outward against her own body, kicking herself sideways to the next anchor without ever fully letting go of the rope, and in the same motion, without pausing, an inward discharge that killed her swing right before she landed, leaving her still at the exact point instead of having to correct afterward. The first time Oliver saw her do it, he thought it was a slip. The second time, he understood it was technique — repeatable, deliberate, something she'd been practicing in private, telling no one, until she found a real use for it.

"That saves you three seconds a point," Oliver said over the radio, unable to stop himself from doing the math.

"Four," Nadia said, with no breath to spare for more words.

With every redirect point, Johanna's lead shrank. By sixty meters, the formation on the other side wasn't descending as cleanly anymore — Oliver could hear Johanna correcting twice where she'd only corrected once before, the rhythm of her orders falling a little further behind the movements of her people. Pier noticed it too.

"We're catching them," he said, his voice tight with concentration, not surprise. "Keep it up."

Eighty meters down, with a hundred forty still below them to the evacuation ledge — no net, nothing in between but bare rock and the narrow outcrop Oliver had barely registered until that moment as just another irregularity in the wall — Petra lost her anchor.

Oliver saw it from his position, between the two lines, fifteen meters to the north: the carabiner meant to hold the south line's redirect rope came open for no visible reason — fatigued metal, or badly closed, or both — and Petra fell.

It lasted a little over a second, but it didn't feel that way: her whole body spinning out of control, a scream that never quite finished forming, the loose rope whipping against the rock two meters above her, useless. Oliver counted without meaning to — one, two — the time stretching in a way that had nothing to do with how fast she was actually falling.

"Petra!" Asha's shout, fifteen meters south, already moving toward the nearest redirect rope, her hands slipping on the knot one second too long. She wasn't going to make it. She seemed to know it herself halfway through the motion, and kept going anyway.

"Nobody moves from their point!" Pier's voice cut across the whole radio, loud but never cracking, a tone Oliver had never heard him use before. "Asha, hold what you've got. Riku, stay put. Nobody improvises a jump that leaves us with two people hanging instead of one."

No one argued. The fear in Pier's voice was there, whole, underneath the control — Oliver heard it anyway, the way his breath caught between one sentence and the next — but the control won out, and that was why everyone followed his orders instead of their own instincts.

Oliver didn't move. He wanted to — his whole body was begging him to let go, cross over, do something — and he couldn't find anything to do that wasn't exactly what Pier had just forbidden. He ran through every copied Domain in half a second, looking for one fast enough at that distance, and found nothing that qualified — Momentum wasn't enough, Impulse had no clean angle from where he stood. Whatever needed time to work was worthless with less than two seconds ahead of him. He stayed where he was, hands closed uselessly around his own rope, watching.

Petra didn't scream again. Her eyes searched for mass comparable enough to Swap into — the whole reflex of her Domain — and found nothing in time: the loose rope moved too fast to fix her eyes on, and the rock wasn't a valid target. Twisting her body to try to read a third point, her temple struck the rock — a glancing impact, not the one that would have killed her, but enough to black out her eyes halfway through the fall.

She was searching for nothing anymore by the time the wall threw her, limp, toward the rock outcrop eight meters below — the one point on the whole face where stopping was even possible, and only if something or someone got there before she did, because whatever came after the outcrop, if she overshot it, no longer had a technical name. Just distance.

Pier, on the center line, turned his head toward the scream and could do nothing — no possible angle from where he stood, his hand closed around his own rope with nowhere to take it. For half a second, something crossed his face that had nothing to do with tactical calculation: he'd seen her the day before, sitting across from him in the rope room, still undecided about Vienna, and the thought arrived whole and ugly before he could push it away — that the question she hadn't finished answering for herself might no longer be hers to answer.

Dara was twelve meters south, halfway down her own rope, when she understood no one was going to make it in time — not Asha, not Pier, no one with rope of their own close enough. She couldn't let go either without falling herself. She didn't scream, didn't hesitate, didn't ask if anyone had a better idea. All she had was the Double, and she used it.

---

She didn't think it through. Oliver, from his position, watched the Double take shape directly on the outcrop, already planted, legs spread and arms ready, in the same second Dara finished shouting the order — not falling to reach her, but already there, before Petra covered the last meters separating her from the rock.

The catch wasn't clean. Eight meters of free fall doesn't stop without a cost: Petra's body, already unconscious, hit the Double at full speed, and the Double itself had to give ground with the impact to keep from shattering against the rock. No last-second reflex, no arm reaching to grab hold — just dead weight falling onto another body.

It worked. And it wasn't enough.

The outcrop was too narrow for two bodies and offered nothing to hold onto — no edge, no crack. Petra, unconscious, couldn't grab anything herself, and the momentum of the catch started tipping the Double toward the edge, in the exact instant Oliver mistakenly thought it was already over.

The second Double didn't appear because of any decision made in advance — it appeared because the real problem didn't reveal itself until the first one was already occupied and running out. It took shape beside the broken anchor, eight meters above the outcrop, gripped the loose rope with both hands against the rock, and pulled until it found tension again — the same rope still tied to Petra's harness. The pull was sharp, with no room to cushion it: Petra's body, still unconscious, jerked against the harness, and her right shoulder gave way with a dull sound Oliver felt before he fully heard it. But it took the weight off the first Double that had been dragging it toward the edge.

Both dissolved almost together, seconds later, the effort exhausting whatever kept them whole. But it was enough: Petra ended up hanging from the rock, alive, her shoulder dislocated and her breath cut off, instead of falling the hundred-plus meters still left to the ground.

"I've got her!" Asha arrived two seconds later, pinning Petra to the wall with her own body while someone from the ground team secured a new rope from below. Her voice, over the radio, stayed steady. Her face, which Oliver managed to see once they finally crossed the same stretch of wall, didn't — one full second of something raw before she filed it back behind her usual composure.

The real Dara hadn't moved from where she'd been hanging the whole time. Oliver saw her let go of the rope with one hand and bring it to her mouth — real nausea, her whole body shaking in a way that had nothing to do with the cold. A thin thread of blood ran from her left nostril, something Oliver had never seen on her before. Her voice, when she finally spoke over the radio, showed none of it.

"She's secure," she said, and nothing else, as if she needed to say it out loud just to believe it herself.

Two Doubles at once, one solving what the first couldn't manage alone. She'd never done it before. She'd never known she could, and her body was collecting the full bill for it now, all at once — but while it lasted, nothing she did shook, not that Oliver could see.

Across the wall, Johanna had stopped her whole team cold, watching the Valais side without being able to help — the distance was too great. Once it was clear Petra was secure, she didn't pick the pace back up. The competition, as far as she was concerned, had ended right there.

Pier didn't stop anything. He kept lowering Riku at the same pace, giving short instructions over the radio so the rest of the team wouldn't waste time watching what Asha and Dara were already handling. But the moment he reached the evacuation ledge, before he'd even taken off his harness, he crossed straight to where Petra sat with her shoulder immobilized, waiting for a stretcher, and knelt in front of her without saying anything at first.

"You're still here," he said, finally, his voice lower than Oliver had ever heard him use.

"I'm still here," Petra said, still dazed, not quite understanding why those two words seemed to carry so much weight coming from him.

Pier didn't explain what he'd thought halfway through the fall. He stayed a moment longer, his hand closed over Petra's good shoulder, and stepped back when the medical team came to move her.

"Two ways of reading the same moment," Asha said, her pulse back to normal, her command voice back in place. "She stopped everything. You kept going."

"We were both right," Pier said. "She protected her people by stopping. I protected mine by not giving them time to think about what they'd just seen."

Oliver, sitting next to Dara while she still worked to steady her breathing, said nothing. His hands were still closed the same useless way they'd been minutes earlier, and he didn't know how to tell anyone that the only thing he'd done through the whole fall was watch.

"I didn't know I could," Dara said quietly, not looking at him. "I didn't even decide to."

"I know," Oliver said.

---

Petra woke hours later, in St. Gotthard's infirmary, her shoulder splinted, the light overhead too white for whatever they'd given her. She didn't remember the impact. She remembered searching for something with her eyes, and then nothing, and then that light.

"An accident," Asha said, sitting on the edge of the bed, before Petra finished asking. "The redirect carabiner. No one forced it, no one sabotaged it. It just opened."

"How long have I been here?"

"Five hours," Nadia said, standing by the window. "They said you'd wake up confused. That's normal, with the hit you took."

The two of them told her together, without dramatizing it more than necessary and without softening it either — the anchor, the fall, the outcrop, Dara's two Doubles. Petra listened without interrupting, her good hand closed over the sheet, and when they finished she lay looking at the ceiling a moment before speaking.

"Two Doubles," she said, finally, and something in her face shifted toward something like real joy, clumsy with exhaustion but genuine. "That's huge, Dara. We should be celebrating that, not sitting here watching me sleep."

"We'll celebrate once your shoulder isn't broken," Dara said, from the chair in the corner, not quite looking at her.

It was almost comfortable, for a moment — the four of them in the same small room, the bad light, someone botching a joke about climbing gear, Nadia stealing half of Asha's energy bar without asking. The closest thing to a family afternoon they'd had in weeks.

Riku came in without knocking, tablet still in hand, wearing the face of someone who didn't know if the news he was bringing was good.

"Board's updated," he said, without preamble. "No one moved. Not one spot, either way." He looked at Dara. "Except you. A hundred forty-nine to a hundred forty-two. You went up seven."

Dara said nothing. Neither did Asha. Nadia set the half-eaten bar down on the nightstand, and for a second nobody in the room knew what to do with the silence that number left behind — everything that had happened that afternoon, the fall, the rescue, Pier holding the whole radio together with his voice cracking underneath, and the only number the system had decided was worth moving was one.
