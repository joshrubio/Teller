# Chapter 24 — Reserve

Park was eight meters from the entities when the reader finished processing the signature and gave it a name.

**Dokkaebi.** AMAT-KR classification, category spirit of place or object — not of person. The difference from a gwisin wasn't one of degree, it was one of origin: a gwisin carried the shape of someone who had existed. A dokkaebi carried no prior shape. It was what was left when a place's Odem accumulated with no human mold to copy, and the substrate ended up building a body out of whatever was at hand — proportions that came from no anatomy, only from the amount of available Odem and the most efficient way to contain it. That was why the three from the clearing had no face, no clothes, no features a form could describe as "person." They were the island, not a memory of the island.

Park had read the reference file at the base — two pages, no photographs, most of the cataloged cases on the west coast. The common note in every file: burn on contact, resistance higher than standard containment, erratic behavior that matched neither prey instinct nor predator instinct. Nobody had classified the pattern because there wasn't a consistent pattern to classify.

What he had in front of him now, eight meters off, wasn't one of the three from the clearing.

Ji-ho appeared at the entrance to the interior.

He didn't come in. He pointed at three agents from the base and then at the trail where Park stood. A gesture to advance. The three agents crossed the edge of the vegetation and Park waited without moving. The reader in his hand kept marking dense readings downward.

When the three reached his position, Park turned and headed down.

The dokkaebi that had orbited the trail didn't attack them. They let them pass and closed in behind.

The terrain dropped at a steady angle. The trees changed proportions every fifty meters — more trunk, less canopy, roots above the ground instead of under it. At a hundred meters the outside light no longer reached the ground clean — the foliage above too closed, the terrain below too dark for what was midday on the surface. The agents behind Park had no flashlights, moving along the trodden trail.

Nobody turned on a light. Nobody suggested it. The progressive darkness felt like part of an unwritten protocol of the island — something nobody had put down on paper but everyone understood without being told.

The Odem in the substrate was different from the clearing's: not active, not moving, but accumulated, like decades of sediment. Park felt it in the soles of his feet with every step — a different density, older, that made each step cost slightly more than on normal ground. Not resistance. Weight. The reader wasn't jumping now — it climbed steadily, linearly, no visible ceiling yet.

"How much further?" The youngest agent's voice, almost a whisper, the kind of question that isn't looking for an answer so much as company in the silence.

Nobody answered. The sound of the forest had changed without Park being able to say at what point — there were no birds here, there hadn't been at any point in the descent, and only now, with the question hanging unanswered, did he notice he'd gone ten minutes without hearing anything but his own footsteps and the others'.

One of the agents said something else, so low Park didn't catch all of it — something about the silence, about not liking it. Park didn't answer. The dokkaebi that had been on the trail kept moving parallel to them between the trees, not closing in, not falling back, the distance so constant it seemed measured — not pursuit, escort. That was worse.

The trail opened into a natural depression.

It was a bowl about thirty meters across, the trees forming a ring at the edge. Around the bowl's perimeter: rusted metal driven into the ground at irregular intervals, some upright, most tilted, two or three torn out by the root with the metal bent outward as if something had pushed them from inside out. The 1954 anchors. Seventy years in the ground. Park counted the ones still standing: seven of the twelve visible.

At the center of the bowl, working around the remaining anchors like a river around stones, was something the reader didn't know how to number.

Park looked at the screen: the Level indicator flickered between readings for three seconds and went blank. The screen wasn't throwing an error — it simply couldn't find a category.

Nobody spoke. Not even the agent who'd wanted to talk for the last ten minutes. Park felt the group's silence behind him as physical pressure, the same question from the reader repeated in each of them without anyone saying it out loud: what is that.

Then they saw it. First as movement on the opposite side of the bowl — a shift of shadow that could have been the wind, if there had been wind. Then as a silhouette that didn't fit any of the surrounding trees. And then, with no recognizable transition between silhouette and shape, it appeared.

The dokkaebi was longer than it was tall, the wrong proportion for anything Park had encountered before. It looked older, ancient. The torso too long for the limbs holding it up, the shoulders at a height that didn't match the rest. No identifiable face — what should have been a forehead was the same dense, irregular material as the rest of the body, no feature to indicate where to look. The edges of the central core were visible from the bowl's rim, twenty meters off, like pressure in the air, like the space immediately around the entity had more resistance than the rest. The Odem on the surface heated the surrounding air in a way that wasn't temperature — the kind of heat the body reads as a warning before there's any damage.

"Shit," one of the agents said, very low, the word more than the voice.

The dokkaebi from the trail that had followed Park and the team stopped at the edge of the bowl. They didn't go down. They stayed there, at the exact boundary of the depression, as if they knew something about the space that Park didn't know yet.

The containment agent to Park's left pulled out the unit and stepped forward two paces to the edge of the bowl.

"I'm going to try—"

The Elder was already coming.

There was no protocol, no reading, no time for the sentence to finish. It crossed the fifteen meters in under a second, the body that a moment earlier had been working slowly around the anchors turned into a mass moving at a speed that didn't match anything about its size. The agent didn't even manage to raise the unit. The long, disproportionate arm grabbed him by the shoulder and slammed him into the floor of the bowl with a sound that wasn't just impact — something broke inside the sound, a dry crack Park recognized from Hartford.

The agent didn't scream. He convulsed once, whole body, shaking from the Odem entering through the point of contact, and then went still, eyes open, breathing too fast to be conscious.

"GET DOWN!" Park shouted at the other two, already moving.

The Elder didn't wait for the first agent to finish falling. It turned toward the second, the same impossible speed, and the second agent only managed to raise his arm — a reflex gesture, not real defense — before the impact threw him against a tree at the edge. The Class-R on his forearm lit up to maximum at the instant of contact and the bone still gave, an angle an arm shouldn't have. The agent fell at the foot of the tree and didn't move.

Two out in under four seconds.

Park had no time to process the count — the east edge of the bowl was already opening at the same time, the air between two trunks distorting, compressing, and then it appeared. A second creature. Grotesque, visceral, made up of what looked like dozens of gwisin parts imitating a human appearance. The chimera articulated itself out of that without a pause between one thing and the other, as if the two entities had been waiting for the same signal.

"What the hell is that?" The third agent, voice cracked, weapon already in hand but not aimed at anything fixed.

Park's reader buzzed: Level 2, Level 4, Level 1, the screen unable to settle on a number as the chimera crossed the bowl toward them without the slow approach of a normal gwisin — direct, fast, the faces shifting position with every step.

"Move!" Park was already running toward the third agent when the Elder, still at the point where it had left the second one, turned toward them too.

The two converged.

Park got there first, by half a second. He planted his right foot and absorbed the Elder's arm on his right forearm at the same instant the chimera reached the third agent from the flank — he couldn't cover both. The chimera's field passed through the agent along an axis no protocol predicted, and the agent dropped to his knees screaming something that wasn't a word, hands on his head, his spatial orientation broken in a way that wasn't visible, only audible in how he stopped being able to speak sense.

Three agents down. Less than a minute since the Elder had first moved.

The Elder's impact on Park's arm pushed him back two full steps — more force than he'd absorbed in any exchange of the operation. There was no time to read anything. Park turned his body with what he had and released the inertia to the northwest — the Elder went, fifteen meters, the edge of the bowl giving where it hit.

The second step back landed him on a fragment of a torn-out anchor, half a meter of rusted metal with a broken edge at an angle. Park grabbed it without breaking his motion — the hand closing on the metal in the same instant his foot touched the ground — and loaded into it the residual inertia from the retreat, the part that hadn't gone entirely into the previous strike.

The chimera crossed the bowl toward him, the faces still reorganizing from the last dispersal.

Park threw the fragment without stopping, his wrist turning at the last instant so the broken edge went in first. He didn't aim for the center — he aimed at the zone where three faces overlapped, the point where the chimera's signature looked most unstable. The metal crossed the eight meters in a straight line and went into the shape with a sound that wasn't metal against flesh — more like metal against something still deciding what texture to have. The chimera contracted entirely around the point of impact, the faces collapsing inward for one full second.

A second Park had no time to use. The Elder was already coming back, that same speed with no transition, and this time Park didn't wait for the contact — he pivoted on his planted foot and let the Elder's arm pass a hand's width from his torso, close enough that he felt the heat of the Odem on his skin without direct contact. In the same turn he closed his hand around another piece of anchor, smaller, and drove it into the Elder's flank as it was still finishing its own motion — no loaded inertia this time, just the edge and the force of the turn. The metal sank into the dense core and stayed there, vibrating.

The Elder didn't seem to notice. But it stopped for half a second — enough time for Park to complete the turn and, loading inertia into the axis, release a kick into the dokkaebi's body. He felt the dokkaebi's heat biting into his skin, and at the precise instant, let the inertia go. A torrent of force caught the dokkaebi like a rag doll, and the creature rolled across the ground.

The chimera, recomposed from the earlier fragment, closed from the flank in that same half second.

Park absorbed the attack with his left forearm without having fully recovered his balance from the previous turn, the pain climbing straight up from the point of contact, and redirected it south without fine control — the chimera twisted, recomposed, and extended a limb that caught him by the torso. Park turned and released a hook from the abdomen that sent it flying. The creature struck a trunk, the faces losing themselves in the dispersal, but Park already knew that hadn't resolved it, only pushed it back a second.

The Elder was coming again. That same speed, no transition.

Park had no time to think through a strategy — only to move. He threw himself toward the point where the Elder was going to arrive, not to wait for it but to hit first, and five meters out he concentrated every reserve he carried into his left fist — weeks of Chukgi, the clearing, the descent, more than he'd carried into Hartford — and released it all in a single strike, with no margin to calculate whether it was enough.

The floor of the bowl broke. His arm shook.

A four-meter radius, the fractures radiating out from his push-off foot. Two trees gave and fell. The Elder went fifteen meters north, the core contracted, the edges losing definition. How much longer? Park asked himself. At this rate, managing his reserves was no longer an option. The fight had escalated drastically.

There was no silence. And before he could find a solution, the chimera was already coming back from the south, faster than before, the faces reorganizing in motion without slowing its advance.

The Elder still on the ground. Four seconds, maybe five, before it fully recomposed.

Park used those seconds. One of the trees felled by his own strike had a branch broken off at shoulder height, a meter of wood with the tip splintered at an angle — not a spear, but it would serve as one. He tore it from the trunk in a single pull and loaded into it what little was left of the previous strike's inertia.

The chimera was ten meters off when he threw it.

He didn't aim at the body — he aimed at a specific point, the face that had stayed stable longest in the current configuration, the one Park had seen repeat twice across different exchanges. The branch crossed the distance and went in right there. The chimera stopped dead, the whole shape trembling around the point of impact as if the blow had reached something deeper than the surface.

It lost two full seconds reorganizing. Park used them to put distance between them and read the Elder.

The Elder got up before Park could recover his left arm — the Class-R translucent to the max from the elbow down, the fist not answering clean, and there was no time to check if it was all right. It was coming again, faster than the previous two times, the shape more compact.

Park absorbed the impact with his right forearm and the force lifted him off the ground before he could decide to let it.

Three meters up. No footing. Nothing under his feet.

He acted in the air with no time to plan — took the inertia still entering through his shoulder and redirected it south in a motion he'd never made before, not because he'd decided to but because there was no other way out. The trajectory bent. He came down eight meters south of where he should have landed, between the Elder and the chimera, his knees absorbing the impact of the landing before his body finished understanding it had worked.

There was no second to stand still and assess. Both were already coming.

The Elder from the north, the chimera from the south, closing at the same time, and Park had no margin to absorb either one clean — reserves nearly spent, left arm barely functional, right arm carrying the cost of the last impact. One step. That was all he had time to do.

He moved east, off the axis of both, at the last possible instant.

The two fields met where he'd been standing. The chimera's chaotic signature collided with the Elder's dense core — immediate interference, the Elder losing definition, the chimera losing coherence of faces, the shapes blending with no outline.

Two seconds. No more.

Park didn't think them through — he used them. He ran north with the last of what he had, no plan beyond getting the two entities to follow him out of the bowl, the vegetation closing in on the sides, the uphill trail underfoot.

Behind him, the two were already recomposing and coming. The Elder slower than in the bowl but not stopping. The chimera with its faces still unstable, closing the distance all the same.

The trees on the trail gave way to Park's passage — the shift of Odem he carried in his body shaking shallow roots, the earth lifting at the edges of his path. A tree tilted ten degrees as he passed within two meters. The Elder crashed through it behind him and the whole tree came down, the trunk splitting halfway up, cutting the trail a second after Park had already passed.

The trail destroyed behind him. The Elder and the chimera on it, following, not losing ground.

The vegetation opened.

The edge of the interior. The entrance to the trail.

Ji-ho was there. Kiki to his right. Four agents from the base with units deployed in positions spread around the edge.

Nobody shouted. Nobody asked what was coming behind Park. The whole team moved at the same time, with no verbal order, the units orienting toward the trail before the Elder had even finished coming out of it — the kind of coordination you only get after seeing enough things come out of that same vegetation.

Park didn't stop — he crossed the edge and turned to face the interior. The Elder came out of the trail eight meters behind him, slower than at any point in the fight, the surface still marked by the fracture from the first strike. The chimera emerged from the west flank, the faces trembling between configurations, more fragmented than before.

Still active. The fight went on.

Kiki looked at Park for a second — she didn't ask how he was, she didn't need to, the left arm hanging with a delay said it all — and then looked past him, into the interior.

Ji-ho looked at the reader in his hand. Not into the interior — north, toward the clearing and beyond. Something in the reading that wasn't the Elder or the chimera. Something coming from another direction, and from the way Ji-ho's face didn't change, Park understood he'd already known before looking at the screen.

"Kiki," he said.

It wasn't an instruction. It was information.

Kiki opened the case without asking what it was. Her fingers already knew the order.
