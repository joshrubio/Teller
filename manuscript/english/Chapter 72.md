# Chapter 72 — Proximity

---

Riku went back to sessions on Wednesday.

Maren announced it at breakfast with the economy of someone reporting, not celebrating: the left arm's signature was stabilizing, the Domain's function under controlled conditions was reproducible, Stage 3 needed further documentation before active training. Riku listened from across the table with his left palm open on the tray, looking at his own fingers, and said nothing.

Oliver had seen him do that four times that week. It wasn't the gesture of someone searching for the origin of something — it was the gesture of someone checking that what he remembered having is still there. That the hand answers the way it should. That the space between his thumb and index finger is the same distance it was before the forest.

The rest of the team picked the pace back up from the weeks before the mission with the unsettling speed of systems that don't know how to do anything else. Asha went back to instruction. Pier had theory sessions two mornings running. Park had been at the Center for four days and then hadn't been at the Center for four more, the usual pattern. Yusuf walked the outer perimeter Thursday morning as if the previous Thursday had never happened.

Oliver put in the passive hours Maren had prescribed, and in the gaps between hours he carried something he hadn't set down anywhere yet.

Nine days.

The man from the square — Martin, the false name with the last name that never landed — had described the cost of the Registry with a precision no protocol had ever given him. He'd named Sisi's situation in terms Oliver had never heard from anyone inside the Center. And Oliver had gotten back into Yusuf's van and said "Air" when Park asked without asking, and since then the information had lived somewhere between what he knew and what he did with it.

He hadn't reported it. He'd managed it as if it were his own.

Nine days of that.

---

The knock at the door came after ten.

Late for visitors — the Center had no formal curfew, but most of the team stopped moving around after nine thirty. Oliver was sitting on the attic floor with his back against the bed and a book on his lap he hadn't read in forty minutes. The Registry in passive, radius minimal, the kind of stillness Maren called "active rest," which Oliver had learned to recognize as the state in which unsolved problems grow fastest.

He opened the door.

Sisi.

She had her coat on, as if she'd come from outside or was about to go out. Hands in her pockets. She wasn't leaning against the doorframe — she stood without support, which Oliver had learned to read as a specific state in her: present, alert, not comfortable. The posture of someone who's made a decision and is checking whether it was a good idea.

She'd come to his door. It had happened once before, under different circumstances. It wasn't something Sisi did by default.

With anyone else at the Center, Oliver would have had the Registry to fill in what he saw — the Domain's passive frequency, the Odem density, the small map of tension that gave context to what a face didn't show. With Sisi he had none of that. Mind's Domain had no outward expression — no readable channel, no frequency to catch. Sisi was the one person in the building the Registry simply couldn't reach, and standing in her doorway at ten at night, that fact registered more than it ever did in any training session.

He had only what he had: posture, hands he couldn't see, whether or not she made eye contact.

"I need to talk to you," she said. Not a question.

Oliver stepped aside to let her in.

---

Sisi didn't take off her coat. She sat on the edge of the desk with her hands still in her pockets and looked around the room before looking at him — the visual sweep of someone who assesses spaces before speaking in them. Checking exits, probably. Or just the habit of not talking in places she hasn't inspected.

Oliver closed the door and stayed standing by the window. Far enough not to look like he was controlling the space. Close enough that the conversation wouldn't have to cross the whole room.

"I've been asking around," Sisi said. Her tone was flat. Informative. "Different people, so it wouldn't look like a pattern."

Oliver waited.

"I asked Nadia about unsupervised outings during a shared session, in passing. She's had two unsupervised hours a week since last month. I asked Dara at lunch about outside communication — she can make one unmonitored call a week, as long as she logs it twenty-four hours in advance." A pause. "I asked Pier, in the abstract, how the evaluation phases worked and when conditions changed. He explained the conditions without knowing exactly what I was doing with the answer."

That was investigative work. Distributed, careful not to trip any surveillance pattern. Oliver didn't say it out loud, but he logged it.

And she'd come to him. Not to Pier, who would have listened and documented. Not to Maren, who would have answered exactly enough. To him. Oliver knew why — he'd known since Sisi told him, four days after the evaluation session, what she'd done with the Domain and what she'd found. She knew he had an arrangement that ran outside the official channel. She knew he could sit on information if someone asked him to. She'd read that before deciding whether she could trust it.

"And then I asked Maren directly," Sisi went on, "whether my regime was going to change in the next few months." First time she looked at Oliver instead of at the fixed point on the wall. "The answer was too careful."

Oliver said nothing.

"What I have isn't evaluation." No emphasis. Like a technical observation about something that existed outside her. "Evaluation conditions change over time based on results. Containment conditions hold until the system decides it doesn't need them anymore. I've had the same conditions for nine months with no result documenting anything that technically justifies them." She looked back at the window. "It's something else."

She'd said it in the third person, like a technical classification applied to something outside herself. But they both knew what she meant.

"No," Oliver said.

"Did you know?"

"I knew the conditions weren't the same. I hadn't added up the exact number." A pause. "Now I do."

Sisi nodded with the precision of someone filing a confirmed variable. Not relief, not anger — just an adjustment to the model.

"What I want to know is whether this is temporary." Direct, no detour. "Whether there's some point where the protocol changes because something in the evaluation changes, or whether this is how they're going to manage me for as long as I'm here."

Oliver couldn't answer that with certainty and wasn't going to pretend he could.

"I don't know," he said.

"Can you find out?"

"Yes."

"And if the answer is that it doesn't change?"

"Then I'll tell you."

Sisi studied him a moment with that sideways attention Oliver had never fully managed to classify — not evaluative the way Asha's was, not Park's reading of terrain. Something stiller. As if she were calculating the specific weight of an answer before deciding whether to trust it.

"Do you trust them?" she said.

"I trust some of the people who work here."

"That's not what I asked."

Oliver considered the honest answer.

"No," he said.

Sisi nodded again. This time with something different — not confirmation of a variable, but something closer to recognition.

---

There was a silence that wasn't uncomfortable.

Sisi looked at the window. Outside was mountain darkness — no city to soften it, no streetlight, only the differentiated black of the sky and the darker silhouette of the ridgelines against it. Oliver wondered how long it had been since he'd seen anything through a window besides this: mountain and Center and the same fifty-meter radius repeated under different light conditions.

Seven months in the system for Sisi — four of them here. Six for him at the Center, almost seven.

"The previous system was worse in some ways," Sisi said. She said it toward the window, not toward him. The tone of someone adding context because it's relevant, not because she wants sympathy. "Three months in a facility in the south of England before Voss arranged the transfer." A pause. "The logic was the same. When you don't know what to do with something, you manage it. And managing it means the something doesn't move."

Oliver listened without interrupting. Sisi wasn't finished.

"I came here because Voss said there was real evaluation here. That there was a process with a documented end." For the first time in the conversation, her tone lost its flat calibration and turned into something harder, more worn. "Between the two systems, I've spent seven months being told there's a process with a documented end. I still don't know when it ends. Or if it ends."

That was the vulnerability. Not the information — she'd have said that to Pier or Maren the same way, with the same inventory tone. It was the count. The seven months. The point where someone who's learned not to show the cost shows it anyway because there's nowhere left to put it.

Oliver thought about the bench in the square.

The man — Martin, or whatever his real name was — had said: *she's been in that kind of system longer than anyone should have to be. I've seen earlier versions of the same thing.* He'd said it with the same flat cadence Sisi was using now. As if he knew it firsthand, or close enough that the difference didn't matter.

He had the information. He could give it to her: not all of it — not the name, not the impossible signature, not what he knew about the Registry — but enough for her to know that someone outside the Center had seen her situation exactly and named it without euphemism. He could tell her that. It would have been honest, and it would have been useful, and it would have been the opposite of what he'd spent nine days doing.

He chose not to.

Not as a strategic decision. As the moment a decision he'd already made without knowing he'd made it simply confirmed itself. Telling Sisi about the square meant explaining that it had happened, that he hadn't reported it, that he'd spent nine days knowing something about her situation he hadn't shared with her. The conversation happening now would turn into something more complicated, harder to sustain, and what Sisi had come to ask him — to find out something real and tell her — would get buried under all of that.

Or so he told himself.

"What do you need me to do?" he said instead.

Sisi took a moment before answering. The kind of pause that isn't doubt about the answer but about whether the answer is worth what it costs to give.

"Find out if there's any version of this where I get more room. Don't tell me yes or no right now." A fraction of a second. "Find out for real, and tell me what you find."

It wasn't rescue. It was honest information about what existed. The difference mattered: rescue assumed Oliver could pull her out of the system, honest information assumed the system was what it was and she needed to know it exactly in order to make her own decisions.

Oliver understood that.

"All right," he said.

Sisi got up from the desk. Nothing more happened — no hug, no verbal confirmation, none of the closure the same conversation would have had between any other two people. Just her crossing the room to the door, the click of the lock, and the specific silence of a space that has held something that isn't there anymore.

---

Oliver didn't move for a moment.

The commitment — all right, I'm not going to ignore this, find out for real — had a concrete weight his earlier versions of help hadn't had. Tomorrow he'd talk to Voss. The conversation with Voss had a predictable shape: Voss would answer exactly enough, Oliver would carry the answer back to Sisi, and the likely answer was that the protocol wasn't changing. Not while Mind's Domain had no standardized evaluation. Not while AMAT didn't know what to do with something that operated where it couldn't read it.

If that was the answer, Oliver would give it to her. That was what he'd promised.

He went to the window.

Below, the inner courtyard was empty except for the security lights along the edges. Half the windows in the residential wing across the way were dark. At the north end, one window was still lit — Park's habit of staying up later than everyone else, established over the months he'd been at the Center, as consistent as anything Oliver had learned about him.

He could knock on that door right now.

Not as a dramatic gesture. As what it actually was, operationally: relevant information he'd spent nine days not passing along. The man from the square had a signature Oliver couldn't classify. He knew specific things about the Registry nobody outside the Center should know. He'd mentioned Sisi without Oliver having said her name. Any one of those three things alone was enough for a report. All three together were exactly the kind of variable Park and Voss had spent months trying to place — something moving near the Center with too much knowledge and too much precision.

Park would listen. Oliver could give him the false name, the description, the signature in Registry terms. Park would do something with that — fast, direct, without Oliver having to keep carrying the weight of having known and not said.

He thought it through in enough detail that it stopped being hypothetical. The whole conversation, in the room at the end of the north corridor, with Park not bothering to turn on the bedside lamp because he didn't need it, and Oliver in the one chair, telling him everything from the start.

Then he thought about what would happen after.

The man from the square would become an operational thread. Operational threads got managed at the speed and in the direction Park thought correct, and Park was effective the way he was effective: no friction, no unnecessary margin. Sisi's situation — a variable in that thread, the objective the man had mentioned — would get managed within the same motion. Sisi's protocol was already the most restrictive at the Center; under an active-threat protocol, the natural direction was more restriction, not less.

He'd promised to find out if there was more room. The likely answer was already no. Under active-threat protocol, the answer would be no with even less chance of review.

That was also a justification. Oliver knew it while he built it — the second one in an hour, more solid than the first but not fundamentally different. The first had been *telling Sisi complicates the conversation.* This one was *telling Park complicates what I promised Sisi.* Both arrived at the same result: Oliver holding back information because letting it go cost him something he wasn't ready to pay yet.

The north-wing window was still lit.

He stepped back from his own window and lay down.

He didn't sleep for a long while.

Tomorrow he'd talk to Voss. That was what he could do, which was what he'd promised, which was less than he should be doing but more than he'd done so far. The rest — the man from the square, the unclassified signature, the nine days of chosen silence — could stay his a little longer.

Until it couldn't.
