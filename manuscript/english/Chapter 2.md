# Chapter 2 — The Deal


It was a quiet night, the kind that no longer sat well with Farid. He'd spent ten days staring at the same line in the black notebook — Walter Doyle, six hundred dollars, March — without anything new appearing to write beneath it. Doyle hadn't shown up at the docks since, or at Foss's tavern, or in the room he rented above the bakery on Ainsley Street. Farid had sent word around twice. No one knew anything, or no one wanted to know anything, which came to the same thing. He closed out the register slower than usual that night, without quite admitting to himself that he'd spent the last half hour doing nothing but turning that number over.

The bell kept ringing a second longer than it should have, as if the air in the shop couldn't quite let it go.

Farid didn't look up from the register right away. It was a calculated slight, the same one he used on anyone who walked in just before closing: let a moment pass, finish counting the drawer, and let whoever it was feel unwelcome enough to decide on their own whether staying was worth it. It worked on drunks, on teenagers who wandered in to look without buying, on collectors from other businesses who sometimes got the wrong door.

It didn't work that night.

"We're closed," he said, without looking up from the bills.

"I know," the man answered. "That's why I came now."

Something in the tone made him look up. It wasn't the line — any drunk trying to sound clever could manage something similar — it was the way he said it: no urgency, none of the faint pleading Farid recognized by heart in anyone who needed something from him outside business hours. The man talked as if the hours were, if anything, Farid's problem, not his.

He stood just inside the door, without having taken a single step closer to the counter. Farid tried to fix the details with the same reflex he used to appraise a watch: age, clothes, the quality of the fabric, anything that would tell him how much of a hurry the man was in. He put him at his early twenties, maybe thirty with luck, with features that never quite settled on any one place on the map — something Asian surfacing in the cheekbones and vanishing again under something paler. His English sounded local for a whole sentence and foreign in the next, and Farid couldn't have said from where. The coat was dark, good without being showy, the kind he'd have approved of on a customer himself.

The face, though, kept slipping away from him. It wasn't that it was plain or forgettable. It was, rather, that every time Farid thought he'd fixed it in place, something shifted an inch and he had to start over, like looking at a photograph through running water. He had, briefly, the absurd suspicion that it wasn't his own eyes failing him — that someone, somewhere behind that face, was deciding in real time how much of it was worth letting him see. Years later, when he tried to describe it, he'd never get past "calm" and "younger than he moved, older than he sounded." Even that, in time, he'd stop being sure was true, or something he'd invented just to have an answer ready.

"If you're here to pawn something, you'll have to come back tomorrow," Farid said, closing the drawer harder than necessary.

"I'm not here to pawn anything. I'm here to propose a business deal."

Farid had heard that line before, from people trying to sell him stolen watches dressed up as legitimate, from people trying to ask him for money disguised as a partnership. He had an answer ready for both.

"I don't do partnerships."

"I'm not asking you to." The man finally took a step forward, and the ceiling fan — which had spun on its own schedule for years, whenever it pleased — stopped dead, as if someone had unplugged it, or as if Farid, for one fraction of a second, had stopped needing to watch it turn. Neither of them mentioned it. "I'm offering to solve a problem you've been carrying for years without telling anyone — not even yourself, not entirely."

"I don't have problems a stranger can solve at nine at night."

"You have a black notebook," the man said, "that you carry home every night tucked in your coat pocket. There's a name in it that hasn't turned up anywhere it always used to, for ten days now. Six hundred dollars, since March. Walter Doyle. You already sent word around twice, and both times it came back empty. Am I wrong about any of that?"

Farid felt, for the first time in years, the kind of cold his father used to describe without quite naming it: the one that arrives when someone knows something they had no way of knowing. He hadn't told anyone. The notebook slept with him, literally, some nights. There was no employee, no customer, not one single person in Breakwater who could have pieced together those facts — the exact name, the exact figure, the exact ten days — and handed them to a stranger.

He didn't ask who the man was. He'd spent eighteen years learning that the question never got anyone anywhere useful — people lied better about who they were than about anything else.

"Whatever you're selling," he said instead, "I don't need it."

"Someone who solves, now and then, the same kind of problem you do, on a larger scale." He smiled, and the smile didn't hold still long enough for Farid to remember it clearly afterward either. "You collect small debts with patience and the occasional quiet favor. I offer a collection that never fails. Not once. Not with the most stubborn debtor, not with the one who decides to vanish one night without a word."

"That doesn't exist."

"It exists. What doesn't exist is someone willing to pay what it costs — almost no one is. You are, though you don't know it yet. And I'm not a moneylender, since you're probably wondering — I deal in something close to what you deal in, only more concentrated. You sell time. I sell certainty, the kind people need to believe in order to sleep at night. Sometimes it lines up with what's real. Most of the time, it only needs to look like it does."

Farid wanted to laugh, and tried to, and the sound came out shorter than he meant it to. He'd spent eighteen years learning to tell con men apart from people who genuinely had something to offer, and this man fit neither category he knew. He didn't ask for money up front. He didn't promise anything that smelled like a cheap miracle. If anything, he spoke with the same cold economy Farid himself used when appraising a watch — as if the deal were already done and all that remained was for Farid to learn the details.

"And what do you want in return?" he asked, because there was always an answer to that question, and the answer was the only thing that really mattered to him.

"One in ten." The man didn't move from where he stood, hands still at his sides, as if stepping closer might spoil something. "Every time you cross out a name that way, one tenth of what that account was worth becomes mine. Not yours, not Doyle's, not anyone else's. Mine."

Farid did the math in his head before he could stop himself, with the same speed he used to appraise a watch. Sixty dollars, out of a debt he'd already written off entirely, the way things stood — there was no pressure left to apply to a man who couldn't be found. Looked at that way, the numbers didn't add up in favor of saying no, and he hated, briefly, how quickly he'd reached that conclusion.

He didn't ask how the man planned to collect it. He'd already decided, without quite admitting it to himself, that he'd rather not know the answer.

"There's no envelope to send, no address to memorize," the man said anyway, as if the question had stayed written on Farid's face without his ever having spoken it. "Cross out the name, and my share leaves your register the exact same way the debt leaves your book. The same gesture handles both at once — and there's no crossing out without paying it, either. It comes bundled in. The only real decision, Mr. Yamal, is whether you cross it out or not."

He'd addressed him that way without Farid ever giving him the surname — though the shop sign, Farid reminded himself at once, already said it for him, letter by letter, for anyone who walked past on Dunmore. It was the only detail of the whole night he managed to explain without effort, and he clung to it more than he should have.

"Why not all of it?" Farid asked, because an offer with such a specific price felt, somehow, less reassuring than if it had been free. "If it really works the way you say, why set it at a tenth instead of half?"

"Because I need you to keep using it, Mr. Yamal, not scare you off the first time and leave the notebook shut in a drawer." Something in the man's voice shifted, barely, for the first time since he'd walked in — lower, less rehearsed, almost tired. "Ten percent of something that used to be zero is already the best deal you've had in years. And honestly, a lot of small accounts spread out over time serve me better than one big account you stop touching out of fear the first time. I have my own debts to see to, and calibrated this way, this is the cleanest way I've found to do it."

He should have thrown him out of the shop right then. He even thought about it: his hand already drifting slowly toward the drawer where he kept, without ever telling anyone, something more persuasive than a calculator. But the man wasn't frightening the way a thief was frightening, or a thug sent by another moneylender. He carried the same kind of calm a doctor carries when he already knows the diagnosis before asking the question — and Farid, who'd spent half his life being that same kind of person for other people, recognized the gesture before he recognized the danger.

His father had told him one more thing, only once, that Farid hadn't remembered again until this exact moment: *never owe anything to anyone whose face you can't describe afterward.* He'd said it in passing, about a traveling buyer who came and went from the harbor with no pattern, and Farid had filed it away back then as one more of his father's oddities, not a warning. Now, with the pen still capped and the man waiting with no visible impatience, he finally understood what he'd meant — and understood, too, that it was too late for that understanding to do him any good.

"Why me?" he asked instead, because that was the question he actually wanted to ask.

"Because you already understand the only thing worth understanding," the man said. "That fear which runs past the line stops being yours. That you collect exactly what's owed, not a cent more, so that next time they pay sooner. You've spent eighteen years practicing the one virtue that matters to me, Mr. Yamal. Precision. All I'm going to do is take away your margin of error."

No one, not even his father, had ever put a name to that idea out loud. Hearing a stranger say it, that night, with the fan stopped and the street empty on the other side of the glass, was the closest Farid had ever come to feeling understood.

That, more than anything else, was what finally decided it.

He pulled the notebook out from under his coat. The man didn't move to help him, didn't step closer to the counter, didn't try to see the pages. He stayed exactly where he was, as if he knew that coming any closer would break something neither of them could afford to break yet.

He opened it to Doyle's page — March, six hundred dollars, the handwriting a little rounder back then than it was now — and held the pen suspended over the paper, not asking what needed to be written, waiting for the man to tell him anyway, the way he had with everything else that night.

"Today's date, next to the name. In your own hand. That's the only thing that makes what comes after yours — not the name, which was already there before I arrived. The date. The exact moment you decided."

He wrote the date. His hand didn't shake, and years later he'd wonder why not, given everything that came out of that one line of ink.

The moment the ink dried, the fan started turning again. Forty seconds exactly, same as always. Farid noticed, and couldn't tell if that was a good sign or the worst one yet.

"That's it," the man said, and for the first time in the entire conversation he sounded, briefly, tired — the specific tiredness of someone who has repeated the same gesture many times in many different places, not the tiredness of a single night. "You won't feel anything strange. Not yet. The first time, you never feel anything. After that, you'll notice on your own. And by then you won't care."

He already had his back turned, hand on the door, when he said that last part — as if he knew exactly which question came next, and preferred not to give Farid the satisfaction of asking it.

The bell rang again when he left, this time without lingering in the air. Farid stood alone, the notebook open on the glass counter, staring at the date he'd just written beside Doyle's name, still not understanding what he had just bought, or with what.

He didn't lock up until well past midnight. He sat that extra while behind the counter, lights off, waiting — without quite knowing for what — for something to change in some visible way. Nothing changed. The street stayed empty. The fan kept turning. When he finally got up to lock the door, he told himself, with the same calm certainty he'd used his whole life to appraise broken watches, that probably nothing had happened at all — that some strange man had taken advantage of him, with information he'd somehow gathered by means more mundane than Farid cared to admit, just to get him to write a bit of nonsense in a notebook.

He fell asleep believing that. He almost managed it.

The following two days passed with exactly the normalcy Farid needed to keep believing it. He opened the shop on time, appraised the usual things, haggled over the price of a fur coat with a woman who swore it had belonged to her mother. No one mentioned the man in the dark coat. No one seemed to notice anything different about Farid, and neither did he, except for one small detail he registered only halfway: those two nights, the notebook weighed nothing at all in his pocket, as if something inside it had stopped needing to be carried with care.

Three days later, a neighbor who'd gone up to Doyle's room above the bakery — indulging a curiosity that wasn't his business — found the door unlocked and the room empty, as if the tenant had stepped out to run a quick errand and simply never remembered to come back for the rest of his things. No one in Breakwater ever saw him again, not alive, and not in any other form Farid cared to name.

That same morning, before opening the shop, Farid checked the notebook out of habit and found Doyle's entry crossed through with a single line — the same gesture he used to mark a settled account, done in his own handwriting, on a page he remembered with total clarity having left untouched the night before, except for the date.

When he reconciled the numbers that afternoon, the figure he was owed to add to the register wasn't the full six hundred dollars — it was five hundred forty, not a dollar more, as if the remaining sixty had evaporated on their own somewhere between one page and the next, the exact same way Doyle himself had evaporated from his room above the bakery. He wrote it off as a miscalculation, as tiredness, and moved on.

What he felt instead was something far more dangerous: that he'd finally been right all along. That he'd spent eighteen years collecting well, with precision, without cruelty — and that someone, somewhere, had finally handed him a tool worthy of his own talent.

It would take him years to learn that feeling — the feeling of finally being right about himself — was exactly the price the man had charged him that night, before Farid even knew he was paying.
