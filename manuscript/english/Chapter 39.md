# Chapter 39 — Free Agent - Arc 4

The sun hadn't fully risen yet when Park understood the obvious thing.

Nobody in that room could give him an order. Ferreira wasn't his commander, had never been — Park had come into Lisbon as a free agent from day one, collaboration without hierarchy, and that condition didn't stop being true just because the operation had ended badly. He didn't have to wait for a file. He didn't have to wait for authorization from anyone in Seoul, or from AMAT-EU, or from a protocol that still hadn't decided what to call what had happened that night.

"I'm going to Budapest," he said, out loud, before he'd finished thinking it through.

The silence that followed wasn't disagreement. It was the silence of people too tired to fake surprise and too awake not to feel it anyway.

"With what authorization?" Costa asked, but without the edge the question would have had eight hours earlier.

"With what I have," Park said. "None. And all of it." His narrow eyes were open wide beneath hair gone stiff with dried sweat.

Ferreira was the first to move. He closed the folder that had been open all night over Rafa's name and slid it across the table to Park.

"Then take this," he said. "Filipa's full pattern, the fourteen years, the four cities. And this." He pulled out a card with a name and number written by hand. "Salgado's contact at AMAT-HU. Owes her a favor from years back. It won't open doors for you, but it'll return your calls."

Filipa already had the rest printed out: the flight manifest, the grainy taxi camera photo, the connecting route through Frankfurt. Vitória put a charger that wasn't hers into his hand without saying anything. Rui didn't say anything either, but he was the one who handed him a bag with what was left over from the night — bandages, something for the arm Park still hadn't looked at clearly.

Park went over to Mariana before leaving. She was still wrapped in the blanket, her eyes dry now, something worse than tears settled in behind them.

"I'm going to find him," Park said.

"I know," Mariana said, not quite looking at him. "I'm not asking you to. I'm not forbidding it either. Just don't get the reason wrong."

"And why do you think I'm doing it?"

"Because you think you can fix something." She finally looked at him. "Nothing from last night gets fixed, Agent." For a second, the word sounded strange to Park.

He didn't have an answer for that. He went to get his things.

---

The Class-R was still rolled up at the bottom of the bag where he'd left it his first night in Lisbon, with the innocuous look of an ordinary medical wrap. Park put it on both arms and legs before leaving, feeling the material settle against his skin with the exact familiarity of something he'd worn for two years and still didn't quite know how to name. He filled the rest of the bag without thinking too much — the clothes that didn't smell of smoke, Vitória's charger, Ferreira's folder folded in half to make it fit.

The city, outside, didn't know yet what it had cost that night. The streets around headquarters were starting to fill with ordinary Tuesday traffic — delivery vans, someone sweeping the entrance of a pastry shop, the ordinary noise of a Lisbon about to have breakfast without ever finding out that a twenty-year-old boy was never going to climb up to the Carmo again on any first of November. Park zipped the bag shut and didn't look back on his way out.

The phone rang while he was already on his way to the airport, the taxi crossing a Lisbon coming into morning without knowing what had happened that night to a boy whose name would never appear in any newspaper.

"Cartwright."

"They tell me you're pulling out of the Lisbon file before anyone's signed off on the closure," Cartwright said, no greeting, her voice with the particular texture of someone who already knew the answer before asking the question.

"I'm going to Budapest."

"They told me that too." A brief silence, the kind Park recognized as the sound of someone typing while they talk. "Non-standard field anomaly, confirmed active, risk of regional escalation. That's what I'm going to write in the file I'm opening right now, while we speak."

"Are you authorizing me?"

"I'm giving you paper," Cartwright said. "Not the same thing, and we both know it. A file opened by me, with those words, takes six to eight hours to reach someone with the authority to close it. By then you'll already be in Budapest, you'll already have made contact, and AMAT-EU won't have any choice but to decide whether it prefers one of its agents operating without cover or one of its agents operating with it." Another pause. "Guess which one they always pick."

"Why are you doing this?"

"Because last night a twenty-year-old boy died in a room nobody's going to be able to explain in a standard report," Cartwright said, and for the first time something cracked in her professional tone. "And because if the man who did it reaches Budapest before anyone from AMAT picks up his trail, a year from now we'll be having this same conversation about a whole city, not a room."

Park said nothing for a moment.

"Six hours," he said finally.

"Eight, if you're lucky. Use them well."

The call cut off before Park could say anything else — the kind of deliberate cut Cartwright used when she'd already said everything she meant to say and didn't want to leave room for anyone, even Park, to ask her to reconsider.

---

The flight to Frankfurt had half its seats empty and no distraction capable of competing with what Park had been running on a loop in his head since sitting down.

He rebuilt it whole, again, the way he'd been doing since the curb in front of the Santa Justa elevator. The exact point where it could have gone differently. Not the strike on the drum — he'd done that well, or as well as anything can be done with two variables held together for barely long enough. He looked further back: the visit to the house in Mouraria, when he'd brought Bruno for Mariana to show. But that didn't quite fit either — Báthory had known Mariana before any of this started. He'd known Rafa long before Park had, probably his whole life. Park's visit to Mouraria hadn't taught Báthory that Rafa existed. There was no point of origin to find there, no matter how much Park wanted there to be.

He found no other moment that fit better. Every version he built came apart the moment he looked at it more closely — too late to have changed anything, too early for Park to have known. Maybe there was no exact point. Maybe that was the part hardest for him to accept: that sometimes there's no mistake to point to, only a chain of reasonable decisions that end up in the same place anyway.

Without him calling it up, the voice he'd been carrying since childhood arrived, the institution's vocabulary, climbing from the specific to the general before he could stop it: *every arc you resolve leaves something worse behind. Not from bad luck. From the simple fact of having been there.* It echoed something Ji-ho had told him without quite telling him, months earlier, at a camp in Korea, about how long the substrate takes to forget. The answer was still the same: never. Only now Park had a concrete name to put where the abstract answer used to be.

He thought of Cartwright assessing his judgment in Alfama — not his strength, his judgment, the ability to read a situation and choose well inside it. He asked himself, without arriving at an answer that helped, whether going to Mouraria had been good judgment or just the option that had seemed most human in the moment. Both things could be true at once. That was, maybe, what no one had ever fully explained to him about strength: that sometimes the same gesture that proves it is the one that opens the door for something worse to come in behind it.

He hadn't reached a conclusion by the time the plane started descending. He put the thought away where he kept everything he still didn't know how to solve, and let the weight stay there, nameless, waiting for a moment when it would hurt less to look at it straight on.

---

Budapest, in a sense, had been waiting longer than Lisbon.

It wasn't a single old wound reopening every century, like the earthquake Portugal still mourned every November. It was a different kind of accumulation: a hundred and fifty years of Ottoman occupation, the Hungarian Shoah, the siege of the city in the winter of 1944, the 1956 uprising crushed by tanks that still had a name and a date in the memory of the people who'd lived through it. A pattern closer to Korea's than to Portugal's — repeated trauma, not singular — and a branch of AMAT built accordingly: sturdier than AMAT-PT, capable of standing on its own without depending on anyone else, though smaller in scale than the full machinery of AMAT-KR.

Here the Odem isn't inherited as a diffuse longing or a sharp shape defined by collective belief. It's inherited as a physical mark — the same principle that in actual folklore gave shape to the *táltos*, the shaman born already marked, an extra finger or an odd tooth, recognized as chosen from childhood without anyone needing to teach it to him. Not an outsider to the system, a respected specialist within it. That's the cultural legitimacy a lineage like the Báthorys had used as a front for generations — they hadn't invented anything foreign to Hungary in industrializing the awakening. They'd simply taken something old and accelerated it further than anyone before them had dared.

The plane began its descent over a city split in two by a wide gray river, Buda climbing in hills toward a castle half-lit by the November sun, Pest spreading flat on the other side in a grid of wide avenues built for parades no one held anymore. Park had read, in the file Filipa had sent him during the flight, that that same river carried centuries of stories about what lived beneath it — nothing confirmed, nothing with its own file yet, only the kind of institutional silence Park had already learned to recognize as AMAT's way of saying *we know something and we're not ready to say it*.

Park landed at nine forty-seven in the morning, local time, with a dull ache in his forearm and a contact who still might not call him back.

---

AMAT-HU headquarters looked nothing like anything Park had seen in Lisbon or Seoul — a gray administrative building near the Danube, no architecture drawing attention from outside, exactly the kind of discretion a branch with seventy years of practice at not drawing attention knows how to build. Inside, the corridors had the cold, uniform lighting of any European government office, unnamed institutional portraits hung at regular intervals, and a background silence that wasn't peace but accumulated exhaustion — the kind of silence that settles into a building when it's spent decades managing a problem it knows it will never fully solve.

Salgado's contact, a middle-aged woman with the name Ilona on her desk plate, gave him fifteen minutes of her time and not one minute more. On her desk sat three different phones, two of them switched off, and a coffee cup that had been there long enough to have stopped steaming a while ago.

"Zoltán Báthory landed three hours ago," she said, no preamble, turning the screen toward him. "With burns nobody at passport control bothered to ask about. No report of where he went afterward. The family doesn't talk to us, and we stopped trying a long time ago."

"Nobody's tailing him?"

"Nobody has a mandate to tail him," Ilona said. "He's a guardian, not a fugitive, according to the file this branch keeps on him. You in Lisbon just discovered fourteen years of pattern. We've known he exists for generations without knowing what to do about it besides watch from a distance."

"Generations?" Park repeated.

"His family has been protecting something under the Danube since before this office existed," Ilona said, with no more detail than Salgado had already hinted at in Lisbon. "We have a file. They have a blood obligation. They don't compete on the same level, Agent, however much your branch in Lisbon might think they do."

"And the crisis?" Park asked. "The uncontrolled awakenings. That doesn't have a clear mandate either, I'd guess."

Something in Ilona's face closed off, the kind of gesture of someone who's answered that question too many times without ever reaching an answer she likes.

"That has mandate to spare," she said. "What it doesn't have is a single target. There's no building to storm, no one person to stop, to bring the awakening rate down. We've spent months documenting the increase without being able to say for certain how much is Báthory and how much is something that no longer depends on him." She closed the folder in front of her, ending the meeting. "Welcome to Budapest, Agent. Nothing gets solved here with a well-planned operation. It gets contained, and you wait for it to get worse more slowly than it could."

That was all he got from the official channel. The rest, Park had to piece together himself: a call to Filipa that got him the name of a hotel near Nyugati station where the pattern from Prague, Vienna, and Marseille suggested Báthory tended to recover before moving on again; a message from Vitória with the address of a pharmacy that had, that very morning, dispensed burn-treatment supplies to a man who paid in cash and gave no name; a photograph that Rui, without anyone asking, had pulled from a traffic camera and sent with no comment — the same scorched coat crossing a bridge over the Danube before dawn.

It wasn't much. It was, still, more than anyone else in that city was willing to give him.

---

The hotel near Nyugati had the kind of facade that exists only to not be remembered — blackened brick, a half-lit sign, the noise of the station's trains bleeding through the windows every few minutes. Park showed the front-desk clerk Rui's photograph and a bill worth more than the man's discretion, and got the key without having to give any explanation for who he was or why he was asking.

Room 214 smelled of cheap disinfectant and, underneath, something more specific — the same burnt smell Park had had stuck to his clothes since the night before. The bed was only half-made, a sunken rectangle near the edge where someone had sat, not lain down, for hours. In the bathroom, the trash can held gauze wrappers and an empty bottle of something that wasn't ordinary pharmacy alcohol — stronger, faster-acting, the kind you only get without a prescription if you know who to ask.

On the nightstand, half hidden under the pillow like someone who doesn't want it noticed that he put it there, Park found a used train ticket. Budapest to Miskolc, less than two hours earlier. One way, no return purchased.

This wasn't a man fleeing toward the border. This was a man heading toward something specific, in a direction no pattern from Prague, Vienna, or Marseille had predicted.

Park pocketed the ticket and was dialing Filipa's number when the phone buzzed first, a message from Vitória with a local news link and a single line above it:

*This just happened. 40 minutes ago. Fifteen kilometers from where you are.*

The headline, clumsily translated by the phone, spoke of a partial collapse at an abandoned factory north of the city — no explosives, no apparent cause, twelve injured, none of them able to clearly explain what they'd seen right before the walls started moving on their own.

Miskolc lay in that same direction.

Park pocketed the phone, left room 214 without closing the door behind him, and for the first time since landing felt that Cartwright's clock wasn't the only thing running faster than he could keep pace with.
