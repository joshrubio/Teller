# Chapter 32 — Mantle

The hospital couldn't do anything for Bruno.

Not for lack of trying. Two days of tests, a neurologist who ran the same scan three times because he didn't trust what he saw the first time, or the second, and in the end a report that said, in the most careful language possible, that they couldn't find anything to explain why Bruno still wasn't fully responding to his own name. Park read it twice in the waiting room before going up. Four pages of technical vocabulary to say, basically, that they didn't know.

The room smelled of disinfectant and something underneath the disinfectant Park didn't want to name. Blinds half down, the afternoon light coming in bands across the sheets. Bruno was awake, sitting up, the IV still in the back of his hand, but every few minutes his gaze drifted to a spot on the wall with nothing special about it — no window, no clock, no stain — and came back, as if something tugged at him without warning and he had no say in how long he let himself be pulled.

Ferreira had been standing by the window since before Park arrived. He hadn't moved from there during the two previous visits either, according to Costa. Park didn't ask why. Some things a man does on watch without anyone needing to ask him to.

"It comes back," Bruno said, his voice raw from disuse, or from using it wrong. "Every time there's more than one person breathing in the same room, it's like I hear everything at once and can't turn the volume down on any of it. Last night a nurse came in to take my blood pressure and for a moment I couldn't tell her pulse from mine."

Nobody said anything for a while. Out in the hallway, a food cart squeaked toward another room. Bruno closed his eyes, not to sleep, but with the concentration of someone trying to shut a door that has no frame.

Ferreira, by the window, said nothing through that. When Park caught up with him in the hallway a while later, the door already closed behind them, the decision was already made on his face before they'd started walking.

"A hospital doesn't fix this," he said, not stopping, voice low though nobody was near. "Neither does Salgado with instruments, though he's going to try anyway because it's the only thing he knows how to do when something scares him. I know exactly one person in this city who knows how you learn to live with this from zero, without it killing you in the attempt."

"Who is this person, exactly?" Park asked, still walking toward the elevator. "By the way you say it, that doesn't sound like an ordinary work contact."

"Mariana Vasconcelos." Ferreira said the name the way you'd set something down carefully on a table, without quite letting go of it. "The same one who was in Alfama the night of the mark. A family of guardians, generations before AMAT-PT even existed — they protect something in this city in a way even Salgado doesn't fully understand, and they've been doing it long enough that nobody in the branch dares tell them how. She doesn't work for us. She doesn't work against us either, as long as we don't give her a reason to."

"I've already met her." Park said it without weight, but something got caught behind the sentence: the exact way Ferreira had said the full name, with the same care you use handling something that can cut you if you grip it wrong. He still didn't know how much.

"Then you already know not to underestimate her." Ferreira stopped for a moment in front of the elevator doors, without pressing the button yet. "She's spent half her life teaching people in this city how to control what fell on them without their asking for it. There's no school for that, Park. She is the school."

---

Mariana met them at the door of a building with nothing of a headquarters or a shelter about it: a tenement in Mouraria, its facade peeling in layers, laundry strung balcony to balcony over the interior courtyard, someone cooking on an upper floor with the window open, garlic and something Park couldn't name drifting down the stairs along with a radio turned too low to make out the lyrics. A gray cat watched them pass from the bottom step without moving.

"I knew you'd come," Mariana said, no surprise in it, looking first at Ferreira and then at Park, a second longer on him. "What I didn't know was which one of you."

"We didn't come for work," Ferreira said. "We came because one of mine woke up wrong, and you're the only person I know who knows what to do about that before it becomes permanent."

Something shifted on Mariana's face — not surprise, closer to being found on ground she'd chosen, rather than having someone invade it. She stepped back from the door unhurried, as if she'd spent the whole morning waiting for that exact gesture.

"Come in."

The interior courtyard held more people than Park expected for a Tuesday mid-afternoon. Two older men played cards at a plastic table under a patched awning, unhurried, a deck so worn the corners had gone round. A woman sewed something on the porch — a dark fabric, maybe a mourning dress — the needle moving with a precision that had nothing to do with ordinary sewing, an almost mechanical rhythm, no hesitation, no pausing to check the next stitch. Park noticed it before he understood why he'd noticed it, and decided not to ask yet. And in a corner, under the shade of a grapevine climbing a rusted iron trellis, three young people, none older than twenty, sat in a circle around a lit candle in broad daylight, silent, each with their eyes closed, the flame standing straight as if the whole courtyard were holding its breath.

"They're practicing Void," Mariana said, noticing Park's gaze linger there longer than it should have. "The candle shouldn't move no matter what they do. It's the simplest exercise, in theory, and the one that takes the longest to learn properly."

"Void?" Park asked.

"The name we use here is different, but the name doesn't matter. What matters is what it does: shut off the signal, become unreadable. The first thing anyone needs to learn, even though it's the last thing they learn to do well."

One of the three, a girl with short hair and hands covered in calluses that didn't match her age, opened her eyes at the sound of the conversation, without the candle moving a millimeter for the gesture.

"It took me eight months to stop moving the candle," she said, unprompted, with the specific pride of someone who'd beaten something hard and wanted it on the record. "Rafa still moves it if somebody distracts him."

The boy called Rafa, without opening his eyes, raised his middle finger in her direction. The flame trembled, barely, a tilt of a couple degrees that corrected itself almost instantly, and the three of them laughed without fully breaking concentration, a contained laugh, almost airless, like someone laughing while holding something bigger inside.

"Rafa," Mariana said, no harshness in it, the specific tone of someone who's spent years correcting the same person for the same thing and no longer expects it to change, only hopes he'll try a little harder each time.

"Yeah, yeah." The boy didn't open his eyes, but the smile stayed on his face a beat too long, and something in the way Mariana looked at him afterward — longer than she'd looked at the other two, with an attention that wasn't just a teacher's — made Park file the detail away without knowing yet what to do with it.

Park stood there a moment longer, saying nothing, letting the courtyard do its work before anyone asked him for an opinion. The sun reached him in uneven bands through the vine. Someone upstairs switched off the radio mid-song. He felt something loosen in his chest that he hadn't known was tense since he'd walked through the door. It wasn't what he'd imagined — not a cult, not a temple, not danger held back by force behind a calm facade. It was people teaching each other to live with something they hadn't asked for, with the exact same patience someone uses teaching a child who's still afraid of water how to swim.

---

"Bring him," Mariana said, before anyone had asked her to.

They brought Bruno that same afternoon, in a wheelchair more as a precaution than a necessity — he could walk, but slowly, like someone who distrusted the ground under his own feet — and Mariana set him up in the courtyard, away from the card table, close to the three young people but not so close that it would break their exercise. Bruno looked around with the specific distrust of someone who'd spent two days surrounded by machines that found nothing, and was now sitting across from a woman with no coat and no instruments, in a courtyard with laundry strung over his head.

"The first thing isn't Void," Mariana said, sitting across from him, close enough that their knees nearly touched. "Void comes after, once you already know what you're shutting off. The first thing is Mantle."

"I already know the name," Salgado said. He'd come with them and had stayed quiet by the card table until then, his voice carrying its usual enthusiasm but more contained than normal, almost careful. "I've taught it for years. I've never seen it taught this way, though."

Mariana looked at him a moment, weighing something she didn't say out loud.

"You teach it out of a manual," she said, no scorn in it, almost genuine curiosity. "We teach it because if we don't teach it well, someone in the family actually gets hurt, not just loses points on an evaluation. It's the same technique. Not the same fear behind it."

Ferreira didn't argue that. He crossed his arms, leaning against the trellis, and waited.

"Teach us, then," he said. "From the fear, if that's what it takes."

Mariana took Bruno's hand between hers, not to do anything for him, just so he'd feel something solid while she talked, something that didn't ask anything of him yet.

"Everyone has Odem," she said, her voice shifting into something slower, more rehearsed, the kind of explanation given so many times she already knew exactly where to breathe inside each sentence. "But only some wake up. It's not a choice, not a merit, no ceremony or test to pass. One day you have the usual amount and the next day you have a lot more than the usual amount, and the ability to feel it, and sometimes — not always — something you can use, a gift with a shape of its own."

"We call it something else," Park said. "Domain."

"That's your name for it, in your world. Here we say waking with something, or waking with nothing." She looked at Bruno, not letting go of his hand. "You woke with something. That's not a failure, and I want you to hear that clearly, because you're going to want to believe otherwise over the next few weeks. Most people who wake up don't have anything usable. What they do have, if they learn it, is control over what they're already carrying."

Park noted the distinction without anyone underlining it for him. Two words for the same thing, two worlds that had gone generations without comparing notes. He wondered how many other things had double names that nobody at AMAT had ever bothered to ask about.

Bruno was breathing faster than normal, eyes fixed on her the way you hold onto the one solid thing in a room that won't stop moving. Behind him, without him seeing, the short-haired girl had stopped pretending to keep at her own exercise and was watching too, the candle forgotten for a moment.

"Mantle is first," Mariana went on. "Covering yourself. Not outward, inward: taking what you have overflowing and laying it over yourself like a second skin, tight, still. You won't feel it less. You'll feel it contained, which isn't the same thing."

She set Bruno's hand on his own chest, still not fully letting go of it, guiding it.

"Breathe. And when you breathe, instead of letting the air come in and out the way it always does, imagine what comes in staying stuck to the skin for a moment, on the inside, before it lets go. Don't push it. Don't rush it. Just let it stay a moment longer than usual before you let it go."

Bruno closed his eyes. It took three tries, his breath catching each time, his chest rising and falling in a rhythm that broke apart just before finding its own, until on the fourth something in his shoulders dropped all at once — not relaxation, closer to a weight finally finding somewhere to rest after two days of searching for solid ground and not finding it.

"That," Mariana said, and for the first time since they'd arrived something like warmth crossed her face. "That's Mantle. You're going to have to do it every minute of every hour for weeks before it becomes automatic, and you're going to fail more times than you'd like. But now you know what it feels like when it works. That's all you needed today."

Park, standing behind Ferreira, felt something close to hearing a word in a language he hadn't consciously practiced in years, and realizing the body still recognized it before the mind did.

Park had learned this before he'd realized what it was. Under another name, another language, another voice, another courtyard that wasn't this one, but the exact same gesture: hold before you release. It wasn't something he'd worked out on his own with the Class-R or with years of unexplained Chukgi — someone had sat down with him, the way Mariana was sitting with Bruno now, and taught him this first, before anything else. Before learning to use Momentum, before understanding how to use Mass, before understanding the true nature of what his family and AMAT called Strength, it was a memory worn thin by time, but the gesture had stayed intact.

"This..." Park said. "I know this. From a long time ago. Under a different name."

Mariana looked at him, no surprise in it, with the kind of recognition of someone finding another person who speaks the same language with a different accent.

"Doesn't surprise me. The technique doesn't belong to any one house — the house that teaches it well, teaches it to all its children first. The name changes, the voice saying it changes, but it's the same hand closing over the same thing." She looked back at Bruno, breathing slower now, shoulders still down. "He didn't have that luck. I had to give it to him today, from zero, in a hospital that didn't know what to do with what he was missing."

Then Park thought about Grayhaven, about Oliver, about Voss, about whatever it was Oliver had seen written that night in the notebook.

"And the rest?" Ferreira asked.

"Void, once he already knows how to wrap himself without thinking about it, not before — there's no use shutting off something you don't know how to contain yet. Pressure and Focus, much later, if he gets there at all. Not everyone gets there. They don't need to." Mariana stood, brushing off her knees, the brusque gesture of someone returning to the present after a while somewhere else. "The only urgent thing today was getting him to stop drowning in his own noise long enough to sleep tonight. The rest can wait until after he sleeps."

---

Park found Mariana alone a while later, at the edge of the courtyard, watching the three young people continue their candle exercise as if Bruno's interruption had never happened. The light had shifted, lower, more orange, filtering through the vine leaves in patches that moved slowly across the worn tile floor.

"Thank you," he said. "You didn't have to help us."

"I didn't do it for you." It wasn't hostile, just precise, said without looking at him, her eyes still on the candle. "I did it because someone woke up wrong because of something that started outside my city, and that part is my business. Your part, the job, the investigation, that's separate."

"How long have you been doing this?" Park asked, with a gesture that took in the whole courtyard, the card table, the sewing woman, the young people under the vine.

"My whole life, in a sense. Since before I knew it was what my family did." She sat on the edge of a large clay pot, cracked from years of sun, with the gesture of someone who's carried that weight so long she no longer notices it as weight, only as shape. "My grandmother taught me Mantle in this same house, in this same courtyard, though there was no vine yet back then. I taught it to my younger brother before he could teach it to anyone else."

Park looked toward the corner where the three young people were still at the candle, and understood without being told which of the three it was.

Mariana noticed, didn't deny it. Didn't confirm it in words either — just a brief nod, the same dry gesture she used for everything that actually mattered to her, as if saying it out loud cost something she'd rather not spend.

"He isn't part of what my family does with A Saudade," she said, after a silence Park didn't interrupt. "He's not going to inherit any of that, and I don't want him to. He woke up on his own, three years ago, nobody touched him, no ritual, no lineage behind pushing him into it. I taught him the same thing I teach anyone who comes through that door, not one minute more of attention on the technique. The only difference is I love him in a way I don't love the others, and that shouldn't change anything about how I protect him, but it changes everything."

Something in the way she said it — lower, slower than the rest of the conversation — made Park not ask further down that road.

"A Saudade," Park said instead, letting a moment pass before changing the subject. "Ferreira told me what your family does. What I don't know is why you, specifically, keep doing it."

Mariana looked at him a long moment before answering, as if deciding how much of the answer Park deserved that afternoon, the light dropping another degree through the leaves.

"Because the alternative is someone else trying it without knowing how, and seventy thousand more people ending up under the rubble, like in '55." She looked toward the candle group, her voice dropping too. "And because every person who comes through that broken door, like Bruno today, reminds me that what I protect isn't an idea or a family obligation. It's actual people who don't know they exist yet, until something wakes them up wrong."

"The man who left the mark," Park said. "Do you know who he is yet?"

Something crossed Mariana's face before she could stop it — not surprise, something older, more tired, the kind of wound that already knows exactly where to press to make it hurt again.

"I know who he is." She didn't say the name. "And I know how he thinks, better than I'd like to. He's not a stranger to me. He was..." she stopped, choosing the word more carefully than anything else she'd said in the whole conversation, "someone close. A long time ago. Before he decided there was a faster way to do what our two families do."

"But you don't agree with him," Park said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I don't support anything he does." Her voice hardened, but not all the way — something underneath stayed soft, against its will. "But I'm not going to hand him to you on a plate either, just because I no longer share his method. This isn't a file, Agent. This is someone I knew well, for a long time, and I still have the right not to turn that into a name inside your report."

Park let that go without pushing. He'd interrogated enough people to recognize a door that doesn't open by force, only breaks.

"A method for what, then?"

Mariana didn't answer that question right away. She looked up at the sky, where the afternoon was already settling over the rooftops of Mouraria, antennas and laundry lines cut black against an orange darkening at the edges, and for a moment she looked far more tired than her age should allow, something ancient showing through the young face.

"That," she said finally, "is what I still don't know, and what scares me most not knowing." She fell silent a moment, and when she spoke again her voice came out lower, almost to herself. "What I do know is that I loved him once. And part of me still hasn't found a way to stop."

---

Ferreira found Park that night, back at the office, going over Bruno's medical report under the light of a single desk lamp, with an expression Park hadn't seen on him before — something close to relief, but with a sharp edge underneath that wouldn't quite go away.

"He's going to sleep tonight," he said, without Park asking, dropping into the chair across the desk. "First time in two days. Costa's staying with him, just in case, but the nurse says his pulse isn't spiking every two minutes anymore."

Ferreira's phone buzzed on the table, a short hum against the wood. He glanced at it first, no urgency, and the relief on his face vanished completely in the second it took to read the screen.

"That wasn't the face of a man who just read good news," Park said, the lamp casting a long shadow between them.

Ferreira turned the screen toward him without a word, his hand a little stiffer than necessary to hold a phone.

It was a report from Vitória, sent from the field: a warehouse in Xabregas, broken into during the night, three security guards with Odem burns no civilian should be able to produce. A security camera had caught, for less than two seconds before failing completely, four figures leaving through the loading dock. One of them, blown up and grainy to the edge of recognition, had the slight build and the hair of a boy who, that same afternoon, hours earlier, had raised his middle finger at a candle without opening his eyes.

"Rafa," Park said, the word out before he'd finished being sure.

"We can't confirm it at that resolution." Ferreira didn't sound convinced of his own sentence. "But it's his age, his build, and he was half an hour's drive from where this just happened."

Ferreira pocketed the phone, his jaw tight in a way Park hadn't seen from him before.

"If this goes up through the normal channel, nobody in Lisbon is going to ask whether he was forced or acted on his own. They're going to see the name Vasconcelos next to a warehouse with three wounded, and they're going to assume. That's how a file works."

"And isn't that what it is?" Park asked. "A file?"

Ferreira didn't answer right away, which was, in itself, an answer.

"We still don't know what it is," he said finally. "And until we do, I'd rather not be the one deciding for Mariana what her brother's name is going to mean in a report."

Park said nothing more. He thought about the candle not moving, about the way Mariana had looked at Rafa a beat longer than necessary, and about how little he still knew, whether that boy had chosen something or something had chosen him first. There was no way to settle that question tonight, and something on Ferreira's face said he wasn't in a hurry to try either.

Park thought about the courtyard, about the candle, about the three young people under the vine, about the way Mariana had looked at Rafa a beat longer than necessary. And he thought about what she herself had said an hour earlier, without his quite having asked: *I loved him once, and part of me still hasn't found a way to stop.*

He wondered how many of the people Mariana loved as family had already chosen, quietly, the other house.
