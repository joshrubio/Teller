# Chapter 91 — Depth

---

The days after Val Bedretto blurred into each other in a way Oliver only noticed in retrospect, when he tried to remember which exercise had come before which and discovered that the Registry's perfect memory wasn't much use against the simple fact that they had all started to look alike.

Deadlift again, the weekly retest Reber had promised since the first day — the whole group improved its mark, without exception, though none of them were close yet to what the military candidates did without visible effort. After that came a full day dedicated to firearms, timed disassembly and reassembly, basic aiming, and there the difference with the rest of the center turned almost comic: Dara stripped a training rifle in an acceptable time and took twice as long to put it back together, Riku mixed up the order of the pieces twice, and an instructor none of them knew by name had to repeat the same instruction three times before Petra, the only one who seemed to be taking it with genuine calm, managed a time that didn't embarrass the group.

"This shouldn't scare me as much as it does," Riku said, holding the stock against his shoulder with a discomfort he didn't hide. "I interfere with half the city's electronics when it gets away from me. This is just metal."

"That's exactly why," Pier said, without looking up from his own rifle, already half stripped with an ease that surprised more than one person. "A Domain feels like an extension of you. This is a tool that doesn't know you and doesn't owe you anything. It's harder precisely because there's nothing of yours you can borrow from it."

"And how do you know how to strip this so fast?" Riku said, looking at him with half-joking suspicion. "I thought you were a physicist, not a soldier."

"Fishing," Pier said, without further explanation for a second, as if the word already said it all. "I've been fishing since before I knew I caused gravitational anomalies without meaning to. Cleaning and assembling a reel isn't so different from this. Hands learn the same kind of patience no matter the object."

The military candidates, meanwhile, ran through the same exercise with the boredom of people who had repeated it thousands of times.

In between there were endurance sets, field first aid that left everyone with fake blood on their hands, ropes and knots that came as naturally to Pier as breathing — "fishing teaches this before any manual does" — and communications under interference where Asha, again, found the rhythm before the rest. None of those days stayed in Oliver's memory with more detail than a general sense of accumulated tiredness, the kind of week that gets remembered as a single block.

What did stay was the last day.

---

The pool wasn't marked on any map the group had seen until then — a closed facility inside the complex itself, chlorinated water lying still under a light too white, with Reber waiting at the edge alongside two instructors Oliver didn't recognize.

"This is based on a real exercise, used for decades to train combat swimmers," Reber said, without preamble. "Hands tied behind your back, feet tied together. You have to enter the water, retrieve a marked object from the bottom, and get out, without using your hands for anything. Two-minute limit before someone goes in to pull you out. This isn't a test of strength. It's a test of whether your head keeps working when your body panics and has no tool to solve it with."

Nobody asked what happened if they failed. They had already guessed the answer.

Oliver was third in, after watching Dara complete it with a tight but real time, and Riku come out without the object, coughing water, with the face of someone who had just discovered a fear he didn't know he had. With his hands tied behind his back, the Registry offered him nothing — there was no Domain to read underwater, no structure to study, only his whole body screaming that something was wrong while his mind tried, with an increasingly forced calm, to remember that two minutes was a long time if he stopped counting it in seconds.

He found the object on the bottom, gripped it between his teeth because he had no other way to hold it, and kicked upward with his bound feet moving like a single clumsy fin, his chest already burning before he broke the surface.

Petra did it better than anyone in the group. She came out of the water with the object pinned to her chest by her chin, her breathing controlled in a way that didn't seem entirely human, and when someone asked her how she'd done it, she simply said she'd spent enough years in rooms where visible panic cost more than anything else, and that water, underneath it all, wasn't so different.

Nadia went after, her ankle finally free of the sling but still protected, and although the instructor offered to modify the exercise for her, she refused with the same quiet firmness she'd carried since Val Bedretto. She managed it, slower than Petra but within the time, and when she came out of the water, coughing but standing on her own feet, nobody in the group said anything — it wasn't necessary; Nadia's face already said everything there was to say about what that meant to her.

A candidate from Dax's group did it in half the time of anyone from Valais, his pulse not seeming to change at any point in the process, and Oliver, watching from the edge of the pool with his clothes still dripping, thought that was probably the exact point of the exercise Reber wanted them to understand without saying it out loud: the difference wasn't in the water. It was in how many times someone had already been there before.

"At this rate we should start charging admission," Riku said, still coughing water, nodding at the candidate from Dax's group who had just come out without his pulse having changed at all. "It's the closest I'm ever going to get to a free circus show."

He laughed at his own joke before anyone else did, the kind of laugh that comes out when the body is still shaking with cold and shame at once. Nobody in the Valais group fully joined in — Nadia offered half a smile out of courtesy, nothing more. A candidate from Dax's group, younger than most, leaning against the wall with his arms crossed, said nothing. He kept looking at Riku a moment too long, not smiling, not visibly offended either — the kind of look that files something away for later instead of spending it right there.

---

The screens in the hangar, that afternoon, told a more complicated story than the previous exercise had.

Riku dropped from #163 to #171 — eight places, the group's only real fall that week, the panic underwater costing him more than the previous weeks' discipline had built. Dara barely moved, from #157 to #156, a fluctuation so small it said nothing in either direction. Pier climbed from #51 to #47, Asha from #34 to #31, both steady as always. Nadia climbed from #192 to #183 — nine places, less than the Val Bedretto jump, but a climb, and this time without any rescue involved, only for completing the test without modification. Petra climbed more than anyone in the group that week, from #95 to #82 — thirteen places, the water test weighing more than anyone had anticipated in the total calculation. Oliver barely moved, from #93 to #91, two places that changed nothing real.

Nobody commented on the numbers out loud this time. They'd had enough weeks of this by now to know that commenting on them didn't change what they said.

---

That night, the Valais table in the mess hall had only four seats occupied — Pier and Asha had been in an extended communications test since mid-afternoon that the rest of the group still hadn't quite understood, something about redundant protocols under interference that Reber had insisted on stretching well into the night. That left Oliver, Dara, Nadia, and Riku, the tiredness of the entire week weighing more than any single day on its own.

Riku, still in the same nervous mood from the pool, picked the joke about the candidate from Dax's group back up, this time with more detail and less humor, loud enough to carry further than he'd calculated.

Oliver noticed before Riku finished the sentence: the same young candidate from the pool, standing by the entrance to the mess hall, tray still in his hands, turning his head toward their table with the same filing-away look he'd used hours earlier.

"That's the same one from before," Dara said, quietly, barely moving her lips. "Number seven. I saw him on the board last week, dropping a couple of places. Odd, for someone that young."

The candidate set his tray down on the nearest table without sitting and walked straight toward them, unhurried, every step calculated to arrive exactly as he finished speaking.

"Joke again," he said, his voice carrying a heavy accent, sentences built with the slightly displaced order of someone thinking in another language before translating. "First time, in water, I let pass. Second time, in front of everyone, not joke anymore. Something else."

"It was just a comment," Riku said, the smile already completely gone.

"In my country," the candidate said, without raising his voice, each word placed with the same cold precision as someone building something, not arguing, "comment has price too. Here too. You choose pay now, among friends, or pay later, when you don't choose how anymore."

"What's your name?" Oliver said, more to buy time than out of real curiosity.

"Yegor," the candidate said, no surname, the same economy Anka or Reber used with their own names, though on him it sounded less like authority and more like a warning. "Come. Both. Where things really get tested."

Riku looked at Oliver, and Oliver, without saying anything out loud, stood up with him.

"We're going to find Pier and Asha," Dara said, already standing too, Nadia a step behind her. "I don't like this."

"Not necessary," Riku said, without real conviction.

"I wasn't asking you," Dara said, and left the mess hall with Nadia without waiting for a reply, leaving Oliver and Riku following Yegor toward a part of the complex neither of them knew yet.

---

The corner of the complex Yegor led them to turned out to be a converted storeroom, old mats stacked against the walls instead of laid out on the floor of the official pit, a single bare bulb hanging from the ceiling leaving half the room in shadow. No instructor, no tablet, nothing recording what happened in there. Far more military candidates than Oliver had expected — a dozen and a half, maybe more, scattered between the floor and stacked crates at the back, settled in with the familiarity of people who'd been doing this long before Valais arrived at the center.

Nobody welcomed them. Nobody threw them out either. That ambiguity, more than anything else, told Oliver that this place ran on its own rules, unwritten.

He recognized a couple of faces among those present, though he'd never exchanged a word with either — the board had stuck in his memory more than he liked to admit. Number ten, a stocky man who took up twice the space he needed without meaning to, sitting on a crate with his arms crossed. Number eleven, thinner, leaning against the wall near the door with the kind of stillness only people who already know they won't need to move much to do what's necessary have.

"What is this?" Riku said, quietly, to Oliver.

"Nothing that's going to show up in any report," someone said, having heard them, without needing to be asked directly.

Iker was among those present, leaning against the back wall, but something in his posture set him apart from the rest without needing to be said — the others had already settled in with the anticipation of people expecting a show, a couple of them even smiling in advance, and Iker, instead, had his arms crossed and the face of someone who'd rather be somewhere else. When Yegor explained, in two sentences, what had happened, Iker didn't laugh at the joke or seem offended by it. He simply stood up, without the rest's enthusiasm.

"Against me," he said. "Again, if needed. No judges, no board, nothing to write down. Just so it's clear that jokes get paid for with the body here, not with a note in anyone's file."

Riku looked at Oliver for a second, searching for something — permission, maybe, or simply the confirmation that he wasn't as alone as he felt — and Oliver gave him neither, because he had no right to decide this for him.

"I'm going to lose," Riku said, already taking off his jacket.

"Probably," Oliver said. "That doesn't mean you shouldn't try with everything you've got."

Riku opened with the only thing he knew how to do well: a short, direct jab, the kind of punch he'd practiced hundreds of times against a bag that never hit back. Iker let it land. He didn't dodge it — he received it with an open palm, closing it around Riku's fist halfway there, and right there, in the exact instant of contact, Oliver saw the tell: a minimal flicker of contour in Iker's hand, a contrast that switched on and off faster than a normal eye could follow.

Riku felt the punch turn strange before he understood it. A tingling climbing up his knuckles, not pain yet, more like touching a doorknob after dragging your feet across a carpet — but stronger. His whole forearm went numb for a second, his fingers not fully responding.

"What was that?" he said, stepping back half a pace, shaking his hand as if he could give it back control.

Iker didn't answer with words. He answered with his feet.

He moved — not fast in the normal sense, but *different*, a small lateral hop that shouldn't have given him that much distance in that little time, and Oliver, with the Registry still open, saw the same contour flicker this time under the soles of Iker's feet, lighting up at each footfall, a minimal pulse of Static pushing against the ground at the exact instant of contact — not to strike, to *propel*. Every step carried something extra behind it, a fraction of a second's advantage Riku couldn't calculate because he'd never trained against a body that cheated the physics of the ground itself.

Riku turned to follow him and lost the angle. He threw a hook at where Iker should have been; Iker was no longer there. The second time Riku tried to close the distance, Iker had already circled his flank, and the elbow that drove into his forearm arrived with the same pulse as before, denser — a localized lash, the muscle contracting on its own, without permission, leaving the whole arm momentarily slower than it should be.

Oliver understood what he was seeing a second before Riku understood it from the inside: it wasn't a trick, it was a whole axis. Iker knew how to feed Static into the point of contact of a punch to stun, and knew how to feed it into the contact between his own foot and the ground to move faster than his body alone would allow — the same principle of Focus Anka had taught them weeks earlier, applied to two different problems by someone nobody had ever told what the "correct" way to use it was.

"He's using his feet to position himself, not just his fists," Oliver said out loud, unable to help it.

Riku no longer had time to process it. Tired, slower, he threw one last desperate punch that Iker dodged with another of those impossible steps, and answered by closing the whole distance at once — an open palm that struck him full in the chest, and this time the pulse carried inside wasn't a tingle or a localized lash: it was a full discharge, spread across his whole torso in the instant of contact, every muscle in his chest and abdomen contracting at once with nothing Riku could do to stop it. His legs gave out before the pain finished registering. He fell backward onto the mat, unconscious for a second, maybe two, before the ceiling came back into focus above him.

He lay there, his breath cut short, a strange, localized pain throbbing in three different points of his body at once, each one an echo of where Iker had touched him.

"That," he said, when he finally managed to speak, his voice broken, pointing at his own chest without quite touching it. "That wasn't just the punch."

"No," Iker said, without real arrogance, already stepping back, the matter settled for him. "Static, fed into the contact. In the fist, in the foot, wherever it's needed. I don't know how to fight with my head. I know how to fight with what I've got."

"He still hasn't paid for the joke in full," Yegor said, without moving from where he stood. "Again. For real this time."

Iker didn't move right away. Something in his face said he'd already called the fight over, that this shouldn't be happening anymore — but the room's silence, waiting, a dozen pairs of eyes fixed on him and not on Yegor, weighed more than he wanted to admit. He turned back toward Riku, still on the ground, and this time the punch carried no Static behind it — just the fist, dry, harder than necessary for someone who was no longer defending himself.

Oliver stepped forward before he'd fully thought it through.

"He's already lost," he said. "It's over."

Nobody laid a hand on him, but two bodies moved to block his path without needing to touch him, enough to make clear he wasn't going to get any further. He searched for Riku with his eyes, ready to try anyway.

Riku found him first. From the ground, his face already marked, he shook his head — barely a movement, but enough. *No.* Not out of pride, Oliver understood, but because Riku knew, better than he did, that forcing it would only multiply the problem into two people instead of one.

Iker stopped himself a second later, his hand still closed, looking at it as if he didn't quite recognize it. He took a step back, for real this time, without saying anything.

Yegor walked over and gave Riku one last kick to the side, short, almost procedural, more to sign off on the end than to inflict new damage.

"That's it," he said. "Now it's done."

Someone by the door raised two fingers in a quick, silent gesture.

"Someone's coming," he said, barely a whisper, and the whole room moved at once, with the kind of order that only comes from having practiced this before. Mats returned to their place, the bulb switched off on the way out. In under twenty seconds, the room was empty except for Oliver and Riku, still on the ground.

The door opened fully a moment later. Maren came in first, and behind her Pier, Asha, Dara, and Nadia, out of breath as if they'd run most of the way. Nobody said anything at first — only Maren's gaze sweeping the empty room, then Riku, then Oliver, and the looks the rest of the group exchanged among themselves, the same uncomfortable certainty shared among all of them without needing to be put into words.

Riku didn't say who had been there. Not that night, and Oliver, following him back to the rooms much later, didn't ask him either.
