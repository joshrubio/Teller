# Chapter 100 — Hot Trail

---

The name on the third-floor mailbox was Marcel Aubry, and over the three days following the discovery of the photograph on the unmade bed, Yusuf became, with the same methodical discipline he applied to everything, the person who knew more about Marcel Aubry than anyone else on the planet, including — he suspected — the man's own family.

Forty-one years old. No registered partner, no children. Employed at the Lausanne station for six years, no disciplinary incidents, no promotions either — the kind of track record that drew nobody's attention, for better or worse. The two bank letters Yusuf had seen on the doormat turned out to be just the tip of something longer: Aubry had gone eight months without paying rent on the apartment, covering it with savings that, according to the statement Yusuf obtained without needing a formal order, had run out exactly five weeks earlier.

Five weeks. Long enough for someone with no resources to grow desperate. Long enough, too, for someone with Kaspar's patience to find exactly who to make an offer to.

---

Aubry's bank account showed no irregular income — nothing as simple as a suspicious transfer the day before he vanished, nothing a less methodical investigator would have expected to find and given up on not finding. Yusuf hadn't expected it to be that easy. Kaspar didn't leave that kind of trail.

What he did find, after two days combing through six months of transactions with the kind of attention only real desperation justifies, was a pattern far more boring and far more telling: small cash withdrawals, each one bigger than the last, from different ATMs, none near his home or his workplace. Someone preparing to disappear without leaving a single bank transaction that could be traced afterward.

And one purchase, the only one outside the pattern: a plane ticket, paid in cash at a physical travel agency — one of the few still left, the kind of business that survived precisely because it left no digital trail like an online purchase — bound for Bogotá, departure date six days before Kaspar spoke to Aubry's contact at the luggage lockers.

Yusuf stood staring at the receipt, a copy on thermal paper already nearly illegible that the agency had taken half an hour to dig up from a filing cabinet nobody had opened in months, and felt the same uncomfortable sensation that had been keeping him company for a whole week: the feeling of reading exactly what someone wanted him to read, in the exact order they wanted him to read it.

"Are you sure you want this copy?" the agency clerk had asked, no real curiosity, just courtesy. "It's over a month old."

"Absolutely sure," Yusuf had said.

---

He called a contact who still owed him a favor from years back, someone in customs with access to entry records no civilian should be able to consult, and confirmed what the receipt had already hinted at: Marcel Aubry had entered Colombia six days before the date at the luggage lockers, and hadn't left the country again since, not under his own name and not with his own passport.

The same contact, without Yusuf fully asking for it, added one more detail: the destination address Aubry had declared on the entry form matched a rural property registered, according to Colombian land records, to a foreign nonprofit entity — a fund headquartered in Luxembourg Yusuf had never heard of, the **Meridian Fund**, dedicated, according to its own public documentation, to "social reinsertion and rural development programs." The kind of name boring enough and generic enough to draw no attention from anyone who wasn't already looking for something.

Yusuf wrote it down without knowing yet what to do with it. It wasn't the first time a Kaspar trail had touched money with an apparently legitimate origin — but it was the first time he'd found a real name behind the money, instead of a simple chain of faceless transfers.

That didn't prove Kaspar was there. It proved someone had prepared the path months in advance — the gradual withdrawals, the ticket paid in cash, the disappearance timed with surgical precision relative to the meeting at the lockers — and that whoever it was had the resources and the patience to plan something far bigger than a single clue left to chance.

Yusuf began to understand, with the uncomfortable clarity of someone who's spent days gathering pieces without seeing the full picture, that he wasn't looking at a fugitive hiding. He was looking at someone building something, with real people, in real places, over months — and that Aubry, with his eight months of unpaid rent and his dwindling savings, had been exactly the kind of person who'd accept any offer someone like Kaspar could make without asking too many questions about why.

He didn't know yet what that offer was. But he was starting to suspect that "recruit" was a more precise word than "witness" for what Aubry had become.

He spent the next afternoon checking something that had occurred to him later than he'd have liked: if Aubry wasn't an isolated case, there had to be others. He reviewed unexplained work absences within a two-hundred-kilometer radius of Lausanne over the past year, filtering by profile — accumulated debt, no close family, the kind of life a disappearance wouldn't leave anyone behind to ask too loudly about. He found three. An accountant from Geneva, two months before Aubry. A lab technician from Fribourg, four months before that. A security guard at a warehouse near the French border, nearly a year back.

None of the three had turned up again in any record Yusuf could check without a formal order he didn't have. None had, on the surface, anything in common with the others besides the same kind of quiet desperation Aubry had carried through his last eight months of unpaid rent.

Four people, in under a year, gone the same boring, methodical way. This wasn't the pattern of one man on the run alone. It was the pattern of someone recruiting patiently, one every few months, choosing carefully who to offer whatever it was he was offering — and Yusuf, for the first time since leaving the Valais Center, felt that the real size of what he was chasing no longer fit inside the word "fugitive" he'd been using since the start.

---

The message arrived that same afternoon, slid under the door of the hotel room Yusuf had been using for the past two days — no sender, no signature, a single line handwritten on paper with nothing special about it except the fact that it had gotten there without anyone seeing who brought it.

*Well done. Most give up before the lockers.*

Yusuf stood by the door a long while, the paper between his fingers, running the angles of a problem that had no angles to run — nobody knew he was in that hotel besides the front desk, and the front desk hadn't seen anyone come in who wasn't a guest. Whoever it was had been there, in that hallway, and left without any of the hotel's security systems logging anything out of the ordinary.

It wasn't the first time he'd felt this — the certainty of being watched without being able to confirm by whom or from where — but it was the first time someone had confirmed it to him in writing, with the same cold economy the rest of the trail had been demonstrating for a solid week.

*Most give up before the lockers.* It didn't say who "most" was. It didn't need to. Yusuf understood, with the same uncomfortable clarity as before, that he wasn't the first person Kaspar had let find this path. He was, simply, the first to get this far without giving up.

---

He sat on the edge of the hotel bed with the phone in his hand for nearly ten minutes that night, Park's number already dialed, his thumb over the call button.

He had real reasons to make the call. A trail crossing an ocean, a recruitment pattern that suggested something far bigger than the hunt for one man, and now a handwritten note confirming Kaspar knew exactly where he slept every night. Any reasonable AMAT protocol would have said this was, days ago already, the moment to call for backup.

He didn't call.

He told himself, putting the phone away, that it was because he still had nothing solid to offer — a suspicion, a pattern, no proof that would hold up in a formal report — and that calling for backup now would only let someone else make decisions about a trail he'd spent a solid week reading up close, without that other person having the full context. It was a reasonable reason. He'd given it to himself before, on other jobs, in other countries, and it had almost always been true.

This time, somewhere beneath the reasonable reason, he knew there was something simpler and less defensible: he'd spent thirty years being the one who found things alone, and he still didn't know how to stop being that, not even when caution was asking him to with a clarity that left little room for other interpretations.

He thought, briefly, about Park — about the years they'd spent crossing paths on different operations without ever quite overlapping, about the kind of professional trust that didn't need much conversation to work. Park would have listened. Park, probably, would have understood better than anyone the temptation to keep following, alone, a trail that was finally, after days of dead ends, starting to really move.

That was, maybe, the most dangerous part of all this: the decision not to call didn't feel like a mistake. It felt like the same professional instinct that hadn't failed him in thirty years.

He put the phone away in the inside pocket of his jacket, next to the photograph of Kaspar's real face he'd been carrying for days without looking at it again, and turned off the light without having fully decided whether what he'd just done was prudence or the first real mistake of a career that, until now, hadn't made a single one too big to fix afterward.

---

He bought the ticket to Bogotá that same night, paying with his own card this time — there was no point hiding from a man who already knew what hotel he slept in — and spent the rest of the night awake, going over what little he had on Colombia in general and on the address customs had confirmed as Marcel Aubry's last known point of entry: a rural area in the north of the country, far from any major city, the kind of place a man could disappear completely if someone with the right resources made it easy for him.

He didn't know what he'd find there. He didn't know, either, whether the note under the door meant a warning, an invitation, or simply the kind of gesture someone makes when they want their target to know that the game, if it was a game, still had rules only one side fully knew.

The only thing he knew for certain, looking out the taxi window toward the airport before dawn, was that he'd gone a whole week without feeling the crash his body had owed him since day four — the adrenaline of finally being close to something real, holding him up in a way discipline alone had never managed to sustain for this long.

That, more than anything else, should have worried him. It didn't worry him enough.

The airport, at that hour, had the kind of gray stillness that only exists between four and six in the morning, a handful of cleaning staff moving between rows of empty seats, the departure boards still showing more flights canceled for fog than confirmed. Yusuf checked the one small bag he carried, the same one he'd used on every job for the last twenty years, and sat down to wait for boarding with the same untouched coffee that had become, without his meaning it to, the habit of this whole trip.

He thought, while he waited, about the word Kaspar had used in the note — *well done* — and about how long it had been since anyone had said it to him that way, no conditions, no performance review behind it. It was an uncomfortable thought, and he set it aside before it could linger too long. He had no way of knowing whether that, too, was part of what Kaspar was building with such patience — the carefully calculated feeling that someone was finally paying real attention.

He boarded when they called his row, unhurried, nothing on his face to suggest that the next few hours were taking him further from any kind of support than he'd been at any point in his whole career with AMAT.
