# Chapter 70 — Discharge

---

The formation took four seconds to complete.

Oliver counted them because it was the only thing he could do while the creature finished emerging from the ground — four seconds in which the team didn't move, didn't fire, did nothing but watch something that until that moment had existed in fragments and underground points turn into something with a shape.

It wasn't a body. It was a mass. Thousands of black shapes stacked on top of each other, each so small that alone it wouldn't have mattered, together making up something the size of a full-grown tree — but without a tree's proportions, without anything fixed. The surface reorganized constantly. One flank advanced while the other retreated; the center gained height and then gave it up; the whole profile was different every time Oliver tried to pin it down. Not because the creature was moving yet. Because it had never stopped moving.

In the Registry: hundreds of signatures that now had a pattern. Not random. Oriented.

"Nobody advances," Pier had said. "Let it finish forming."

Oliver understood the calculation as soon as he heard it: if they interrupted before it closed, the creature could redistribute underground again. So they waited. Four seconds. And when the creature finished forming — when the last stream of worms came out of the ground and merged into the mass — Pier spoke again.

"Dara, front. Thirty meters. North-left flank," Pier said over the intercom.

Asha, at the same time:

"Nadia, Sabine, don't move from the perimeter line. If it splits, call it."

The creature moved for the first time.

---

It wasn't speed in the sense Oliver would have expected — not a sprint, not a charge. It was instant redistribution: the mass on the left advanced three meters in half a second, the right contracted, the center rose and then flattened. The net result was displacement, but the mechanism was fluid, almost with no physics to justify it. As if the terrain between its position and the target simply stopped mattering.

A thirty-year-old pine gave way four meters from where the creature had stood two seconds earlier. It wasn't the trunk — it was the root, torn from below, the ground lifting in a three-meter radius before settling again. The tree leaned slowly, then not so slowly.

Oliver reported over the intercom without deciding to: "Main mass heading north. East flank clear for now."

"Received," came Pier's voice. "Oliver, full signature."

He was right. No one else could see the flanks that didn't have a light pointed at them. Oliver widened the radius — it cost more than at the start, ten minutes in from the beginning of the march, but it held — and gave what he could:

"East flank: four columns. Slow, probably exploratory. South flank: inactive for now. The bulk stays north."

---

Dara's Double appeared thirty meters ahead, among the pines.

Dara had shaped it with the exact posture she used in training — not a passive copy, but something active, something that moved, that turned its head toward the creature. The signature was convincing from a distance. Oliver read it from where he stood, and for a moment his own Registry wavered — the same Odem density, the same distribution.

The creature stopped.

Three seconds of complete stillness.

Then it split. One flank shot toward the Double at a speed Oliver didn't process until it had already happened — fifty worms, a hundred, covering the twenty meters in under two seconds. The Double took the impact. It didn't give right away — Dara held it, Oliver could see it in the signature, the effort of active maintenance under real physical pressure. The Double resisted one second, two.

Then the creature let go.

It withdrew from the Double as if bored. The flank fell back, reintegrated into the main mass. And the creature resumed its advance north, without looking at the Double again.

"It doesn't use the same thing twice," he said over the intercom. "It's identified the difference in signature."

Brief silence.

"Understood," came Pier's voice. Dry. And then, with no pause: "Asha, structure the north flank. Now."

Oliver saw it immediately: Asha tensed the root network of the northern sector at the surface, the vegetal Odem responding like a cable snapping taut. The northern vanguard met resistance — not a wall, but ground that didn't give the way it should, that pushed back. The creature took six more seconds to cover that stretch. It used them to redistribute toward the flank that didn't have that friction.

---

Oliver saw the moment before it happened.

The creature sent a current north, the three pines closest to Pier's flank were in line, and the root of the first already showed tension in the Registry — vegetal Odem under stress, something pushing from below. Pier was still four meters from the tree.

He concentrated Asha's Tension on the root network — not all of it, just the surface system of the closest pine, squeezing the anchor points against the ground like tightening a cable. The root held. The tree groaned but didn't give. The wave of worms pushing from below found resistance where it didn't expect it and swerved sideways.

Two seconds. Pier used them to move three meters east without Oliver having to tell him why.

The pine gave way anyway thirty seconds later. But Pier was no longer there.

Pier had created the weight zone while the tree was still groaning. Oliver saw the field — dense, concentrated, a four-meter radius where local gravity had tripled the cost of moving. The section of worms crossing that sector compressed against the ground, advanced, but slowly, the fluid movement from before turned into something that cost. For four seconds that section fell out of the offensive pattern. The creature redistributed around the field without trying to break it. The weight wasn't enough to pin it — only enough to charge it something.

The cost arrived: not pain, but noise. The Registry filled with interference in the radius where he'd applied the Tension, as if the channel had been clean and now took time to recover resolution. Oliver narrowed the radius. Waited two seconds. Came back.

Eleven minutes.

---

The creature was intelligent in the way things are intelligent when they've never needed not to be.

It didn't attack blindly. Every time the team responded — every time Asha tensed something structural, Pier created a weight zone that slowed a flank, Nadia pushed a current back from the perimeter — the creature adjusted. It didn't retreat. It went around. It looked for the angle nobody was watching.

Oliver was the only one watching all of it at once.

"East current turning north. Going to flank Asha from behind, thirty meters."

"Moving," came Asha's voice.

"Central mass slowing down. Testing something."

"What?" Pier's voice.

"I don't know yet."

He understood two seconds later, when the south current — which he'd logged as inactive — reactivated all at once. Not toward the team. Toward Riku's perimeter line.

Oliver used Anchor before he'd decided to: he took the densest point of the south current, the most consolidated section where a hundred worms moved together like a single block, and fixed them. Not the whole current — he didn't have the capacity for that — just that block. It went still. The rest of the current flowed around it, but the central block that set the pace disappeared from the vanguard. The current reached Riku's perimeter ten seconds later than it would have without the intervention.

Ten seconds Nadia used.

"South current at the perimeter," came Nadia's voice. And then the Impulse pulse — Oliver saw it as a disturbance in the peripheral EM field, the current pushed back three meters. Not destroyed. Delayed.

"Good," Pier said over the intercom.

There was something in that "good" Oliver logged without analyzing it: not praise, but confirmation that the system worked. That the team was a system.

The cost of Anchor arrived later than Tension's and was more diffuse — not interference but fatigue in the active radius. Oliver logged it as data.

Twelve minutes.

---

He'd hit twelve minutes and noticed the difference.

Not in the radius — the radius stayed where he had it. Not in his capacity to read signatures — he could still tell every team member apart, still see the creature's distribution. What had changed was the cost per unit of time. What used to take active effort now took sustained effort. The difference between pushing something and holding up something that wants to fall.

He kept holding.

But the threshold was closer than it had ever been.

The creature stopped again.

In the Registry: every current, every flank, the whole distribution reorganizing simultaneously. Not the erratic movement from before. Something closer to a decision.

Oliver traced the pattern. The movement vectors of every section converged on one direction. Not toward Pier. Not toward the central team. Toward the southern perimeter.

Toward Riku.

"It's going for Riku," he said over the intercom, and his own voice came out flatter than he meant it to. "The whole pattern's converging. It's identified the EM perimeter as the primary threat."

One second of silence.

Then Asha's voice: "Sabine, Dara, to the south perimeter. Now."

And Pier's: "Park."

---

The creature moved in a way it hadn't moved before.

No exploratory pattern, no secondary flanks, none of the fluid redistribution of the last few maneuvers. Direct. The whole weight of the main mass aligned in a single line, and the speed Oliver had seen in flashes during the fight now sustained — every fraction of a second the creature's signature advanced in the Registry, and every fraction of a second Riku was the same fixed point on the southern perimeter.

Three pines gave way in the first two seconds of the charge. Not from direct impact — the creature passed underneath, the root system torn from below by the subterranean mass traveling with the main one. The ground split in parallel strips along the line of advance. The trees leaned into the furrow and fell one after another, the crown of the first still swaying when the third was already on the ground. The creature didn't go around them. It went through what was left.

Sabine activated Anchor before the creature reached a hundred meters. Oliver saw the field extend — Sabine could fix solid objects in space, and the creature was, in some sense, an object, but it was also thousands of objects, and the ones that got fixed simply stopped being part of the vanguard while the rest kept going. Like trying to anchor a wave by fixing water molecules one at a time.

The vanguard didn't stop.

Oliver used Impulse before it made sense to: a forced line against the thirty worms furthest ahead, direct, no margin. The thirty flew sideways. The gap lasted less than a second. The rest of the vanguard flowed around it as if the obstacle had never existed.

Dara's Double appeared between the creature and Riku. Dara had placed it directly in the trajectory — not as a decoy this time but as a physical barrier, her own Odem signature interposed. The creature didn't hesitate. It hit the Double, covered it, and the Double held two seconds, three, Dara's signature under maximum pressure. Oliver watched her fight to hold it. Four seconds.

The Double dissolved.

The creature kept going.

Park's signature appeared in the Registry, moving in from the northeast — he'd calculated the angle, anticipated the line of advance. But the terrain between him and Riku was the worst in the sector: the fallen pine's roots, the ground churned by the creature's passage, the forest turned into an obstacle course. Park was the most physically capable agent Oliver had ever seen. It wasn't enough.

"Riku," Oliver said over the intercom. Not knowing what he was going to say next.

"I see it," came Riku's voice. Completely still.

---

The creature reached him from three angles at once.

In the Registry, Oliver saw the exact moment: Riku's signature — which he'd had as a reference for months, which he knew well enough to read without paying attention — surrounded by the creature's small signatures. First the perimeter. Then closer. Then over him.

Riku's signature didn't disappear. It stayed there, at the center, but now it had layers on top: dozens of tiny signatures that weren't hostile the way things that attack are hostile, but the way water covering something is. Erasing the edges. Diluting the outline.

Riku's intercom crackled.

Then silence.

Sabine's Anchor came down on the mass covering Riku — fixing what it could, which was the outer part, the part that had already passed through — and nothing changed. Dara's Double reappeared inside the mass, trying to create disruption from within, and Oliver watched the Double's signature absorbed in seconds.

Park reached the edge of the mass. Stopped.

Not because he couldn't go in. Because going in with physical force against a creature made of thousands of independent parts was pushing water.

Oliver widened the radius to maximum. Thirteen minutes. The Registry worked, but it was noise — blurred signatures, degraded resolution, every reading with a second of lag it hadn't had before. But he could see Riku's signature. Still there. Weak, covered, but there.

Then Riku's signature changed.

It didn't disappear. It changed. Oliver had no vocabulary for it — it was as if the signature expanded into a different frequency, one the Registry recognized but hadn't seen in Riku before. Wider. Deeper. As if something that had existed in potential for two years of sessions and training had suddenly found the conditions that made it necessary.

The EM field of the perimeter Riku had been maintaining for forty minutes collapsed into a single point.

And then it all came out at once.

---

It wasn't an explosion in the visual sense. No light, no detonation.

It was the Registry emptying out.

In a fifteen-meter radius, every signature stopped existing. Not the way they did when Oliver released them deliberately — but the way a monitor loses signal and the screen goes white. Absolute white for three seconds. Four. And then, from the center outward, the signatures coming back — the ground, the trees, the team, all gradual, as if the world were reinstalling itself.

The black worms were on the ground. Thousands of them, motionless. Some still twitching a little, but with no pattern, no direction. Nothing coordinated them anymore. The network that had made them a single thing for hours had stopped existing, and without it they were just what they were physically: worms.

Oliver searched for Riku in the Registry.

The signature was at the center of the fallen worms. Weaker than it should be. But there.

That was the last thing he read clearly.

The signal faded the way a battery does after running red-hot for too long — not a cutoff, but a gradual decline that suddenly turns vertical. The Registry tried to hold. Oliver kept it up. Two more seconds. Three.

His knees gave out.

It wasn't dramatic. His body decided it no longer needed to hold the weight and acted accordingly — one knee to the ground, then the other, hands in the cold, wet earth of the forest. The intercom still in his hand. The pines around him with their crowns still swaying from what had happened. The smell of turned earth and something else with no name.

Thirteen minutes. Maybe fourteen. He'd lost count somewhere in the last ninety seconds.

He tried to stand. His legs didn't answer the way he needed them to. It wasn't that he couldn't move — it was that the system that coordinated how much effort was worth spending had no resources left to calculate it. The ground seemed like enough for now.

Park's voice came over the intercom. Only one thing:

"Got him."

Two words. Oliver processed them without knowing exactly what they meant yet. But the tone — Park's tone — wasn't the tone of something that had gone wrong.

Asha appeared at his left. She didn't ask if he was okay. She put her arm over Oliver's shoulder and held him at the exact point where the weight was manageable, without having to say anything about it. Her hand on the side that was failing. The temperature of the glove against his sleeve.

"Twelve minutes," Oliver said, because it was the only thing he had to say.

"Fourteen," Asha said. Then: "Good."

Somewhere to the south, among the pines and the mass of motionless worms, Park's flashlight lit up the ground.
