# Chapter 85 — Dead Weight

---

This time the notice wasn't taped to any door. Reber was already waiting for them on the clearing when they arrived, before breakfast, a list on his tablet and no intention of repeating instructions.

"No Domain today," he said, no preamble. "Nobody's going to ask, but if anyone uses one, for anything, the result doesn't count. This measures exactly one thing: what the body can do."

Nobody asked what "this" was yet. Everyone understood within fifteen minutes.

It started with runs — not one, several, each shorter than the last but with less rest between them, until rest stopped existing altogether and only the rhythm was left. Then came the loads: sandbags split between pairs, a course marked with cones that climbed and dropped an artificial slope built against the complex's east wall, back and forth, no announced time limit, which turned out to be worse than any limit.

Oliver felt it within the first five minutes: the Registry had nothing to offer him here. No Domain to read, no structure to study while his body worked — just his own legs, his own chest burning in a way no shortcut was going to ease. He caught himself thinking, somewhere between the third and fourth repetition, about Anka's talk on Mantle — contain before you emit, harden whatever takes a hit — and tried, without much faith in it, holding something tight around his ribs while he ran. He noticed no real difference. Or he noticed one and couldn't tell it apart from the placebo effect of thinking about something else.

Beside him, Petra ran with her jaw clenched and her breathing perfectly controlled for the first two circuits, and visibly less controlled from the third on — she didn't complain, didn't say a word, but Oliver caught her, at one point, looking up at the top of the slope with the same expression she wore facing a diplomatic problem with no elegant way out.

"This one doesn't get negotiated," she said to no one in particular, through her teeth, hiking the sack a centimeter higher on her shoulder. "This one you just endure."

Riku, two steps behind, found breath for a joke from somewhere he had nothing else left in.

"If this is dead weight," he said, no real humor in his voice, just the habit of trying, "I'd rather they just call me dead and get it over with."

Nobody laughed. Nobody had breath to spare for it.

Pier carried the course differently than the rest — not faster, but steadier, a pace that never rose or fell between repetitions, the kind of discipline Oliver recognized, without being able to fully name it, as belonging to someone who'd already done this, or something like it, many times before, somewhere in his years in the system. He didn't smile, didn't say anything, but when he passed Riku at a crossing point in the course, he put a hand on his shoulder for a second — no words of encouragement, just the gesture, and kept walking.

Asha, further up the line, moved with the same contained attention she brought to everything else, wasting no energy on unnecessary gestures, every stride calculated to last until the end instead of standing out at the start. She finished her set neither first nor last, and stayed by the starting line, catching her breath in silence, watching the rest with her usual attention.

Nadia was different.

Her left arm was still protected, the collarbone not fully healed, and the exercise itself wasn't designed for someone in her condition — but when her turn with the sack came, she hoisted it anyway, shifting the weight to her right side in a way that would have hurt anyone with eyes just to watch.

"Nadia," Reber said, without raising his voice much, but in the kind of tone that left no room to pretend she hadn't heard him. "Modified. Half sack, good side only."

"I can handle the full weight," she said, already loading it up.

"I didn't ask if you could," Reber said. "I said modified."

Nadia stopped, the determination on her face visibly colliding with the order, and for a second it looked like she might actually argue. It was Maren who stepped in first, tablet held to her chest, wearing the expression of someone who'd spent the whole morning watching from the edge of the clearing without anyone noticing her until now.

"It's not a punishment, it's a number," Maren said, quieter, just for Nadia. "If you tear that collarbone open again over pride, today's number doesn't measure anything real. It measures an injury, not your level. That's worse for you, not better."

Nadia didn't answer, but she set down the full sack and accepted the half one someone handed her, her face still tight, and finished the rest of the course that way, slower than the others, with nobody commenting on it out loud.

Dara was the last to finish the final repetition.

Not from lack of effort — quite the opposite. She had the smallest body in the group, less muscle mass than the military candidates came factory-equipped with, and every climb up the slope visibly cost her more than it cost any of the others. By the time the rest of the group had finished and stepped aside to catch their breath, Dara still had a full repetition ahead of her.

"You can stop," Reber told her, not unkindly, passing close to her on his round. "The time doesn't count for anyone else anymore. There's nothing to prove by finishing the whole set."

"I know," Dara said, without stopping her climb, her voice broken by effort. "I'm not doing it for the time."

Reber didn't push. He stayed close, not helping, not saying anything else, while she completed the final climb with her legs visibly shaking and the sack slipping off her shoulder every few steps, until she reached the top, dropped the weight where it belonged, and sat straight down on the ground, without the strength left even to find a better spot.

The rest of the group, already recovered, applauded her — not mockingly, with something closer to genuine respect, and Riku, sitting down beside her, handed her a water bottle without a word.

"You didn't have to finish the whole thing," Oliver said, coming over.

"I know I didn't have to," Dara said, still breathing hard. "That's why I finished it."

Reber wrote something on his tablet with no visible comment, but Oliver, who'd spent weeks learning to read that man's silences, noticed him linger a second too long on the screen before moving on to the next exercise.

---

What came next was worse, in the sense that it hurt less physically and humiliated more.

A group from the military contingent — six men and women Oliver didn't know by name, none from Dax's group or Johanna's, just background candidates who'd already been at the Redoubt for weeks or months — ran the same full set, no rest between repetitions, in a little over half the time it had cost the Valais group. They didn't show off. They made no comment aimed at anyone. They just finished, sat apart, and started talking among themselves about something entirely unrelated to the exercise, as if the gap in level were so obvious it wasn't even worth mentioning.

"This is humiliating in a very specific way," Riku said, still sitting on the ground, watching them. "Not the humiliation of losing. The humiliation of them not even noticing you were competing."

"We weren't competing," Petra said, wiping the sweat off her face with the back of her hand. "That's the problem. They were."

Pier, standing a little apart, watched the military candidates with an expression Oliver couldn't quite read — not envy, not exactly admiration, something closer to professional recognition, the kind of look a craftsman gives a job well done even when it isn't his own.

"They've got years of this behind them," he said, without anyone asking. "It's not magic, and it's not a Domain. It's accumulated time, training the body before anyone ever asked them to do it for a living. We've got days."

"That doesn't make it hurt less," Asha said quietly, the first thing she'd said in a while.

"No," Pier said. "But it helps to know what you're losing against, instead of just feeling like you're losing."

Oliver, looking at them closely for the first time since they'd sat down, wasn't so sure it was only accumulated time. The contour around their forearms and torsos wasn't entirely dark — a low, constant contrast, the same kind of diffuse signature Anka had shown two days earlier while talking about Mantle. None of them had an active Domain. But they weren't entirely empty either.

"It's not just time," he said, almost not meaning to say it out loud. "They've got Mantle up right now. Contained, almost nothing, but it's there. That's why the body doesn't bill them the same way it bills us."

Pier looked at him, genuinely surprised for the first time all morning.

"That's not a Domain," he said, not as a question, checking the rule out loud.

"No," Oliver said. "Reber said no Domain. He didn't say no Odem."

The silence that followed carried a different weight than the one from a moment ago — not resignation, but the beginning of something like a strategy. If the military advantage was partly technical and not purely genetic, then it wasn't a wall. It was, simply, something they hadn't learned to do well yet.

Maren came over to the whole group when Reber moved off to organize the next round, tablet still against her chest, and for the first time spoke to all of them at once instead of privately.

"I'm going to say this once, and I'm not going to repeat it in the report," she said, "because it's not a number anyone can usefully put a name on: what I saw this morning wasn't a group losing. It was an entire group deciding not to quit in an exercise literally designed to make most people want to. That doesn't show up on any Board. It should. But it doesn't."

Nobody answered right away. Riku was the one who broke the silence, his voice still tired but the tone already recovering something of its usual register.

"Is that a compliment, Maren?"

"It's an observation," Maren said, not quite smiling, but not denying it either. "Take it however you want."

---

The hangar screens lit up that same afternoon, no ceremony, exactly as Reber had promised the first day: the general Board, updating in real time with the morning's results.

Oliver looked for his number before deciding whether he wanted to find it. #109 to #112. Not a dramatic fall, not enough for anyone else to notice without looking for it directly, but enough for the number to feel real in a way it hadn't when it was just a fixed figure on a dark screen.

Petra dropped from #103 to #105. Riku, from #172 to #173. Pier stayed almost exact — #58 to #59 — a minimal fluctuation that probably reflected his steady pace better than anything else. Asha climbed, from #45 to #41, the only one in the group whose number improved that afternoon. Nadia, with the half sack and her protected arm, fell from #211 to #213 — last on the entire list, one shorter now than the night before. She said nothing about it, her face already back to its usual quick, practiced smile.

Dara dropped too, from #167 to #168, despite finishing the whole set when nobody demanded it of her.

"That's not fair," Riku said, looking at Dara's number with something like genuine indignation. "You finished the whole set. You should be climbing, not dropping."

"The Board doesn't measure willpower," Dara said, no real bitterness in her voice, just the kind of tiredness that no longer had energy left to be indignant about something she'd already known. "It measures time and weight moved. I took longer and moved less than the rest. The number's right, even if it doesn't tell the whole story."

Oliver stood looking at the Board a while longer, after the rest started drifting off toward dinner, thinking about the exact difference between what a number could measure and what he'd seen with his own eyes that morning — a girl smaller than any of the military candidates, finishing a full set with her legs shaking, with nobody demanding it of her, only because she needed to know she could. The Board had no box for that. Maybe, he thought, no number ever would.

---

Dinner that night had the slow, quiet rhythm of a whole group whose legs had turned into something else. Nobody talked much at first — they ate with the kind of concentration usually reserved for tasks more important than chewing, and for a while the only sound at the table was cutlery.

It was Nadia who broke the silence, pushing food around her plate with her good hand without really eating it.

"I hate that she's right," she said, without anyone asking what she meant. "Maren. I hate that she's right, and I hate even more that I needed her to make me stop."

"You didn't need her to stop," Petra said, sitting beside her. "You needed her to stop pretending you didn't need to. Those are different things."

Nadia didn't answer that, but she didn't deny it either, which, coming from her, was almost a full admission.

"What worries me," Riku said, still massaging a thigh under the table, making no attempt to hide it, "is that this repeats. Reber said so. Every week, the same torture, to see if we're improving."

"Two weeks gives us time to improve the number," Asha said, without raising her voice much, but with the kind of quiet certainty that made people listen anyway. "Or to accept that there are parts of this we're never going to match, and stop wasting energy pretending we will."

"That sounds pretty discouraging put like that," Riku said.

"It's not discouraging," Asha said. "It's just true. I climbed today because I found the pace that works for me, not because I tried to copy theirs. That's the only thing I know how to do with what I have."

Pier, who'd been eating in silence, with the same steady calm he'd carried through the whole exercise, looked up.

"Eleven years in the system," he said, without anyone asking him either, "and there are still mornings that make me feel like I'm starting from zero. The difference, at this point, is that I already know that feeling passes. No Board teaches you that either."

Dara, still holding the water bottle Riku had given her earlier, raised it a little, like a tired, unceremonious toast.

"To finishing whole sets nobody's asking us to finish," she said.

Riku clinked his glass against the bottle, and after a second, one by one, the rest of the table did the same — Petra, Nadia with her good hand, Asha with the smallest gesture, Pier last, with something like a real smile for the first time all day.

Oliver raised his glass too, a second behind the others, and thought, as the gesture dissolved back into the ordinary noise of dinner, that this small, clumsy toast probably wasn't going to change anything about the number he'd seen on the screen that afternoon. But it didn't need to. There were things, he was starting to understand, the Board simply wasn't built to measure — and he suspected, without being able to prove it yet, that this was going to be the real lesson of the Redoubt, far more than any number on any screen.

That night, each on their own, without any of them mentioning it to the others, all seven tried the same thing in their own way — each with the clumsiness of two days' practice, each hunting for the same tight contour around exhaustion that Anka had named without promising results. None of them would have called it by the same name. All of them, that night, fell asleep still trying.
