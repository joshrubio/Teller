# Chapter 99 — What's Left Over

---

The letter arrived through the official channel, not the informal one her mother liked to use when she wanted something to feel personal instead of institutional — which, in itself, already told Petra more than the contents would before she'd even read them.

*There's an opening at the Vienna delegation, starting in January. I arranged it myself, calling in favors people have owed me for years. I'm not asking you to take it. I'm putting it in front of you so you stop pretending the option doesn't exist.*

*Two weeks, Petra. After that, the post goes to someone else, and this time I won't pull any more strings.*

Petra read the letter twice, standing in the hallway, before folding it and putting it in the inside pocket of her jacket, where she carried the things she didn't want anyone else to see before she'd decided what to feel about them herself.

Vienna wasn't just any city to her. She'd spent two summers there as a teenager, following her mother from reception to reception, learning to read a room before anyone taught her the technical name for what she was doing — who was talking to whom, who was avoiding whom, which silence meant agreement and which meant the conversation had already ended even though nobody had moved yet. Her mother used to say Petra had the gift before the training, that some people were born reading rooms the way others were born with perfect pitch. Petra had spent years not knowing whether that was a compliment or an elegant way of saying she'd never had another option.

The Vienna post wasn't a minor one. She knew that without needing to ask — her mother didn't spend twenty years of favors on something not worth spending them on. It was the kind of opportunity that, at any other point in her life, she wouldn't have hesitated a single afternoon to accept.

This wasn't any other point in her life.

---

She found Pier that same afternoon, in the secondary training room, taking apart and reassembling the same rope rig he'd spent weeks using to teach the others without needing words.

"My mother got me a post in Vienna," she said, no preamble, because with Pier one was never needed. "Two weeks to decide."

Pier didn't set down what he had in his hands, but he slowed enough to make clear he was really listening.

"Is it what you want?" he said.

"I don't know," Petra said, and the honesty of the answer surprised even her. "It's what I'm supposed to want. I've been training for that world since before I can remember. I'm good at it. I've never said I didn't like it."

"That's not the question I asked," Pier said.

Petra stayed quiet a moment, letting the distinction settle.

"No," she said, finally. "It's not the same question."

"Eleven years here taught me one thing, if it's worth anything," Pier said, going back to the ropes. "Nobody else can tell you the right answer. They can only help you notice when you've spent too long avoiding the real question."

"Did you ever ask yourself the real one?" Petra said. "Before staying, I mean."

Pier didn't answer right away. He finished the knot in his hands, undid it, and started again before answering, as if he needed his hands busy to say this.

"I asked it late," he said. "Later than I'd have liked. I spent my first years here convinced I was just passing through, waiting for the day the physics wouldn't need me anymore so I could go back to my old life. It took me longer than it should have to admit the old life didn't exist anymore, and that the one in front of me, even though I hadn't fully chosen it at the start, had become the only one I actually wanted."

"And if you'd left before realizing that?"

"Then I'd have spent the rest of my life wondering," Pier said. "Which is, maybe, the one thing I can tell you for certain: the question you don't ask in time doesn't go away. It just waits."

Petra didn't answer that. She stayed a while longer, watching him work, and left without saying whether the real question already had an answer or not.

She walked back to her room the long way, the one that circled the archive wing instead of cutting straight through the main hallway, giving herself the time she knew she'd need. She thought about the two versions of herself she'd spent weeks failing to fully reconcile: the one who'd spent two whole years learning to be useful inside AMAT without stopping being herself in the process, and the one who, at some point during those two years, without fully deciding to on purpose, had started building something that belonged to no one else — not her mother, not AMAT, not even the idea she herself had of who a diplomat's daughter was supposed to become.

It wasn't that the Valais Center, or St. Gotthard now, had given her a better life than the one Vienna could offer. It was simpler and harder to explain than that: for the first time, the question of what she wanted to do with her own life had no ready-made answer, inherited, waiting for her since before she was old enough to ask it. She had to build it herself, no template, and that construction — clumsy, slow, hers — was exactly what she wasn't ready to give up yet for a post, however well earned it was.

---

While Petra was talking to Pier in the rope room, at the other end of the complex Anka had nine candidates lined up against the wall of the secondary pit — Oliver, Riku, Nadia, Dara, and five more Oliver knew only by sight, none from Dax's group or Johanna's.

"Today you're not going to hold up standing still," Anka said, no preamble. "Today you're going to push."

She explained the exercise with her usual economy: what had dropped them to their knees that first class of hers — Odem expanded outward from the body, instead of contained inward — wasn't just a weapon she could use against them. It was a technique, trainable, with its own name in the vocabulary AMAT taught any Awakened, with or without a Domain. Pressure, she called it.

Four rows of metal chimes hung from the ceiling of the pit, each one farther than the last — three meters, five, seven, and a fourth at ten that Anka pointed to without extra comment. Few people reached that far, she said — Johanna was one of the few names she cited, giving no detail of when or how. The goal was simple to say, not to do: make a row ring without touching it, using only Odem pushed outward from the body, without using your own Domain if you had one.

The first of the five strangers took four tries to make the three-meter row shiver enough for a barely audible sound. The second, none — he gave up red-faced with effort, the nearest chimes completely still. Riku managed it on the third try: the three-meter row rang clean, a short peal, and for the first time in weeks he looked genuinely surprised at himself instead of just relieved.

"Interesting," Anka said, when it was Oliver's turn. "You've got the Domain least compatible with this in the whole room. The Registry reads inward. It's never had to push anything outward."

She wasn't wrong. Oliver tried five times, feeling each time the same internal motion he used to read someone else's Odem but inverted, like writing with the wrong hand — and five times the nearest chimes didn't even move. On the sixth try, something gave, not fully controlled, without the direction he'd been aiming for in the previous five. The three-meter row vibrated without ringing. The five-meter row, to everyone's surprise including his own, rang whole, a long peal that turned several heads in the room.

For a moment, while the sound was still fading among the rafters, it was the best mark in the group — better than Riku, better than any of the strangers. Anka said nothing, but she stayed looking at the exact spot where the peak of the discharge had been, as if noting something that didn't quite fit with what she'd just explained about his Domain.

"Not every Domain helps here," she told Nadia, when her turn came. "Some even get in the way. That's information too."

Nadia didn't manage it either. Her Impulse was directed force, learned in a straight line from day one — the idea of releasing Odem with no direction, no target, no calculated trajectory, left her visibly uncomfortable, as if the exercise were asking her to unlearn the one certainty she had about her own Domain. The three-meter chimes barely trembled.

The three remaining strangers tried after her, with mixed results — one made the three-meter row ring on his fifth try, shaking visibly; the other two gave up before managing it, one of them with his hands on his knees, breathing like he'd been running. Anka didn't push them to keep going. She noted something in her head that she shared with no one and moved to the next name.

Dara went last. On her first try, the five-meter row rang whole — matching Oliver's mark without visible effort. Before anyone finished processing that, she tried a second time, without Anka asking her to, and the seven-meter row rang too, louder, a peal that lasted longer than a single strike of Odem should have. And for a fraction of a second, while the echo was still running through the rafters, the fourth row — the ten-meter one, the one few reached — trembled. It didn't fully ring. But it trembled, visibly, the metal moving against the still air of the rest of the room.

Nobody said anything for a moment. As the rest of the group scattered for water, still talking about the fourth row's tremor, Anka didn't let Dara go with the others.

"A moment," she said, and her voice dropped low enough to reach only where Dara stood.

Oliver, twelve meters away, filling a bottle at the wall fountain, couldn't hear the rest. He saw Anka turn her head toward Dara the way she turned toward any Odem that had just lit up, and saw Dara go still, arms crossed, in the posture of someone waiting for something they don't know if they want to hear.

He caught fragments, nothing more, when the wind shifted for a moment.

"You made the ten-meter row shiver on your second try," Anka was saying. "Few get that far. Johanna's one of the few names I can cite you, and it took her more than a year of practice to reach what you just reached in two pushes. And even so, what you just pushed wasn't a fraction of what you actually have available."

"That's supposed to be the exercise," Dara said. "Push just enough."

"No. The exercise was to see how far each of you could reach." Anka turned her head a degree, the way she turned toward a newly lit Odem. "Your reserve is bigger than anyone else's here. Bigger, even, than Riku's, and he's spent half his life training his. You've been rationing yours without knowing it, as if you only had exactly enough to pull out one Double and nothing more. That's not true. It never has been."

"One Double already costs plenty," Dara said, more defensive than Oliver had ever heard her.

"One, yes." Anka let the word hang a moment, as if calculating how much to say. "With the reserve you have, there's no technical reason you should only be able to hold one at a time. If there's a reason, it's not in the Odem. It's somewhere else."

Dara didn't answer right away. Oliver, still by the fountain, couldn't see her face from that angle, only her back, stiffer than usual.

"...when I asked you to activate it just to see it, that afternoon," Anka went on, her voice dropping again, "it wasn't only curiosity."

"I already knew that," Dara said, louder again, loud enough to carry clearly. "What I don't know is why."

Anka didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice dropped again, and Oliver only caught the end of the sentence.

"...someone I knew, a long time ago, had a Domain that left two signatures where there should have been one. I never learned to tell them apart fully" — Oliver wasn't sure he'd heard that part right, the wind shifted again — "until there stopped being two."

Dara didn't ask what that meant. She stood looking at Anka a moment longer, something in her face stiller than before, and when she came back to the group she said nothing about what they'd just discussed — not the reserve, not the double signature that wasn't hers. Oliver didn't ask either. But he filed the two sentences away where he kept everything he still didn't know how to fully read.

Back in his room later, he caught himself thinking less about the double signature than about the first part — about how much Odem Dara had to spare without knowing it, about whether that was any different from what had happened to him with Momentum, weeks spent building something nameless in his chest before anyone told him he could let it climb higher. He wondered how many times Anka had looked at someone in the group and already seen the real limit, long before that person even dared approach it.

---

That night she tried writing the answer to her mother herself, without the three rounds of revision she usually applied to anything leaving her hand bound for the diplomatic world.

*I'm not going to Vienna.* She wrote it, looked at it a while, deleted it.

*I need more time than you're giving me.* That wasn't it either.

She tried four more versions, none closer to whatever it was she was trying to say. It wasn't the kind of indecision she knew — the kind that got resolved by weighing advantages, deadlines, the reasonable against the comfortable. It was something else, something that didn't have diplomatic vocabulary to cover it yet. She closed the message without sending anything, neither the yes nor the no, and told herself she'd finish it the next day, knowing even as she thought it that it probably wasn't true.

---

She found Oliver in the dining hall, nearly empty at that hour, and sat across from him without asking if he minded the company.

"Train with me tonight," she said, not as a question.

Oliver looked at her a moment, surprised — they'd gone only a couple of days without being alone together since that night in the secondary room, a little over a week back, when she'd still been finding the real limit of what she already knew how to do.

"Now?"

"Now," Petra said, already on her feet. "I want to show you something. And I want you to watch it closely, because I'm not going to show you again."

She didn't explain what she meant by that. Oliver followed her anyway, to the same room where, a little over a week earlier, she'd let him see, for the first time, that she wasn't starting from zero.

The room was empty, the same bare bulb hanging from the center. Petra took her place in the middle of the space, closed her eyes for a moment, and when she opened them again, something in her posture had changed — the same calm as always, but more deliberate, like someone who'd finished deciding something and no longer needed to keep thinking it over.

"Watch," she said, again, quieter.

Oliver opened the Registry and said nothing else. Neither did she.
