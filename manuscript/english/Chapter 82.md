# Chapter 82 — What Goes Unsaid

---

The Classification Room turned out to be smaller than the rest of the complex suggested — rows of tables facing a wall covered in case files reduced to code, plus a flat-voiced instructor who spent the first hour explaining how the system archived a case before closing it. Oliver took notes he knew he'd never need to reread; the Registry archived better than any notebook could, but writing gave his hands something to do while the instructor's voice turned procedure into background noise. He'd stopped being surprised by how well he retained things a while back — at first he took notes just in case; now he knew, without needing to check, that the whole class would still be intact weeks later if he needed it. Before all this he'd been an ordinary guy, the kind who forgets where he left his keys. Now, among all the oddities Odem had piled onto him, he had a memory that no longer quite qualified as human, and he still didn't know what to do with that part of himself besides use it.

"This is the boring half," Hana had told them that morning, without apologizing for it. "The other half is going to hurt more."

She wasn't exaggerating. For Oliver, though, the room felt less boring than urgent. Every case file reduced to code on that wall had once been someone who became a Field Agent — and what the class taught, underneath the procedure, was the exact margin between the person who reads a situation in time and the person who doesn't. Park had managed it in under two years. Nobody at St. Gotthard talked about timelines out loud, but Oliver knew, from hallway conversations and from how carefully Hana chose her words, that his own was counted in months, not years. He still didn't know whether that was an advantage or a sentence. He listened with the same attention he gave someone else's Domain, trying to turn procedure into something he could use before his time ran out.

Halfway through the session, the instructor projected a real case, declassified enough for teaching purposes — a failed containment incident, years back, in a branch he didn't even name. The lesson wasn't about what had gone wrong technically. It was about what nobody had reported in time, the signs three different agents had each noticed separately and none of them had put together, because nobody had asked. Oliver wrote down the line the instructor repeated twice, as if he wanted it to stick harder than the rest: *what isn't communicated doesn't exist for the system, even if it exists for the person who saw it.* He had no idea, in that moment, how much he'd end up thinking about that line before the day was over.

The field session started after lunch, on the same stretch of ground as the day before, this time with a joint exercise: pairs crossed between groups, each evaluated on a simulated containment scenario. Oliver was paired with a candidate from Johanna's group, thin, quiet, who didn't activate anything recognizable as a Domain from the first second — everything she did came from pure control over Odem, no fixed signature for the Registry to grab hold of. At the other end of the clearing, Nadia was paired with a candidate from Dax's group, a solidly built man Oliver had barely seen up close until then.

Oliver's opponent moved differently from anyone he'd faced so far. There was nothing to copy because there was nothing there to copy — she channeled Odem straight into her body, a sustained pulse under the skin that gave her extra speed and blows that landed heavier than her size suggested, pure technique, trained for years, with no Domain behind it. When Oliver tried to get the Registry to fix on something, anything, he found nothing to grab: no closed pattern, no anchor point, only raw control that no shortcut was going to hand him for free. It was the first time in months he'd faced someone and the Registry, for once, had nothing to offer him. He had to fight with what he already knew how to do with his own two hands.

It didn't go well at first. He took two clean hits before he learned — not with the Registry, but with his eyes — to read the split-second of visible tension she carried before each advance, the moment she loaded Odem. The third time she came at him, Oliver got out of the way in time — no elegance to it, but in time, and he felt, for the first time since arriving at St. Gotthard, something like pride over an accomplishment that had nothing to do with someone else's Domain.

The sound reached him from the other end of the clearing before he had time to process it — hollow, dry, the kind of impact that makes everyone freeze for an instant before they run. Oliver hadn't seen what happened to Nadia. He only heard it, and ran toward it with everyone else.

Oliver got there second, behind Petra.

Petra didn't arrive running. She arrived with her hands already out, the Domain active before her body had finished moving — Oliver felt Swap light up like a whipcrack, sharper than anything he'd caught in passing from months spent near her, but not clean: fragments that almost fit together, an axis, something like an anchor point, the kind of image you catch an instant before your eye can bring it fully into focus. It wasn't a complete reading. It was more than he'd ever had before — and still nowhere near something he could use. A second blow was already coming, the candidate still in motion, with no time to check his own momentum. Petra swapped Nadia with an empty equipment crate three meters away — Nadia appeared at the edge of the clearing, and the crate took the second impact in her place, crumpling against the metal with a crunch that had nothing hollow about it.

The instructor stopped the exercise. A medic arrived in under a minute.

Nadia's collarbone was probably hairline-fractured — the medic didn't say so out loud, but the way he immobilized her arm said it for him — along with a shallow cut above her eyebrow that bled more than its real severity justified. She sat where she was told, let them examine her, and when the rest of the group came over, with the kind of concern nobody quite knew how to voice without sounding like they were overreacting, she smiled.

"I'm fine," she said. "The scare was worse than the hit."

Nobody quite believed her. Nobody contradicted her either.

---

Oliver found Petra alone near the locker rooms fifteen minutes later, her hands still faintly trembling — not from the effort of Swap, he suspected, but from what had almost happened if she'd been half a second slower.

"Thank you," Oliver said, not entirely sure it was his place to say it.

"I didn't do it for you," Petra said, but without any edge to it.

Oliver hesitated before saying the rest.

"I felt it," he said. "When you activated Swap. Different than other times."

Petra looked at him then, really looked, something crossing her face that wasn't quite alarm but wasn't far from it either.

"How much?" she said, and the question came out sounding more tired than hostile.

"I don't know," Oliver said, honestly. "I don't know how to measure it yet. Or if I want to know right now."

Petra didn't say anything for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had a different edge than it had in the meeting with Maren and Hana.

"It's not an accusation," she said. "It's just that I don't know what to do with the idea of someone holding on to something of mine without my having decided that. I still don't know. Maybe I won't for a while."

"I don't know what to do with it either," Oliver said.

"Good," Petra said. "Then we both have something unfinished."

She left before Oliver could answer, and he stayed there a moment longer, with no answer left to hold on to, feeling the weight of a conversation that hadn't gone anywhere and that, he suspected, still had to happen for real at some point.

He took out his phone, came close to writing to Park about it, and stopped before he finished the message. He still didn't know what words to use for something he didn't fully understand himself.

He deleted the message without sending it.

---

He found Petra again that afternoon, without quite meaning to. Turning toward the infirmary corridor, he saw her from the entrance, sitting on the floor by the door, knees pulled to her chest. Asha stood beside her, leaning against the wall with her arms crossed, and Dara took up the rest of the bench, legs stretched out, with the same tense patience as the other two. Oliver stopped before he reached the corner, not yet sure whether to go over.

"You didn't have to stay," Nadia said, when the door opened and they let her out, her arm already in a sling. "I'm fine. I've said it like fifteen times today."

"I've heard it all fifteen times," Petra said. "And I haven't believed it once."

"Me neither," Dara said, without getting up. "For the record."

Asha didn't say anything, but she pushed off the wall and made room for Nadia between herself and Petra without anyone having to ask.

Nadia dropped into the space they'd made for her, the earlier smile completely gone now.

"I couldn't let them see me scared," she said, in a much lower voice. "If they see me scared, they start wondering if I should be here. And I don't have anywhere else to go. This is the only thing I have, since my father..."

She didn't finish the sentence. She didn't need to.

"Nobody's going to send you home over a hairline fracture," Petra said.

"It's not about the collarbone," Nadia said. "It's about what I thought while I was on the ground. I thought: if this is what it costs to stay, maybe I don't want to pay it. And then I thought about what leaving would mean, and that scared me more than the hit did."

Nobody had a ready answer for that. The four of them stayed there — Dara already sitting closer than she had been at first — without saying anything else for a long while.

"Hey."

Oliver turned around. Pier stood in the middle of the corridor behind him, with the kind of expression that didn't announce anything urgent.

"Give them the hallway," he said, no reproach in it, almost some humor. "Some things don't need a fourth witness. Come on, dinner's almost ready."

Oliver looked once more at the four of them, who hadn't even noticed he'd been there, and followed Pier back toward the mess hall without a word.

---

Dinner that night ran longer than usual — nobody in a hurry to get up, everyone sharing the same silent reluctance to go back to a room where there was nothing left to do but think about the day.

Riku told, with more detail than necessary, the story of a candidate from Johanna's group who'd fallen flat on his face trying to impress someone during a break, and got Dara laughing for real for the first time since they'd arrived. Pier talked about an old mission where he'd had to convince an entire committee that a building wasn't actually collapsing, only looked like it was — Asha listened with the full attention she reserved for very few things, her palms still on the table.

"And the building?" Dara said. "Did it fall in the end?"

"Three years later," Pier said, without changing his tone. "Nobody remembered my report until it was too late for it to matter. That's the part I don't tell in the formal briefings."

Riku laughed again, and this time it lasted long enough that two people at the next table — from Johanna's group, Oliver thought he recognized, though he wasn't entirely sure — turned their heads for a moment before going back to their own conversation.

Nadia, her arm still in the sling, ate with her left hand without complaining, and when someone asked how she was doing, she said fine, and this time, with dinner well underway and the mood looser, it sounded a little more believable. Petra cut up her food without being asked, a small gesture Nadia accepted without comment, and Oliver, watching from across the table, understood he was seeing the quiet continuation of what had happened in the infirmary hallway — something neither of them was going to name in front of the others, but that didn't need naming to be real.

At some point Dara asked if anyone else had noticed the food tasted different here than at the Valais Center — more processed, less homemade — and the question, small as it was, sparked five full minutes of nostalgic comparisons about specific meals none of them was going to admit missing for reasons bigger than the food itself.

Nobody mentioned the door. Nobody mentioned Voss, or Geneva, or the number Hana hadn't wanted to give them that morning. The conversation moved around the edges of all of it, touching every adjacent topic without ever landing in the center, and Oliver understood, somewhere between the second course and the moment someone brought coffee nobody had asked for, that this was exactly what Maren had described as different from the Valais Center: seven people deciding, each on their own, how much of what they felt was worth saying out loud that night.

At some point it was Riku who brought it up, without quite thinking it through — asking out loud if anyone else had seen the instructor's face when he stopped everything. He realized, mid-sentence, where the question was headed, and closed it off awkwardly with a comment about the food that fooled no one.

"It's fine, really," Nadia said, a hand on his arm for a second, enough to say she was okay without having to say it again. "Besides, I didn't even see the third hit coming. Ask Dara, she was closer."

Dara hesitated, but gave in, maybe because telling it out loud was easier than letting everyone imagine something worse.

"He read the pattern on the second hit," she said. "Not the third — the second. He only waited for the third to act, because by then he already knew exactly where you'd be before you did." She looked at Nadia, not without affection. "It's not that you did anything wrong. It's that he's probably been using Odem longer than any of us have been alive."

"What worries me more is the candidate, not the collarbone," Dara said, more serious than usual. "I mean, technically he did the right thing. But he didn't stop after. He kept closing distance like Nadia was still a real threat."

"That's training," Pier said. "Nobody stops mid-exchange to check whether the other guy's still dangerous. That's part of the lesson too, even if it's not the one they were teaching in the room this morning."

"Sounds like you're justifying it," Riku said.

"I'm not justifying it," Pier said. "I'm naming it. Those are different things."

Asha, who up to then had only been listening, said something that surprised Oliver with how direct it sounded coming from her.

"The candidate didn't choose to be here the way we did, either," she said. "This might be costing him something we don't see too."

Nobody answered that right away. It was Riku who broke the silence, this time with a real question instead of a joke.

"What about you?" he said to Oliver. "You got the one from Johanna's group, right? The quiet one."

"No Domain," Oliver said. "Or if she has one, she never used it once. It was all pure Odem, channeled straight into the body. The Registry had nothing to grab onto, no pattern, no anchor, so I had to fight blind, with nothing to study afterward."

"That's worse than fighting a Domain, isn't it?" Dara said, genuinely curious. "At least a Domain has a shape. You can read it."

"Much worse," Oliver said. "A Domain tells you, sooner or later, what it's going to do. Pure control tells you nothing. It just teaches you, hit by hit, that they've been practicing for more years than you've even been using Odem."

"That's what I meant about not everyone here having the same advantage," Pier said. "The ones without a Domain make up for it with years we don't have. Watch how someone breathes sometime before they load Odem with no Domain behind it. That's where all the training lives, in the second before the hit, not in the hit itself."

"I noticed that with mine," Riku said, warming to it. "He loaded from the heels. Like everything passed through the ground before it went up."

"The one from Dax's group who fought Nadia loaded from the chest," Dara said. "Direct, no wasted motion. That's why she didn't see the feint coming. He didn't need her to."

The conversation stayed there a while, each of them contributing however much or little they'd managed to notice, comparing ways of moving Odem the way people compare accents. Oliver listened, filing away every detail without meaning to, and understood that this too was part of what St. Gotthard was trying to teach them, even if nobody had put it on the official program: to read each other before an instructor had to explain it.

Petra talked less than the others for the rest of dinner. She smiled when it called for it, answered when asked, but more than once Oliver caught her staring off at some point that wasn't the table.

They got up little by little, without anyone giving the formal signal that dinner was over. Oliver was among the last, and crossing the main corridor toward the east wing, he saw her.

Petra stood at the intersection leading to the access level, still, looking down the corridor to where — Oliver knew now, after the orientation — the unguarded door was. She wasn't moving toward it. She was just looking at it, with the same expression she'd had sitting on the floor by the infirmary, as if she were running a calculation she hadn't finished solving.

Oliver stopped at a careful distance, unsure whether to go over or leave her alone.

He said nothing. She didn't see him either, or if she did, she gave no sign it mattered. She stayed there, looking at the door, long enough for Oliver to understand that whatever Petra was deciding that night, she wasn't going to decide it yet.

In the end, she turned around on her own, without crossing the corridor, and walked back toward the dorm wing the same way she'd come. She passed Oliver without a word, and he said nothing either, and the two of them kept walking in directions that turned out to be the same one for longer than either of them had planned, without either mentioning what they both knew the other had just seen.
