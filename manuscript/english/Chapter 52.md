# Chapter 52 — What Wasn't Theirs

Deivis Uzcátegui had been waiting for his sister at the stop for forty-five minutes, and it was that wait, nothing else, that put him on the street at the exact hour the checkpoint appeared where there had never been a checkpoint.

He worked fixing refrigerators and air conditioners, when there were parts to be had and customers who could pay in something other than promises. That afternoon had been a good day — two jobs, real cash in his pocket — and the only thing on his mind while he waited was whether the cash would stretch to dinner for both of them, or whether he'd have to choose between eating himself or letting his sister eat.

They weren't the usual ones. He knew that even before he saw the mismatched uniforms, two with an official patch and three with nothing, all with the same rifle hanging just as loose off the shoulder. The usual ones, at least, pretended to be looking for something — an ID, vehicle papers, the standard excuse for the toll everyone in the neighborhood already knew to pay without arguing. These didn't pretend anything.

"You, come here," one said, pointing at him with his chin before Deivis had made a single suspicious move. "Against the wall."

"Why? I haven't—"

The rifle butt didn't let him finish the sentence. It didn't hurt at first, only stunned him, and by the time the pain arrived he was already on his knees with his hands tied by a plastic zip tie that bit into his wrists every time he tried to loosen it.

There were three others already against the same wall — two young men, not much older than him, and a middle-aged woman who kept repeating someone's name, low, like a prayer she no longer expected anyone to answer. None of them had done anything Deivis could identify. That, he understood, with the kind of clarity that only arrives when it's already too late to be of any use, was exactly the point. They weren't looking for anyone in particular. They were collecting.

"Where are you taking us?" the woman asked, the first time she raised her voice loud enough for anyone beyond herself to hear.

No one answered. They loaded them into the back of a flatbed truck with the tarp torn at one corner, and Deivis, looking at the scrap of sky visible through the tear, thought about his sister arriving at an empty stop, not yet knowing she was about to spend the rest of the night calling a phone no one was going to answer.

The truck picked up four more before the drive was over — an older man who didn't stop coughing the whole way, two girls who couldn't have been twenty, and a teenager who wet himself from fright without anyone reproaching him for it, because everyone understood, without saying so, that any of them could have done the same. Nobody talked much. The woman repeating the name under her breath kept repeating it, slower each time, as if the words themselves were wearing out.

"Where are you taking us?" the teenager asked, at some point, his voice breaking.

"Wherever they take people who don't count anymore, kid," the coughing man answered, without bitterness, only tiredness. "I've spent two years watching them take others. Never thought I'd be the one asking it myself."

---

Park didn't learn anything about Deivis Uzcátegui until much later.

The road back from Casimira's house was slower than the way in — Ronald drove with the kind of care that only shows up after seeing something you'd rather not have seen, taking the curves with half a throttle less than usual.

"She's never wrong about that stuff," he said, on a straight stretch, without fully turning around. "What she said about you. The four fires."

"I don't know what use that is to you," Park said.

"It's useful for knowing whether I can trust you to do something, if it comes to that, beyond asking questions," Ronald answered. "Can you?"

He didn't get to answer. The road, at the next curve, wasn't a road anymore — a whole tree, one of the big ones, fallen across it end to end, its roots still half-buried in a fresh sinkhole that couldn't have been more than a day old. The same tremor from the night before, Park understood. The mountain leaving its signature on something that had nothing to do with Casimira or with Odem readings, only with weight and gravity.

Ronald braked, got off the bike, pushed at the trunk with both hands out of pure habit before giving up.

"This isn't moving even if you drag it with the bike," he said. "What a pain. Have to take the long way around, another hour and a half."

"Give me a second," Park said.

He planted himself beside the trunk, calculated the point where the mass was least evenly distributed, and concentrated just enough Strength there for the tree's own weight to work for him instead of against him — not a blow, a sustained pressure, growing until the wood tore free of the ground with a wet crack and the whole trunk rolled two meters into the ditch, clearing the way.

Ronald stood staring at the empty space where the tree had been longer than it took him to ride the bike past it.

"I don't look like much," Park said, climbing on behind him, as if that fully answered the question Ronald no longer needed to repeat. "But I hit harder than I look. If it comes to that, and it's needed, you'll see it yourself."

Ronald didn't say anything else the rest of the way, but something in how he drove changed — less caution, more resolve, as if he'd just gotten back, all at once, the urgency the fear at Casimira's house had taken from him.

---

That afternoon, when Ronald dropped him back at the hotel, all Park had in front of him was what he'd just heard, with still no way to fit it into anything operational.

Salcedo was waiting with two coffees and no question asked too fast.

"How'd it go?" he asked, finally, once Park had been quiet a while.

"There's a medium who can feel Odem with no instrument at all, more precisely than any reader I've used in four operations," Park said. "And she thinks whatever's happening on that mountain has a name. She wouldn't tell me yet."

Salcedo didn't seem surprised by either thing.

"Send Ferrer what you have tonight," he said. "Even incomplete. She's better at deciding what to do with loose pieces than the two of us together."

Park did, word for word, the sensation of Casimira stepping back from him, the four origins she'd named without needing any file to do it. Ferrer answered in writing, minutes later, a single line: *Confirmed. Follow the thread. Report when you have a name.*

He stayed up longer than he should have, looking out the hotel window at a street where the only light not coming from a generator was the moon's. He'd seen cities at war without any war declared — Hartford had its four cordoned blocks, Seoul its island, Lisbon its neighborhood with an old wound. He'd never seen a whole country behave like a wound — every institution, every uniform, every checkpoint, functioning just enough to look normal and failing just enough that no one, not even the people who lived there, could say for certain who to trust on any given Tuesday. It wasn't a Ruler's territory. It was something humans had done to themselves, alone, generation after generation, with no Odem needed for it to hurt just the same.

He was still thinking about that when Ronald's phone buzzed for the first time.

---

"This one's ugly, bigger than usual," Ronald said, already at night, the phone still warm in his hand after the third call. "Normally it's two, three people, a pickup. This time they've got a whole truck. Someone counted at least eight, maybe more."

"Where are they taking them?" Park asked.

"That's what nobody knows for sure. But the route toward the base of the mountain only has two possible roads at this hour, and one of the two passes an abandoned warehouse they've been using as a stop for months."

Park didn't have to ask what Ronald was proposing to do, because Ronald had already done it without asking him first: when they reached the crossroads where he'd told Park to meet him, there were seven more motorcycles waiting, not one.

"Wilkins, Freddy, you'll meet the others as you need to," Ronald said, gesturing at the group with a quick wave. "All of them have someone on that wall of photos at Nélida's house, or know someone who does. We've spent months talking about doing something the day a real chance came along. Tonight it looks like it did."

An older man than the rest, in his fifties, an old scar crossing his eyebrow, stepped forward.

"We're not fighters, just so it's clear, my friend," he said. "We know how to block a road, cause a distraction, get people out fast from somewhere they shouldn't be. The rest is going to have to be you, if you can really do what Ronald says you can do."

One of the younger ones in the group, who hadn't said anything until then, spoke from the back, his voice less certain than Wilkins's but no less decided.

"I've got two cousins on that list of Nélida's," he said. "If there's a chance tonight that there won't be a third, I don't care about the risk. I've already lost too much waiting for someone else to fix this for us."

Wilkins nodded, not needing to say anything more about it.

"What did you tell them?" Park asked, looking at Ronald.

"That you're trustworthy and you hit harder than you look," Ronald said. "I didn't go into detail, because I don't have detail to give them. Just the hunch that you're the closest thing to a chance we've had in years."

Park didn't correct the word "chance." He had no room left, that night, to worry about what people decided to believe about him.

---

The warehouse was an abandoned industrial building, rusted zinc roof, a packed-dirt yard where the truck was already parked with its lights off by the time they arrived. Park counted the visible men from the cover of the brush — six around the vehicle, three more going in and out of a side shed with what looked like a list in hand, checking something against the people already inside.

"Nine against one isn't a fight, agent," Wilkins said, beside him, his voice low. "That's suicide with witnesses, and the ugly kind."

"They're not going to be nine against one for long," Park said. "I need you to block both exits from the dirt road the moment you hear the first shot, not before. And have a way ready to get people out of here the second the truck stops being watched."

"And if it goes wrong?"

"Then pull back," Park said. "None of you needs to die proving something tonight."

They stayed crouched in the brush longer than Park would have liked, waiting for one of the men at the shed to finish his cigarette and go back in, for the angle of two crossing flashlight beams to stop covering the one strip of shadow that served as his way in. The air smelled of diesel and earth baked all day by the sun, and from somewhere inside the truck came, barely audible, the held-back crying of someone who no longer had the strength to do it out loud. Park waited for the second flashlight to go dark entirely before moving.

---

He moved on the man furthest from the group first — the one smoking alone, ten meters from the rest, the rifle slung on his shoulder instead of in his hands. Park closed the distance from behind without the packed dirt giving away his steps, and the arm that circled the man's throat wasn't looking to put him out gently: it squeezed until the man's legs stopped holding him up, the lit cigarette falling onto his own shirt with no one awake anymore to put it out.

"Who's there?" said the second, swinging the rifle up halfway, his voice already cracked with fear before Park had finished appearing.

He didn't have time to find out if anyone answered him. Park was already on him — a blow to the forearm that broke something with a dry crack before the weapon could fire, and the second impact, to the chin, with the full weight of the turn behind it, burst his lip against his own teeth and left him down, no longer just unconscious, his jaw visibly out of line.

"They're going down, man!" the third one shouted toward the shed, not waiting for an answer, and instead of raising his weapon he stepped back into the shadow of the truck, forcing Park to choose between chasing him blind or letting him go. Park faked the first step into the shadow — enough that the man, already almost invisible between the cab and the bed, grew confident and leaned half his body out to aim with a better angle. That half second of exposure was the real mistake. Park changed direction mid-stride, closing on the short side instead of the long, and the blow to the forearm landed with enough force for the bone to give before the trigger did — a shout that didn't quite become a word, and the man went down clutching an arm bent at an angle no arm should hold.

"You don't know who you're messing with, you idiot!" the fourth shouted, without firing first. He shouted something else toward the shed, a frequency number and a word Park didn't recognize, and only then raised his weapon — calling for backup before defending himself, the kind of decision no rapid-response manual would teach but that a man who knows he's alone in a losing fight makes anyway. He didn't have time to finish aiming. Park's blow, to the chest, left him without air and without a weapon, doubled over on the ground beside the other three, coughing up something that wasn't only saliva — but the call had already gone out.

The fifth answered from the side shed, and the shot he fired did go off — it grazed Park's left side with a hot pain he filed away for later, without stopping to fully assess it. He didn't have Class-R this time. He had no one covering his back except untrained people a hundred meters away, waiting for a signal.

The shot was the signal.

The motorcycles started all at once, a roar of seven engines that broke any chance of the men at the truck keeping calm. One of them ran for the dirt road, straight toward where Wilkins and the others already had it blocked with their own bikes crossed; another stood his ground, aiming at the exact spot the first fallen body had come from.

Park used the second of confusion to close on the one who'd just shot at him from the shed — a short jump, the momentum from the last blow redirected at a low angle. The elbow split the man's eyebrow to the bone, and he went out the shed door backward, blood already covering half his face before he'd finished understanding he no longer had a weapon in his hands.

"This isn't going to stand!" he managed to shout, already on the ground, more out of pride than because he had any way left to back up the threat.

Four remained. Two of them, watching their companions fall in an order they couldn't follow any logic in, decided the truck mattered more than the fight, and ran for the cab.

They didn't get it started. Freddy, from his bike, threw something heavy — a rock, maybe a wrench, Park didn't quite see — straight at the windshield.

"Take that, you thug!" he shouted, his voice cracked with adrenaline.

The driver, covering his face on instinct, lost the two seconds Park needed to reach the door. He hauled him out with a pull that slammed his shoulder against the metal frame with a crack the driver himself felt before he screamed, and left him on the ground clutching an arm he couldn't lift.

The one who'd stood his ground didn't run anywhere. He went straight for the wall where the eight were still tied, and grabbed the teenager by the collar with his free arm, the rifle pressed against the boy's temple before Park could close the distance.

"Stay right there, don't move!" he shouted, his voice too loud, too fast, the voice of someone improvising a plan he hadn't had five minutes earlier. "One more step and I finish him right here!"

Park stopped. Not for the order — for the angle: the man was shaking, and a rifle shaking against someone's head was, if anything, more dangerous than one held steady.

"Put the weapon down," Park said, not approaching, hands visible, open. "The other eight are already free. You've got one way out of here, and that's not it."

"And what do you know about my way out?"

"I know that if you pull that trigger, you have none," Park said. "And so far, everyone who's stayed still when I asked has walked out of here. Everyone who hasn't, hasn't."

The man didn't fully lower the weapon, but the shaking in his arm changed rhythm — real doubt, not feigned, from someone starting to do the math on how much of this night he had left. It was enough. Park closed the last three meters at the exact instant the barrel drifted half a degree off the boy's temple, gripped the man's wrist and twisted it upward before his finger finished deciding anything, the rifle falling to the ground without having fired.

The teenager dropped to his knees, breathing as if he'd gone a whole minute without doing it at all.

The man, now on the ground, still had the official patch on his shoulder — the small flag, sewn crooked, the same emblem Park had seen that morning at the airport, on the welcome signs of a government that no longer controlled even half of what it claimed to. He crouched, tore it off with a sharp pull, and put it in the teenager's hand instead of keeping it or throwing it away.

"This isn't yours," he told the man on the ground, without raising his voice. "You wear it. It was never yours."

The boy stared at the patch in his own hand for a moment, not knowing what to do with it, until he closed his fist around it and said nothing — but something in his face, for the first time since Park had arrived, wasn't only fear.

---

The last two didn't wait for Park to reach them. They ran, and no one chased them — not Park, whose hands were already busy cutting plastic ties with the tip of a knife Wilkins pressed into his hand without asking, nor the motorcycle men, more interested in getting people onto their bikes than in hunting anyone down in the dark.

Eight people came out of the back of the truck, none of them in the same condition — a man with his jaw swollen from a blow taken before Park arrived, a woman who wouldn't stop repeating a name through tears, two young people who said nothing at all, the kind of silence Park had already learned to recognize as something worse than panic.

The woman was the first to reach Park, before he'd even finished putting away the knife. She said nothing at first — she just stared at him as if she still didn't trust that he was real, and then cried, all at once, with her whole body.

"Jesus Christ," she said, her voice broken, almost breathless, the same word she'd been repeating all night against the checkpoint wall, in the truck, low, until it stopped sounding like a name and started sounding like the only thing she had left to keep from screaming.

"Jesus Christ, Jesus Christ..." She took a deep breath, once, twice, her hands still shaking when she finally raised them to Park's face. "Thank you. Thank you, son. Thank you."

Wilkins was the first to climb into the truck bed to help them down, with a gentleness that didn't quite match the scar on his eyebrow or the way he'd talked about "suicide with witnesses" half an hour earlier.

"There now, there now, easy," he kept repeating, to each one, without it sounding hollow from repetition. "It's over, friend. We're going to get you home."

Not all of them could walk on their own. One of the silent young men dropped to his knees the moment his feet touched the ground, and it was Freddy who caught him before he hit the ground, saying nothing, needing no one to explain to him why a stranger deserved that care that particular night.

Deivis Uzcátegui was the last one down, his legs shaking enough that Ronald had to hold him by the arm.

"Why us?" he asked, to no one in particular. "We didn't do anything. None of us did anything."

No one had an answer better than silence.

Yusmary arrived on foot from the nearest crossroads, alerted by Ronald before the operation even started, with a box of borrowed phones so each of them could call their people without waiting to get home. She put hers in Deivis's hand herself, dialing the number he could barely remember with his fingers still shaking.

"She's alive," she told him, after listening ten seconds to the voice on the other end. "She's still at the stop, waiting for you. Tell her you're on your way."

Deivis couldn't manage anything coherent when he finally got the phone to his ear, only his sister's name repeated twice, but it seemed to be enough for both of them.

Ronald came up to Park once almost everyone was distributed among the bikes, without the easy smile he'd brought to the hotel that morning. He stood there, searching for something to say, and what came out was simpler than it seemed to have been planning.

"Thank you," he said, and didn't clarify for which of the things. "I've spent four months thinking my brother really drowned. Tonight, for the first time, I thought maybe not. Maybe he's somewhere like this, waiting for someone to do what you just did."

He didn't cry, but something in his face loosened for a moment, the kind of expression it took Park a while to name until he understood it was simply hope, with nothing calculated behind it.

---

Salcedo arrived fifteen minutes later, once the motorcycle men were already splitting the rescued people between the available bikes, two per vehicle, headed for homes where no one was going to ask too many questions that night. He checked the side shed while Park finished tying up the three captured men, and came back with a laminated folder in hand.

"Transport manifest," he said, "with a seal that shouldn't be on any document from a regular unit. And this." He held out a portable radio, still on, the channel fixed to a frequency Salcedo typed from memory into his own device. "Frequency assigned to a unit that answers directly to General Roa. It's not a suspicion anymore, agent. It's a name on paper."

Park stood holding the radio for a moment, feeling the exact weight of how little that name on paper was going to be able to do against a man with that rank.

"There's something else," Salcedo said, lower. "Ask the woman. The one who wouldn't stop repeating a name."

---

Her name was Rosaura. Salcedo had no way of knowing that the name she'd repeated all night was a prayer, not a clue — but what he wanted Park to ask her about had nothing to do with that. It had to do with her niece, missing four weeks now — not from a truck, not from a checkpoint, not from anywhere a manifest could record.

Her hands were still stained with dirt, from when they'd thrown her onto the truck bed getting her aboard, and she hadn't washed them once through the whole conversation, as if the dirt mattered less to her than anything else she had to explain that night.

"She went out to get firewood behind the house," she said, her voice still cracked at the edges, wrapped in a jacket someone had lent her. "Ten minutes... no more, that's all it was."

She stopped, breathed, and when she went on she sounded more like herself than the woman who'd cried against Park's face half an hour before.

"We were calling for her from the door. When we went to look, there was nothing. No footprints, no trace anyone had taken her. Like the mountain just swallowed her whole, I swear it."

"Has this happened before?" Park asked, though he already knew, with the same cold certainty he'd recognized the pattern with at Nélida's house, what the answer was going to be.

"Three that I know of, just in my own hamlet," Rosaura said, her voice fully steady now. "None with a truck. None with armed men you can hate with a name and a face. Those just... stop being there."

"Three, you say?" Park asked. "Just in your hamlet?"

"Just mine," Rosaura said. "I don't know how many more in the rest of the area. I don't know if anyone knows."

Park thought about the radius of the mental notebook he'd been building since the airport, the same one that had kept narrowing around the mountain with every new name — and thought that radius, until this very night, had only counted the people a truck and armed men could take. No one had been counting the other kind of disappeared. The ones who left with no one left to hate for it.

He thought, without meaning to, of the last times he'd seen someone cry this close. Mariana, over Zoltán's body, crying for someone who could no longer hear her. Katalin, not letting go of her cousin's wrist as he faded. Rafa, before that, on the floor of a machine room no one mourned in time. Every one of those times had ended with someone crying over what Park hadn't been able to save. Tonight, for the first time in a long while, the tears that had touched his face weren't grief. They were something else, something Park didn't remember feeling this close since he started this work, and which for that same reason he had trouble recognizing at first.

Park looked toward the mountain, invisible in the dark beyond the warehouse, and felt the same dense weight he'd been noticing since the first day — except this time, for the first time, he understood that weight had nothing to do with the men he'd just left tied on the ground.
