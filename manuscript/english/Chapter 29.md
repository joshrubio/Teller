# Chapter 29 — Lisbon - Arc 3

Three days after the island, Cartwright's helicopter landed in the courtyard of the AMAT-KR headquarters in Seoul.

Park heard it before he saw it — a rotor pattern that belonged to no Korean transport — and by the time he stepped into the courtyard he already knew who was about to climb out of it.

Kiki was with him at that moment. She'd spent three days coming by every morning, with or without a medical excuse to justify it, and that day she didn't have one either. She'd sat on the bench by the window of Park's room mid-morning, unannounced, her right arm still wrapped in the same kind of bandage as Park's, and she was still there when the rotor noise pulled them both out into the courtyard.

Cartwright climbed down without field gear — a coat that belonged to no Seoul climate — and with the look of someone who'd flown straight in from somewhere else and hadn't slept on the way.

"Park," he said, by way of greeting, then, looking at Kiki, "Rank 2. Congratulations."

Kiki didn't answer. She just looked at him, working out who he was before deciding whether he deserved a response.

"I need you to come with me," Cartwright told Park. "Now."

"Where?"

"I'll explain in the air."

It wasn't a request. Park looked at Kiki once — she said nothing, didn't come closer either, just nodded, the same gesture she'd used all week instead of words — and followed Cartwright to the helicopter.

The private plane was waiting on the military strip outside Seoul. Cartwright didn't say a word during the helicopter ride there, and Park didn't ask — two years had taught him that Cartwright talked when he wanted to talk, not when he was pushed.

Once in the air, Korea falling away behind them and nothing but clouds through the windows, Cartwright opened a physical folder — not a tablet, paper — and set it on the table between them.

"Lisbon," he said.

Park opened the folder. The first page carried a code he already knew: EU-1755-PT.

"The file you gave me in Hartford."

"The same one. You never opened it all the way."

"I didn't have access to all of it."

"You still don't." Cartwright closed the folder with two fingers before Park could get to the second page. "What you do have is this: a small branch, a problem that doesn't read the way it should, and a briefing I'm giving you myself instead of letting you read it alone."

Park stayed quiet a moment. Cartwright never did in person what he could delegate to paper.

"Why the change?"

Cartwright looked at him then, really looked, for the first time since climbing out of the helicopter in Seoul.

"Because this doesn't explain well in writing. And because I want to see your face when I tell you."

It wasn't a threat. If anything, it sounded like care — the dry kind of care Cartwright never called by that name.

"Lisbon earthquake, 1755," he began. "Thirty, forty thousand dead in hours. An Odem signature consistent with Ruler-level activity, documented for the first time in coordinated form by correspondents of the era. In the archives, it's the unofficial origin point of EU-GENEVA."

"The same protocol that covers Hartford."

"The same code. What that means exactly, I don't know, and if I did I couldn't tell you." A brief pause. "What I do know is that AMAT-PT is a small branch, and that they've spent generations managing this case in a way that isn't exactly standard."

Park waited.

"You're going in as a free agent," Cartwright said. "You don't answer to local command. You work with them, not for them."

"Why?"

"Because I want you to look at something with eyes that haven't spent generations looking at it the same way."

Park closed his eyes for a second. His right arm, the one that had taken the full weight of the last strike against the chimera on the island with no reinforcement to protect it, still hadn't fully healed — the field doctor had been clear: hairline fractures in two metacarpals, no displacement, but enough to need Class-R in rotation while it set. They'd prescribed it the way you'd prescribe an anti-inflammatory: two layers, morning and night, until the next checkup. The left one, which had stopped fully responding during the fight in the bowl days earlier, was doing better now — still weaker than normal, but it obeyed when asked.

"What am I supposed to see?" Park asked.

Cartwright didn't answer directly. He looked out the window, the clouds breaking for a moment to reveal some ocean below, and when he spoke again it was slower.

"In Seoul you resolved something that had no category. Rank 3, well earned. But this is different. In Lisbon you're not going to resolve anything by force." Another pause. "If you try, you'll lose before you start."

That was the last thing he said about the mission for the rest of the flight. He opened the folder again, but this time to read it himself, and Park caught, before Cartwright tilted it out of view, a word underlined by hand in the margin of one of the pages — *saudade* — with no context beyond the word itself and a question mark next to it, as if whoever had written it years ago hadn't known quite what to do with it either.

---

The city appeared through the clouds as the plane began its descent — not like Seoul, which announced itself from a distance with vertical lights, but horizontal, spread out, red roofs stepping down in terraces toward a river too wide to look like just a river.

"The Tagus," Cartwright said. He'd spent the last forty minutes with his eyes closed without being fully asleep. "Here they treat it like it's part of the sea. Technically it isn't. Practically, it doesn't matter."

It was the closest thing to a personal observation he made the whole flight.

Cartwright didn't come up to the branch office with Park. He said he had calls to make, and the car that had picked them up at the airstrip dropped him off first, somewhere that wasn't the AMAT-PT office — Park didn't ask where. The driver took him the rest of the way without a word, and when he was dropped in front of the building with the faded tile facade, Park understood without being told that this part of the briefing was over.

The AMAT-PT headquarters looked nothing like what Park had expected after Seoul. No sixteen-story building, no private subway lines — a three-story office in the middle of the city, a facade of faded azulejos, tall windows, the kind of construction that in any other city would just have been old.

"Gaiola pombalina," said the man who met them at the door, noticing Park's eyes on the walls. "A wooden cage inside the stone. They built it that way after the earthquake — it flexes instead of breaking. This whole district was rebuilt on the same principle."

He introduced himself as Duarte Ferreira. Rank 2, a firm handshake, the kind of person who assessed before smiling and then smiled for real. He made no move to mark hierarchy with Park — a nod, nothing more, the gesture of someone acknowledging an equal without needing to say so.

"Free agent," Ferreira said, not as a question. "Cartwright already told us."

"Does that bother anyone?"

"Some." It didn't seem to bother him. "This way."

Inside, the team was small — a fraction of what Park had seen on the island. An older man, lab coat not fully buttoned, looked up from a computer with too many windows open, wearing the expression of someone genuinely happy to have someone to explain things to.

"Henrique Salgado," he said, not waiting for Ferreira to introduce him, already on his feet. "You must be the one from Korea. I read your file. Well, the part they let me read."

"Doctor Salgado runs the branch's theoretical research," Ferreira said. "The institution undervalues him. I don't."

Salgado seemed neither offended nor surprised — he just smiled, the smile of someone who'd made peace with how the institution saw him a long time ago.

"Before you ask anything," Ferreira said, "you should know what we've spent generations working on here. It isn't a case. It's a family."

Park said nothing, waiting for him to continue.

"More than two hundred years ago, sometime after the earthquake, a lineage started taking charge of something even AMAT itself didn't know how to fully close. The branch knows about it. The branch allows it, or allowed it for a long time, and now simply tolerates it without saying out loud that that's what it's doing." Ferreira crossed his arms. "There's no active operation against them. We watch. We coexist."

"Why tolerate it?"

"Because nobody's found a better way," Salgado said, his smile dimming a degree. "And because what they manage doesn't behave like anything else AMAT knows how to close."

"Tell him about the earthquake," Ferreira said. "The short version."

Salgado rubbed his hands together, the genuine enthusiasm rushing back.

"The short version is nobody knows whether the earthquake caused what's under this city, or what's under this city caused the earthquake." He sat on the edge of the desk. "Odem, generally, has a logic of presence. The more something is believed in, the more form it takes, the easier it is to read. Here it's the opposite. Here it behaves as if the whole city has spent three hundred years missing something it can't fully name."

"Saudade," Park said.

Salgado stopped mid-sentence, the surprise lasting barely a second before turning into something warmer — the genuine, almost childlike pleasure of someone from outside knowing the word without him having to explain it first.

"Where did you hear that?"

"In a margin. On the way here."

"Curious that they wrote it that way, untranslated," Salgado said, adjusting his glasses, the full smile back. "But it makes sense. There's no exact equivalent, and you won't need one — you'll feel it once you've spent enough time in this city, whether anyone explains it to you or not. The problem is we can't read with any precision something that by nature resists being read. The instruments the rest of AMAT uses don't work the same way here. You're going to have to learn to look differently."

Ferreira checked his watch.

"Tomorrow we start with the known perimeter. Tonight you get settled."

---

The apartment they assigned him was two streets from the office, above a shuttered shop, with a window that faced an alley too narrow to let direct light in at any hour of the day. Park set his bag down unopened on the bed and stood there a moment, doing nothing, the kind of pause he hadn't been able to afford in days.

He thought about Kiki at the hospital window in Seoul, three mornings running with no excuse needed. He thought about Ji-ho, about the question that still had no name. He thought about his father saying *Rank 2* in the same voice he'd used for *Rank 3*, no difference in weight between the two, and how that absence of difference was, in itself, the part that weighed the most.

None of that existed here yet. A city he didn't know, a language he didn't speak, a problem the branch's own expert admitted he couldn't read well. For the first time in weeks, Park didn't have to carry what he already knew — only what he didn't know yet, which was different, and for a while, easier.

It didn't last. Ferreira's phone rang before Park had finished unpacking his bag, and ten minutes later he was back at the office.

Salgado had the photograph open on the screen when Park walked in, the smile he'd worn all afternoon already gone.

"It showed up sometime before dawn," Ferreira said, turning the screen toward Park. "In Alfama. Nobody saw it happen."

An interior courtyard, somewhere in the old city, with something carved into the stone that shouldn't be possible to carve into fresh stone — a symbol Park didn't recognize, cut so cleanly it didn't look like chisel work.

"Probably the Vasconcelos," Ferreira added.

"I don't think so," Salgado said, voice low. "I've spent fifteen years cataloguing every mark this lineage leaves on the city. I know their way of writing Odem into stone as well as I know my own handwriting. This isn't theirs. The technique is different. More aggressive. As if whoever did it didn't care about being noticed."

Ferreira was already on his feet.

"How far is Alfama from here?"

"Fifteen minutes," Salgado said. "On foot."

"We're going now." Ferreira looked at Park. "You coming?"

Park thought about Cartwright, somewhere else in the city by now, busy with whatever he had to do. He thought about what Cartwright had told him on the plane — *if you try to solve this with force, you'll lose before you start* — and how the first real test of that warning had just shown up carved into a courtyard in the old city, before he'd even finished unpacking.

"I'm coming," he said.

Salgado was already loading instruments into a case, his hands moving faster than his smile had an hour ago. Ferreira waited at the door, no apparent hurry but no stillness either — the calm of someone who'd done this many times before and knew exactly how much time they had before something changed.

Outside, the Lisbon night had already fully fallen, and somewhere in Alfama someone had left a mark the branch's own expert hadn't seen in fifteen years of watching the same city.
