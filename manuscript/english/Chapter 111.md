# Chapter 111 — The Board

---

By the third day, the three of them always met at the same starting warehouse, though never at the same time.

Anka had the map of Milan spread over the same table as always, marked now with more annotations than could fit without overlapping — clusters of activity around the Navigli, where the water and narrow bridges favored whoever knew the terrain; a suspicious emptiness at Porta Genova, too quiet to be coincidence; a dense knot near Corso Buenos Aires, where three separate groups had spent two days hunting each other without anyone gaining real ground.

She didn't read the map with her eyes alone. She let the Registry settle over each zone, a different weight in each one, and noted what the paper couldn't show — where the activity had the texture of a real hunt, and where it was only the noise of tired people moving without a plan.

Reber arrived past midmorning, his clothes dusted to the knee, no hurry at all in his stride.

"Three," he said, before anyone asked. "Small group near the Stazione Centrale. They didn't even manage to run properly."

"Badges?"

"Whatever they had on them. Not much."

Anka didn't look up from the map.

"The total's still dropping faster than it did at this point last year."

"Good or bad depends who you ask," Reber said, pouring himself water from a bottle that wasn't his. "Doesn't matter to me. I bring down the same number of people every day, whatever the total is."

Father Conti didn't arrive. He was simply there, a step behind the table, as if he'd been standing in that exact spot since before the conversation started.

Neither of them flinched — they'd had two days of this already — but Anka felt, again, the same weight pressing on her chest a beat before he said anything, and again wondered, without saying it out loud, where he'd come from this time.

"Isola," Conti said, without preamble, as if continuing a sentence started somewhere else. "Two groups about to cross paths without knowing it. One of them has an advantage it doesn't know it has."

"Are you going to intervene?" Anka asked.

"Not yet." He crossed himself, the same quick, automatic gesture as always. "I'd rather see what they decide to do with the advantage before I decide what to do with them."

"And the six from Valais?" Reber asked, without much interest, the kind of question he asked out of professional habit more than real curiosity.

Anka found the point on the map without needing to look twice — she'd been marking it since the first night.

"Active. Coordinated, most of the time." She hesitated a second before adding the rest. "The Webb boy organizes like we'd been training him for this specifically, not for whatever he's supposed to be doing at St. Gotthard."

"That's not a coincidence," Reber said. "It's exactly what the three of us do — except he learned it by watching, not by being taught."

Conti said nothing to that, but something in his stillness suggested he'd been thinking the same thing before either of them got around to mentioning it.

Anka turned back to the map. Somewhere in that city, six names she knew from St. Gotthard had spent three days doing exactly what she was trying to read from up here, with no map of their own beyond whatever Oliver could see with his own eyes.

---

"Where were you?"

It was Nadia who asked, but everyone in the room above the workshop had been carrying the same loaded question for hours, and Oliver knew it the moment he walked through the door and saw their faces.

"Sweeping the area," he said, dropping the backpack to the floor. "I've been out since before five, and I haven't let the Registry go quiet longer than I had to."

"You've been unreachable for hours," Riku said, his voice tighter than a simple complaint. "We could've organized better with that."

"I know." Oliver didn't fully apologize, and that, more than anything else, was what made Asha look at him a second too long. "But I found something. There's real activity near Porta Ticinese — a small group moving at night between two fixed points, like they've got a base and hunt out from it instead of moving all the time. If we place them right, we can get there before they go back to ground."

He pulled a badge that wasn't his from his pocket and set it on the table with the others.

"One more."

No one celebrated it with the same enthusiasm as the night in the park. Pier counted it with the others, in silence.

"Twelve," he said. "Twelve since the first day, between the six of us."

"And yesterday, none," Asha said, no accusation in her voice, just the bare fact. "We went out, moved all day, and came back empty-handed. That counts too."

"That's exactly why we need to bet differently today," Oliver said. "Like the first night."

"The first night worked out by a miracle," Riku said, not looking at him. "And on top of that, it was Iker who ended up off the board, not us. That's not a repeatable strategy. It's something that happened once."

No one answered that right away.

---

They set the device on the same table where the new badge had been, and the screen lit up with its usual flicker before settling on the numbers.

"Two hundred thirteen on the first day," Nadia said, reading aloud, though everyone already knew it by heart. "A hundred eighty-seven after the first night. And now—"

The number updated as she spoke, as if the Board itself wanted to underline the point.

"A hundred fifty-six," Dara said.

No one said anything for a moment. Fifty-seven people out in two days — more than a quarter of the entire exam, gone from the list without them having witnessed most of those falls.

"Look at the positions," Asha said, sliding her finger across the screen. "This isn't moving like a normal board. It's moving like something that stopped following any order that made sense yesterday."

Johanna showed up at fifteenth, climbing hard from where she'd started. Oliver found himself at thirty-nine — better than he'd been before the night in the park, worse than he'd been the morning after that same night, when the number, for a few hours, had dropped to something none of them had said out loud, for fear of jinxing it.

"Fifty-eight," Asha said, finding her own number. "Yesterday I was at forty-two."

"Sixty-six," Pier said, no feigned surprise at all. "I dropped ten places in a day without anyone touching a single one of our badges."

Riku found his a moment later: forty-seven, worse than the day before. Nadia, seventy-one. Dara, eighty-three.

"We all dropped," Nadia said. "Even you, Oliver — even if yours looks better on paper, you're still farther from where you were the morning after the park."

"Because the rest of the board is moving too," Oliver said. "It's not that we're losing ground. It's that everyone else is hunting too, and yesterday we stood still compared to them."

"With six people and twenty-five spots, the math doesn't work out anyway," Asha said. "Even if we hunted perfectly every day, it's still mathematically close to impossible for all six of us to make it. That doesn't change because we bet big one night."

"Explain it with numbers, if it's so clear to you," Oliver said.

Asha didn't need to think about it. "A hundred fifty-six left, and dropping. Say we reach the end of the exam with a hundred twenty — optimistic pace. We need six of twenty-five spots among a hundred twenty people, all competing for the same slot we are. Not even counting the instructors actively hunting us, the odds of all six of us making it at once are lower than the odds of at least one of us getting left out. That's not pessimism. That's arithmetic."

"So what, we surrender to the 'close to' part?" Oliver said. "The first night we went in with a risky idea and it worked. Today we can do the same thing, bigger, with all six of us instead of two."

"Or we can keep playing as a team, which is what's kept us alive so far," Asha said. "What happened the first night wasn't a strategy. It was luck with legs, and you know it."

Riku didn't take a side. Neither did Nadia. The silence that followed resolved nothing, and they went out anyway, without ever quite deciding which of the two was right.

---

They went out divided the moment the argument ended, with no clear winner — Asha kept her objection, Oliver kept his, and both hung there unresolved while they split up the streets according to the plan they already knew.

They found the Porta Ticinese group shortly after noon, just as Oliver had predicted — three shapes moving between two apartment blocks, the same back-and-forth pattern he'd seen before dawn.

Oliver stayed back, on the roof of a low building overlooking both streets, the Registry open over the whole radius at once — his job that afternoon, decided among all of them before heading out: eyes, not fists. He coordinated over the comm, guiding Riku and Asha down the east street, Pier and Dara down the west, closing the block between the four of them while Nadia covered the one rear exit.

"They're moving west," Oliver said. "Riku, you'll have them head-on in twenty seconds."

Riku reached the intersection in time, but the group had already changed direction — not west, but into a narrow passage between two buildings that Oliver hadn't spotted from the roof, a dead angle the Registry couldn't cover without him moving too.

"I lost them," Riku said. "They went into something that's not on the map we put together."

"Nadia, do you see them?"

"Nothing here."

Oliver felt the frustration rise before he could fully name it — the whole structure they'd built, everyone in position, every movement coordinated with precision, and still the one gap nobody had foreseen had opened exactly where it hurt most. He moved himself, coming down off the roof faster than was wise, but by the time he reached the street the passage was already empty at both ends.

"They're gone," Pier said, arriving a moment later, out of breath. "Clean. Not a trace worth following, not in the short term."

No one said anything right away. The same structure that had worked on paper, coordinated with Oliver as the one point that could see the whole board, had just failed at the one moment it actually mattered.

"It's nobody's fault," Asha said, though the way she said it sounded more like an attempt to close the subject than real conviction. "The plan covered what we knew. We didn't know about that passage."

"That's exactly the point," Oliver said, his voice sharper than he meant it to be. "If we'd gone all in from the start, instead of spreading ourselves so thin, someone would've been there to cover it."

"Or we would've left three gaps instead of one," Riku said. "You don't know that. Nobody knows that. That's what hunting in a city that isn't ours looks like."

No one had a better answer to offer, and the silence that followed weighed more than any reply would have.

---

It was fifteen minutes later, the frustration still not fully faded, when they crossed paths with him.

A lone candidate, young, moving down a side street with no real caution — the kind of mistake only someone already deep down the board makes, someone who's stopped believing caution changes anything. Oliver recognized him through the Registry before seeing him up close: a weak signature, barely trained, nothing like a Domain of real weight behind it.

He didn't put up any real resistance. He raised his hands the moment he saw them surround him, the badge already half-offered, like someone who'd accepted a while ago that this moment was coming.

Oliver got there first. It didn't need to be him — Riku or Asha could have finished this in two seconds without a single blow — but the frustration from the empty passage was still there, looking for somewhere to land, and this kid, without meaning to, handed it the whole thing.

The first hit was more than necessary. The second, with the kid already doubled over, even more.

"Oliver!" Asha's voice cut through before there was a third.

Oliver stopped with his fist still closed, his breathing more ragged than the fight — if it could even be called that — justified. The kid was on the ground, the badge already loose from his neck, coughing, more scared than seriously hurt, but hurt more than he needed to be.

No one said anything for a long moment. Riku picked the badge up off the ground without meeting Oliver's eyes. Pier helped the kid sit up against the wall, with a gentleness that hadn't been necessary a minute before.

Oliver opened his hand slowly, as if he'd just noticed it was still closed.

"Thirteen," Dara said quietly, not sounding remotely like a celebration.

No one said anything else right away. Pier was the last to leave the kid, checking he could breathe fine before turning his back on him completely, and caught up to the group a few steps behind the rest.

"Oliver," he said quietly, just for him. "I know what it's like to carry frustration with nowhere to put it down. But choose better next time where you set it loose."

"I know."

"I'm not asking if you know. I'm asking if you'll do something different the next time it feels the same."

Oliver didn't have a ready answer, and Pier didn't wait for one — he kept walking, leaving the question suspended between them, something he'd have to work out alone, later, once the day's frustration was no longer fresh enough to excuse anything.

They started back in silence, each of them carrying their own version of what they'd just seen, and none of them decided yet whether it was worth saying out loud.
