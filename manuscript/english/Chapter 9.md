# Chapter 9 — Lines


The fridge went first. Then the window sign, then the rest of Dunmore all at once, and in the total dark Oliver felt the pull in his chest not stay in his chest — it stretched outward, a taut thread running out of him in a direction his body recognized before his eyes had seen anything at all.

"Don't move," Park said, and the hand that closed around his arm wasn't the hand of someone asking for calm. It was the hand of someone calculating distances.

"Something's pulling at me. It's *inside*."

"Which way's it pulling harder?"

Oliver pointed, without thinking, toward the empty back room.

That was almost enough. The thread yanked all at once, with a force that had nothing to do with anything Oliver had felt before, and his own feet skidded half a meter across the tile before he could plant them. The watch display case next to the safe bent toward that same point, glass groaning, and shot toward him.

Oliver had no time to process what he saw coming. Park had already moved, the floor creaking under his feet with a sound too heavy to belong to an ordinary body, and the yank that pulled Oliver out of the spot was rough enough to crack his own shoulder. The display case slammed into the wall where his head had been a second before, a dull impact he felt in his chest even from two meters away.

"Door. Now."

They came out onto Halloway just as the first streetlight on the block died with a dry crack, like a switch too big for any human hand. *Farid*, Oliver thought, unbidden, already running: *you close up, I've done enough for today.* It hadn't been tiredness. It had been the same kind of line Farid used to close out any other account, said to his own face, and Oliver, relieved that no one had accused him out loud, had been too slow to actually hear it.

The street didn't go dark all at once. It went dark in order, window by window, streetlight by streetlight, following the same direction they were running, and on every surface the dark touched, for one second, a ring darker than the rest remained: an incomplete circle, never quite closed. Oliver had seen it seventy-four times, in ink, on paper, in Farid's same cramped hand.

A man came out of a doorway three doors down, in a T-shirt, hair mussed like someone who'd just gotten up off the couch when his own apartment lost power. He stood planted on the step, looking down the street at the dark advancing toward him.

"Move!" Oliver shouted at him, out of breath. "Don't stand there!"

The man looked at him, then looked at the dark, and never managed to decide in time. Something with no shape of a mouth closed over the entire step, a clean bite with no visible teeth that took him along with the door, the mat, and a good chunk of the wall behind it — and when the dust finished settling there was no one there, no blood, no clothes, only a gap in the exact shape of a doorway.

Oliver stopped dead.

"No," Park said, not looking back, hauling him by the sleeve. "Not now. Run."

Whatever was chasing them didn't walk. It advanced with the patience of something that knows it'll reach its meal sooner or later, and the sound arrived a beat behind it — a constant scrape, like enormous jaws dragging unhurried across the ground.

Halloway narrowed into a row of old buildings, blackened brick, rusted fire escapes — and one of them, the tallest on the block, had its door standing wide open, the half-fallen sign of an old dockworkers' hotel above it.

"There," Park said, and didn't wait for Oliver to ask why.

They crossed the lobby in the dark, took the first flights of stairs two at a time, and by the fourth floor the steps below them started losing texture, darkening, swallowed by the same invisible mouth that had eaten the neighbor's doorway.

"It's not going to work," Oliver panted. "It's using the whole building."

"I know." Park didn't sound surprised. "That's why up. Keep climbing."

On the seventh floor, still climbing, Oliver asked what he'd wanted to ask the whole way up.

"How do you know how to do all this? The calculating, the—" he didn't find the word "—the moving like that."

"I don't charge it by hitting things," Park said, almost in passing, like he was explaining a technical joke instead of the reason they were still alive. "I keep what hits me. Every time something pushes me — a jump, a fall, a hard stop — I don't let it go. I set it aside. And when I need it, I release it all at once, in the direction I choose, not the one it came from."

"And right now?"

"Right now I've been storing it since we left Dunmore." A pause, brief, deliberate. "Not much. Haven't had the chance to spend it on anything useful."

They came out onto the roof with their lungs burning. The roof was already darkening under their feet, a ring spreading from the north edge, and Park grabbed his arm and hauled him toward the south edge without giving him time to argue.

"Eight floors," Oliver said, looking down.

"I know."

"We can't—"

"I can." It didn't sound like bravado. "And if you hold on tight, so can you."

The floor gave way behind them with the same bite as before, and they jumped.

The world vanished beneath their feet for an instant with no logic of time to it: just wind, and the street rushing up too fast, and Park's hand locked in the fabric of his shirt with a grip that didn't hurt because there wasn't time for it to hurt. Then the fall changed. It didn't stop. It transformed, the speed redistributing in a way Oliver couldn't describe except that his stomach stopped falling even though the rest of him kept dropping. Park kicked off the façade halfway down — a hard impact that left cracks in the brick and changed the angle of the descent — and they landed.

Their feet hit the asphalt with a violence that should have snapped their ankles.

It didn't. But Park didn't roll clean: his right shoulder hit the ground an instant before the rest of him, and the sound he made getting up was different from before — shorter, more contained.

"You okay?"

"It works," Park said, not looking at his shoulder. "Just need to not use it as leverage for a while."

They ran. Oliver had stopped watching the people fleeing around them, or the driverless cars skidding into dumpsters — he was watching the spaces between things, the web of filaments he could now make out more clearly at street level. When they converged on a point, that point stopped existing, swallowed whole, without leaving even dust.

"Not there," he said, pointing at a mailbox without breaking stride.

Park changed course without asking, and the air between the two buildings ahead of them compressed toward him in the same instant, too fast to dodge completely. He managed to raise his right arm — the same shoulder — and the bite threw him into a shop window with an impact that shook the whole ground-floor glass front.

He got up immediately. But something in the way he held that arm no longer belonged entirely to him.

"It doesn't erase by force," he said through his teeth, still moving, when Oliver asked why the Authority tore some things loose and left others untouched. "It erases what stops pushing back. If something doesn't move, if it isn't connected to anything that resists, it lets go of it."

It fit. It fit with a precision that gave Oliver a chill different from fear: seventy-four names, each one an account that had stopped resisting, that Farid had decided, with his own hand, to stop holding up.

They reached a block where several downed power poles still swayed among dead sparks, the ground covered in taut cables that hadn't finished settling. The pressure vanished all at once.

"It can't reach us here," Oliver said, almost not believing it.

"Yes it can," Park answered, not lowering his guard. "It just needs to separate us first."

It was there, in the false stillness of that block, that Oliver started to see something different: not the lines converging on what was already still, but the exact instant a line decided which way it was going to pull — one heartbeat before the jaws took shape.

"It's going to go up that building," he said, pointing without thinking.

"How do you know that?"

"I don't know. I see it before it happens."

The building buckled inward a second later, exactly where Oliver had pointed.

"That's not just seeing," Park said finally, low. "I don't know what it is. But don't stop."

The truce didn't last. The cables stopped vibrating one by one, and when the last one went still, Oliver felt the weight return to the air all at once.

Park didn't run.

He stood planted in the middle of the street, feet firm on the cracked asphalt, the bad shoulder held against his body at an angle he no longer bothered to hide.

"Park."

"I understand how it works now," he said, not looking at him, his voice tight with a calm that was getting harder to hold. "I can't keep dodging forever. And every time I dodge, it learns a little more about us."

"That doesn't mean you have to stand there and—"

"It means exactly that."

The air around Park began to vibrate in a way different from before — not an outside tremor, but something charging from within, every jump and every absorbed impact from the whole chase condensing now into the rigidity of his shoulders.

At the end of the street, the gap appeared — not materializing, not crossing the space between them: it simply was there, where a second before there had been nothing.

"Park, don't—"

"Stay behind me."

The lines began to draw taut from every motionless surface on the street, converging on the exact spot where Park had planted his feet, and for the first time all night Oliver understood that what came next wasn't going to be another chase.

What the gap did wasn't a step. It was an accumulation: folds of absence layering over each other until they finally found something like a shape. Almost human in its proportions, but with nothing an eye could fix on as a feature — where a face should have been there was only a concavity, a point that didn't return a gaze but swallowed one, and still Oliver knew, without needing eyes to confirm it, that it was looking at both of them.

Seventy-four names had ended in front of this, or something like this, in empty rooms and loading docks and cheap boarding houses all over the city, with no one else ever seeing it. It struck Oliver, suddenly, as obscene to be seeing it in the middle of a street, in plain view of any window that still had its lights on.

The air turned so heavy that breathing took a conscious effort, something you had to decide to do instead of simply doing. Park didn't take his eyes off the gap.

He took a step forward. The asphalt cracked under that step, not from impact but from the pressure he'd spent the whole chase storing up — every jump, every hard stop, every absorbed blow, now condensed into a single body about to burst.

The lines lunged at him, faster than before, closing like a whole mouth instead of a bite.

Park didn't retreat.

He exhaled. And let it all go.

It wasn't a blow Oliver could call a blow. It was a discharge, compressed air pouring out of Park in a single direction, and it hit the exact center of the gap with a sound Oliver felt in his molars before his ears. For half a second, at the exact moment of impact, the lines lit up for him with a detail he'd never had before — not just where they were, but what they were made of, layer over layer of something that had spent twenty-nine years accumulating with no one ever looking at it straight on. The silhouette deformed, folding in on itself as if its own internal tensions had been forced to pull in directions that contradicted each other.

It didn't disappear.

It fell back — a meter, maybe two — and the lines holding it up went slack, trembling out of alignment, unable for an instant to decide which way to pull.

Park dropped to one knee. The bad arm hung now without seeming to have decided to let it hang; the other stayed raised, shaking with the same tremor shuddering through the air around them both.

"Park."

"I can't..." It cost him to finish the sentence. "I can't hold this much longer."

Oliver didn't take his eyes off the gap. The lines, slack a second before, were already hunting for their shape again, dragging themselves back toward the same center with the exact patience of something that knows it has the whole night ahead of it — and that Park, in contrast, did not.

"It's re-forming," he said, voice breaking.

"I see it."

Park didn't try to get up using the bad arm. He braced the good one against his knee, found his balance at some point Oliver couldn't identify, and stood up on his own, slowly, with the same silent stubbornness he brought to everything else.

The silhouette finished pulling itself back together before Oliver had time to decide whether that was relief or not. It didn't advance slowly this time. It launched itself whole, one motion with no transition, straight at the exact spot where Park had just planted his feet.

Park didn't retreat, and he didn't stand still waiting for the impact either. He moved to meet it, the good arm already loaded with what little he had left, the trajectory calculated in midair with that precision of his Oliver still couldn't quite name.

The two of them converged on the exact same point of the asphalt, and Oliver never got to see which of them got there first.
