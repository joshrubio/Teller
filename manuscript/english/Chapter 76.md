# Chapter 76 — Departure


The impact arrived before the image did — a dull thud Oliver felt in his chest through the gallery's thick glass before he understood what had caused it.

Below, in a training pit the size of a court, a man in the military candidates' fatigues was on his knees, his breath cut short into a sound that carried clearly all the way up. Standing in front of him was someone who, a second before, hadn't been there.

Oliver hadn't seen the blow land. Nobody in the gallery had — seven people freshly arrived, still against the railing without having decided to stop and watch, all watching the same way.

The pit itself had nothing special about it: bare concrete, lines painted long ago and nearly worn away, an overhead light too white to leave any shadow to hide in. What filled the space wasn't the architecture. It was the silence of the few instructors ringing the perimeter, none of them talking to each other, all with the same attention fixed on the same point — not the man on his knees, but the one still standing.

The winner hadn't moved yet. His arms hung loose at his sides, his head tilted slightly, and for however many seconds it took the fallen candidate to decide whether he'd try to get up, he did nothing — no shift of weight, no visible breath, none of the things a tired body usually does between one effort and the next. It was the kind of stillness Oliver had seen before in hunting animals, in the handful of nature documentaries he'd watched as a kid — the exact instant before something decides it's waited long enough. He couldn't remember ever seeing that same stillness in a human being.

No instructor stopped the fight.

Somewhere behind him in the gallery, someone let out a breath they hadn't realized they were holding. Oliver didn't turn to see who. He couldn't look away from the man standing motionless over the fallen candidate — from the way his weight seemed distributed in a manner that matched no recognizable fighting stance, as if his whole body were calculating something his face gave away nothing about.

Oliver didn't know yet, in that moment, how they'd gotten here. He didn't know, either — and this unsettled him more, though he couldn't have said why — exactly what it was he'd just watched go still.

---

Hours earlier, nobody had spoken during the first hour on the road.

The SUV took the mountain climb, its suspension absorbing potholes Oliver still felt anyway, a dull jolt transmitted more through the seat than the sound. Riku had fallen asleep against the window before it was fully light. Dara stared at the landscape with her eyes open but unfocused — the kind of look that isn't processing what it sees, only using it as an excuse not to look inward. Petra kept her hands laced together in her lap, still, knuckles already a little pale this early into the trip.

Oliver carried Kaspar's note in his jacket's outer pocket. He could feel it there, a rectangle of paper that weighed more than it should, and when the car took a curve and the weight shifted against his side, he let the question he'd been asking himself since that night fill the space the car's silence left open.

*She made the same choice you did, for reasons that were good too.*

It wasn't the question of who Anke had been. That one he could put off — he'd been putting it off for nine days, and he could put it off longer. The one that wouldn't leave him alone was smaller, and had nothing to do with her: whether choosing to stay, that night, had really been a choice at all, or just the story he'd decided afterward to tell himself about something that was going to happen anyway. He had no answer. He closed his eyes against the cold glass of the window and let the engine noise fill his head for a moment.

Seven of them rode in two vehicles — Asha and Nadia in the second. Pier had insisted on riding with Oliver, Riku, Dara, and Petra in the first, without giving a reason worth arguing over. Nobody had mentioned Sabine or Lev all morning. An empty seat for either one of them would have been easier to name than the silence that sat in its place.

Valais fell behind at some point Oliver never noticed crossing. The valleys narrowed, the towns grew smaller and further apart, until the last sign with a name he recognized vanished behind a curve and none came after it. Pier recognized the Furka Pass before any sign confirmed it — he'd been in the system long enough for that — and with the real snow, packed hard over treeless slopes, came thinner air too, air Oliver felt in his lungs before he felt it on his skin. A glacier appeared to the left for a moment and vanished when the road turned. Nobody said anything else after that. They'd stopped commenting on things a while back.

It was Petra who spotted the complex first — not a building at first, an absence: a stretch of hillside where the stone ran a slightly different color, too straight for geology, too plain for civilian architecture. An entrance cut directly into the rock, with a reinforced metal door standing open.

"It's a bunker," Riku said. "I mean, literally. Like the ones from the war."

"The Réduit," Pier said, without turning. "Switzerland built a whole network of them through this part of the Alps, to fall back to if they were ever invaded. Never needed to use them."

"And now AMAT does?" Dara said.

Pier didn't answer that.

The car passed a checkpoint with a small gesture from the driver and entered the tunnel. The light changed all at once — the weak morning sun replaced by white artificial light bouncing off unpolished rock, the engine noise multiplied by the echo into a roar no open road had produced all morning. The tunnel curved, dropped, curved again, longer than the visible distance from outside had suggested.

"This doesn't look anything like Valais," Dara said quietly.

Petra tightened her hands a little more in her lap, without saying anything yet.

"No," she said finally. "It doesn't."

The tunnel ended in a chamber carved out large enough for a dozen vehicles, half the spaces already full — bare rock overhead, reinforced concrete at mid-height, original construction from the middle of the last century, over which someone had installed, decades later, something much newer. They got out of the car one by one, into the chamber's cold, dry air, with a faint smell of metal and ozone.

A corridor opened at the far end, wider than any at the Center, trilingual signage running the length of the ceiling — French, German, Italian, not a single word of Spanish or English, as if nobody had planned for anyone outside this circuit to ever read it. Staff crossed in both directions with the specific hurry of people running late for something, none of them looking twice at the newly arrived group. At the far end of the corridor, a lone figure crossed in the opposite direction, a bag over one shoulder. Nobody in the corridor stepped aside to let her pass, nobody greeted her, nobody seemed to register that she was there — she walked like someone who no longer belonged to anything in that building.

The Registry activated without Oliver deciding it should. What it read wasn't a Domain — it was a gap. A passive signature so low, so flat, that for half a second he thought the Registry had failed. Not like Sisi's structured silence. This was someone emptied out.

The figure reached a door that opened on its own, no guard, no checkpoint, and went through. The door closed behind her with no one holding it.

"What is it?" Riku said, following Oliver's gaze without finding anything to look at.

"Nothing," Oliver said, finally. "I don't know what it is yet."

An instructor in the complex's gray uniform came over with a tablet in hand, confirmed seven names, crossed something off on the screen, and pointed down the corridor without further ceremony.

"This way. Don't stop to look at anything you haven't been told to look at."

Nobody asked why he said it. A second later, they understood.

Fifty meters ahead, the corridor opened onto a raised gallery with a low metal railing — the kind of structure built for watching something below without interfering. The instructor led them there without slowing down, as if the detour were part of the standard route and not a decision of his own.

Below, two people were already fighting by the time the group reached the railing. It wasn't a choreographed exercise — something in the rhythm, in the lack of pauses, said so before Oliver could put a name to why. A solidly built military candidate moved with the tight discipline of years of training, short strikes, deliberate defense, nothing wasted. The other one didn't move like that at all.

He changed. One second he was crouched, still, with the kind of stillness that comes before something — and the next he was no longer where the first strike expected him, but a meter away, in a completely different stance, lower, shoulders forward in a way that matched no fighting style Oliver had seen trained at the Center. There was no visible transition between the two. It was like watching one frame cut and spliced onto another.

The military candidate threw a series of strikes calculated to close the distance. The other let them come — really let them come, no blocking, no fully dodging — and in the exact instant the last strike should have landed, he wasn't there to take it anymore.

And then came the moment Oliver had already seen without knowing he was seeing it: the military candidate on his knees, breath cut short, the rest of the group still against the railing without having decided to stop and watch.

---

Two instructors stood beside the pit with tablets identical to the one the man who'd guided them carried, taking notes without looking up more than they had to.

The military candidate took three seconds to get back into guard. Three seconds that, judging by how one of the instructors tapped something on his screen without looking away from the pit, counted for something too. He charged again, aiming low this time, a takedown attempt that would have worked in any other context — Oliver had seen it work in the Center's courtyard dozens of times. Here, the other fighter simply stopped being at the height the attack expected. He didn't jump. He didn't dodge in any conventional sense. It was as if the ground under his feet had decided, for an instant, that he weighed less than he did.

The military candidate got back up. He attacked again, faster this time, with the urgency of someone who understood that losing this exchange would cost him something too, on some scoreboard Oliver didn't know about yet. The other fighter waited a beat longer than he needed to — baiting him, or just measuring him — before closing the distance first. His movement now was purely animal, no visible calculation, and what followed lasted less than two seconds: a pivot, a point of contact that shouldn't have held that much weight, and the military candidate on the ground again — this time not getting up, one arm bent at an angle no arm bends naturally.

The sound arrived an instant after the image did. Dry. Wet. The scream cut in half when the body itself decided that screaming would hurt more than staying silent.

Someone in the gallery held their breath. Oliver didn't know who.

The winner stood over him for a moment — not triumphant, not cruel, simply motionless, as if waiting for his own body to decide what to do next. When he finally straightened all the way up, Oliver saw him clearly for the first time: hair cut almost to the scalp, nothing an instructor could fault at inspection, and an expression that hadn't changed once through the whole fight — not when the first attempt failed, not when he won the second, not now, with someone broken at his feet. The same hard, fixed look of someone processing what's in front of him as information and nothing else.

And then, in that motion — the shoulders drawing back, the posture returning to something recognizably human — Oliver felt the Registry stir before he could stop it.

It wasn't a full read. It was a whip-crack, half a second of contact with something that had no shape matching any known structure — not Strength, not Density, none of the signatures Oliver had spent months cataloging. Something that changed pattern every time he tried to pin it down, as if the Domain itself refused to hold still long enough to be read.

Oliver stepped back from the railing without deciding to. The instructor who'd guided them didn't turn to look at him, but said, to no one in particular:

"That one, not yet. You'll understand that one when it's your turn."

Below, two medics were already entering the pit to see to the fallen candidate. The winner still stood where he'd finished, looking at something on the ground Oliver couldn't make out from the gallery, and for an instant, before the instructor moved them along, his eyes lifted straight to the glass — to the exact spot where Oliver stood, as if he'd felt the Registry's read as clearly as Oliver had felt his Domain.

The instructor was already moving toward the next corridor. Nobody in the group stayed behind to check whether what Oliver had just imagined was real.

They walked the rest of the way in silence. Dara broke it, well clear of the gallery now, her voice lower than usual.

"Was that training?"

"I don't know what that was," Pier said, looking straight ahead. "But someone was keeping score of something. That much I saw."

"Looked to me like nobody was going to stop it even if it needed stopping," Riku said. His hands were shoved in his pockets, shoulders hunched in a way that had nothing to do with the cold of the tunnel.

Nobody corrected that. Asha walked a step behind the rest, her eyes fixed on some neutral point on the floor, and when Oliver glanced at her sideways he noticed her palms were closed into fists for the first time since he'd known her — nothing dramatic, just a small change he filed away without mentioning it.

The corridor ended in a windowless waiting room, metal chairs lined against the wall, another instructor with another tablet already standing by the door, ready to start reading names again. A map of the complex covered the far wall, no legend, most of the zones marked only with numbers — 4, 4A, 4B, a dozen subdivisions within what looked like a single level out of the many the mountain hid. Oliver counted eight marked levels before someone told him where to sit, and suspected those weren't all of them.

Oliver sat where he was told. He put his hand to his jacket's outer pocket, checked that the note was still there, and didn't take it out.

The question he'd been asking himself since the road still had no answer. The only thing that had changed was that it now had company: if choosing to stay had really been a choice, then what he'd just seen in the pit — someone switching fighting styles the way another person switches languages, no transition, no visible effort — had also been a choice someone made, at some point. Oliver didn't know yet whether that made it less frightening or more.
