# Chapter 53 — What People Carry

Rosaura left that same night with the promise that someone would come back for her in a couple of days, and Park spent the next forty-eight hours understanding that in Yaracuy, that promise meant something different than it meant in any other country he'd worked in. There was no operations room waiting for him, no file to review behind a closed door. There were houses. Lines. People.

---

The first day began before it was fully light, in Yusmary's kitchen, with a bucket half-filled under a tap running slower than any pipe should allow.

"It comes every three days, if that," she said, not looking at him, nudging the bucket with her foot while her hands kept peeling something. "When it comes, you fill everything that can be filled, even the pot you were using to cook. You learn not to trust that tomorrow's going to look like today."

Park stood in the doorway, not quite sure what to do with his hands, until Yusmary put a second bucket in them without asking if he wanted to help. It wasn't a task an AMAT agent had any training to solve faster than anyone else in that kitchen — that, Park understood as he held the weight of the water against his hip, was exactly the point. Being what he was counted for nothing here. Being one more pair of hands counted for something.

The gas line, later that morning, taught him a different version of the same lesson. Whole blocks of cars parked with their engines off to save what little fuel they had, people sleeping in shifts inside their own vehicles, a man selling coffee from a thermos between the lines as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world — because, Park understood, there it was. No one around him talked about it like a crisis. They talked about it like the weather. Something that was simply there, every day, that no one wasted breath being outraged about anymore.

"You can tell you're not from here," a man in line told him, without malice, nodding his chin at the way Park had spent half an hour standing instead of sitting down to wait like everyone else. "You learn fast to sit down here. What you can't rush, you don't rush standing up."

Park sat on the edge of the curb, and waited, and said nothing for the rest of the time about how long he'd been in the country or what he'd come to find.

---

He spent the afternoon making the rounds, with Yusmary as his guide, of three different houses in the network of mothers Nélida had been weaving together for months. He didn't go to ask about the missing that afternoon. He went, by Yusmary's instructions, "so people could see him up close before trusting him with anything else."

At the first house, a woman named Marisela served him coffee in a glass with a chipped rim and told him, without Park asking, how she'd lost the land where she used to keep her chickens — a local collective had "bought" it from her at a price nobody argued twice, and now that same land had a hand-painted sign with a name everyone in the area knew better than to say out loud near certain men.

"They didn't kill me, didn't burn my house down," Marisela said, with the dry calm of someone who's told that story too many times for it to hurt the same way anymore. "They just told me a price, and I understood the other price, the one for saying no, wasn't one I wanted to find out."

At the second house, a middle-aged man who introduced himself as a teacher — of mathematics, he clarified, as if the detail still mattered to him — explained, while fixing someone else's motorcycle engine on the side, that he taught in the mornings and repaired whatever needed repairing in the afternoons, and that between the two he barely made what he used to make with just the one.

"You don't choose to stop being what you studied to be," he said, without looking up from the engine. "You only choose to keep eating."

At the third house, a young neighbor with two small children playing at her feet told him about the latest "vaccine" she'd been charged at a checkpoint for carrying resale goods in the back of her car — a word, Park understood, that meant nothing medical here. It meant the toll a uniform charged anyone who had something to lose by not paying it.

"You pay and you move on," she said, shrugging with an ease that Park found harder to stomach than any story of direct violence he'd heard in the country. "It's cheaper than arguing."

None of the three stories had anything to do with Roa, with Sorte, or with anything Park could put in an operational report. Yusmary told him so plainly, on the way back, when he asked.

"I didn't bring you here to investigate anything," she said. "I brought you here to understand what's happening underneath what you came to investigate. Your mission is a symptom, agent. Not the disease."

---

Night fell without the power coming back, and the neighborhood reorganized itself around that fact in a way Park hadn't seen in any other country across the whole run of missions he'd been carrying. There were no new complaints, no panic. There were candles. Flashlights running on half-charged batteries. And, at the door of a neighbor everyone simply called "old Ovidio," a dominoes table already set up under a canvas awning, tiles clicking against the wood every few seconds and music drifting in, distorted by distance, from a radio in some house two streets over.

Yusmary took him there without asking if he wanted to go.

"Life happens here too," she said. "Not everything in this country is lines and checkpoints. If that's all you saw, you'd leave with half the picture."

Bottles were making the rounds, slow and sparing, more to keep the conversation company than to get anyone drunk. Ovidio, seated at the center of the table with the tiles already dealt, played like a man who'd spent sixty years winning that same game against the same three neighbors.

Park sat on the edge of the porch, a little apart, and that was where he saw her.

A girl, barely past her teens, sitting on a nearby step with her back against a doorframe, brown skin shining with sweat under a candle's light, curly hair half-tied back with what looked like an old elastic band. She wore a one-piece dress, faded from too many washings, the kind of garment Park recognized, without anyone explaining it to him, as probably the only one she had for a night like this. Her flip-flops had been mended at the strap with thick thread, more times than Park cared to count. She wasn't doing anything in particular — watching the dominoes, laughing quietly at something Ovidio said, fanning herself with her free hand against a heat the night hadn't managed to take off the edge of.

Park caught himself imagining, for one whole second he hadn't asked for and didn't expect, what it would be like to build an entire life here — not passing through, not on assignment, but for real, with that same candle, that same gas line every week, that same dress washed until it lost its color because there was no other one to wear. The thought didn't last longer than that second, but it left something behind that felt like anger — not at anyone in particular at that table, but at the exact distance between what he could do with his hands and how little of any of this those same hands could actually fix. He could bend a rifle, tear a whole tree off a road, carry the weight of a man turned into something else across a bridge. None of that got that girl a different life than the one she had. He'd always believed, the way his whole family believed, that strength was precisely what let you change the outcome of something — but if what he had couldn't change this, then what did having strength actually mean?

"Like the view?" Yusmary asked, sitting down beside him without his having heard her approach, her voice low but without any real accusation in it.

"That's not it," Park said, and it cost him a moment to find the rest of the sentence. "It's that I've spent all afternoon watching what people here have to carry just to stay on their feet, and for a second I thought about what it would be like to carry it myself. I didn't like how little I came up with that I could do about it, if it were my load instead of someone else's."

Yusmary said nothing for a while, letting the sound of the domino tiles fill the space where an answer might have gone.

"That," she said, finally, "is more than most people who come from outside ever get to feel in an entire trip."

---

The conversation at the table drifted, the way a night without electricity and with enough rum in it tends to drift, toward the stories nobody tells in daylight.

"What I don't do is go out past nine along the river road," one of the players said, without looking up from his tiles. "That's where you hear it, if you're unlucky. The crack of the whip, the whistle dropping in pitch the closer it gets. Whistle far off, you're fine. Whistle close, you run."

"El Silbón," Yusmary explained to Park, low, seeing the face of someone who didn't know the name. "Supposedly carries his father's bones in a sack, punished for something he did centuries ago. You're afraid of him from childhood without anyone ever quite having to explain why."

"And La Llorona's been walking the dry ravine since it actually dried up, with the drought this year," a woman added from across the table, crossing herself almost without thinking about it. "My grandmother used to say she cries because she lost her children with her own hands, and that she's still looking for others to take in their place. I don't let my boys out alone at night for anything, and it's not only because of the gangs."

"And don't forget El Mocho," Ovidio said, not looking up from the dominoes, setting down a tile with a sharp knock against the wood. "That one scares me more than the other two put together, because he doesn't stay far off. He stays close, in the same hills we all know, and they say he takes people who wander too far from where they should be."

Someone, further down the table, laughed under his breath — not at the story, but at how seriously everyone else was taking it, and that was when the woman who'd crossed herself earlier raised her voice a little, with the kind of firmness only people who truly believe what they're saying carry.

"Laugh if you want, but I rebuke those things in the name of Jesus every time they get mentioned, and you should too," she said, looking at the man who'd laughed. "I cover myself with the blood of Christ every night before bed, every single night, since all this started getting worse. That's the thing that has real power. The rest of it" — she made a vague gesture toward the dark beyond the awning, toward the hills none of them named in the direction of Sorte — "the rest of it is what's left when people stop covering themselves with what actually works."

No one argued with that out loud. Ovidio set down another tile, and the game went on as if the sentence had simply hung in the air with no one needing to answer it, while Park, quiet at the edge of the porch, thought that this woman, without knowing it, had just described, more precisely than any AMAT report, the exact difference between what he'd seen glowing in Casimira's house and whatever it was that breathed, slowly, somewhere on that mountain.

---

Casimira showed up the next afternoon, without anyone having gone to fetch her, and sat across from Park on the same porch at Nélida's where it had all started, an unlit cigar between her fingers for the first time since he'd known her.

"I prayed all night for a different answer," she said, without preamble. "I didn't get one. So I'm going to take you."

"What kind of answer were you hoping for?" Park asked.

"That there was another way up that mountain that didn't run through me," Casimira said. "I've spent forty years serving those courts, agent. I know every path, every clearing, every spot where the mountain turns strange without anyone else noticing. If I take you, and something goes wrong, it'll be because I took you. If I don't, and something goes wrong anyway, it'll be because I kept what I knew and did nothing with it. There's no version of this where I come out clean."

"You don't have to come," Park said. "I can find the way without you, given time."

"You don't have time, and you know it," Casimira said. "And even if you did, I'd rather be the one who takes you up there knowing what's waiting, than let you walk in thinking it's some abandoned coffee farm with a few armed men inside. That thing up there breathes, agent. You told me so yourself. I'd rather you know what you're walking into before you walk into it, even if I'm the one who has to tell you."

She sat for a moment looking at the unlit cigar, turning it between her fingers.

"Last night, at Ovidio's table, someone mentioned El Silbón, La Llorona, El Mocho," Park said. "And someone else rebuked them in the name of Jesus, as if they carried the same weight as whatever's on that mountain."

"And she's right to give them that respect, each in its own way," Casimira said. "But those are old stories, agent, told to explain the hills at night and to keep children from wandering off alone. What's in Sorte, what's forming up there, isn't an old story. It's something new, made by force, in less time than any old story ever needed to become true. That's what scares me most about all of this. It didn't even get the decent amount of time it takes to become a legend before it turned into something else."

She said nothing more. She lit the cigar, finally, and sat looking toward the exact point on the horizon where the mountain began to cut its shape against a sky that still had two more nights to go before it trembled again.
