# Chapter 114 — Free Fall

---

The panic arrived before anything else — before the cold, before the wind roaring whole in his ears, before his brain even finished processing that there was no longer any ground beneath his feet. All of Milan stretched out below, lights and rooftops and a grid of streets closing in too fast to be real.

Conti fell beside him, or something like falling — his cassock barely stirring, his hands crossed behind his back just like in the warehouse, the same exact calm of a man waiting for a bus.

Seeing that, somehow, did more for Oliver than anything he could have told himself. If the priest wasn't in a hurry, there was still time. Not much. But something.

"Why me?" he managed to say, his voice torn apart by the wind. "There were five others down there."

"Because I've spent the whole week watching you hunt alone, and tonight I finally got to see how," Conti said, no need to raise his voice above the wind, as if physics didn't quite apply to whatever came out of his mouth. "Four badges, in a quarter hour, from people who'd done nothing to you directly. You count well. You choose well who to hit first. But you haven't stopped stealing from whoever was already losing."

"They were hunting the weak ones first."

"And you took what they'd hunted, instead of letting them go." Conti looked at him a moment, no real accusation in his voice. "Milan would say the same thing about his own people, if you asked him. That he hunts too, so there's enough to go around. For his people, not just for himself."

"It's not the same."

"No," Conti conceded. "But you've spent the whole week using the same math that disgusted you in him, without calling it by its name."

"I'm not doing it for myself."

"I know." Conti looked at him a moment, the whole fall unfolding beneath the two of them without either seeming to fully notice it. "That's precisely the problem you haven't finished seeing yet."

Oliver didn't have a ready answer, and he didn't need one — Conti wasn't waiting for one anymore.

"Count whatever you like," the priest said, his voice as calm as if they were sitting in the warehouse instead of falling toward the asphalt of an entire city. "But ask yourself, when you land, whether the number you're missing is the one you think it is."

He gave no time for a reply. A step to the side, the same gesture as always, and he stopped being there.

Oliver fell alone.

The panic came back all at once, rawer than before — Conti's borrowed calm gone with him, the city still closing in, now with no one beside him to suggest there was time to spare. He tried thinking of Anchor, of something to grab onto, and there was nothing at that height, not a surface, not a fixed point. He tried thinking of Swap, and there was no one to trade places with, no object of comparable mass in sight that would do him any real good.

It was the memory of Park that arrived, without warning, handing him the answer.

The fall itself was that — not a fixed force, but something building up every second, faster with each instant. If he could absorb it the same way he absorbed any other impact, not all at once at the end but now, as it grew, he could keep part of it for himself before the ground took all of it.

He started doing it slowly, with nothing solid to touch yet — taking the extra speed he gained with every meter, storing it instead of letting it all add up. He didn't stop the fall. But he made it, little by little, a little less lethal than it would have been.

A facade met him sooner than he expected — a narrow alley, more luck than aim, old brick walls on either side. He hit the first one sideways, Momentum releasing all at once to redirect the impact instead of absorbing it whole, and the spin that generated threw him into a rusted fire escape a floor below, the metal giving way under his weight with a long screech, every rung shaving off a little more of the speed that would have killed him on the first one.

He ended up on the ground, between the base of the fire escape and a dumpster he didn't recognize from any street he'd hunted before, his breathing in tatters, a pain spread across his entire left side that was going to take days to explain to anyone.

Nothing elegant. Not even close.

But lying flat on his back in an alley of a city he could no longer name, his lungs on fire, his heart still trying to catch up with his body, Oliver understood, with an almost offensive clarity, that it could have been much, much worse.

---

It took him almost an hour to limp to the hideout he'd been using for two nights — a gap between the frame of a billboard and the low wall of a rooftop, hidden enough to sleep without anyone noticing from the street.

Dax was already there.

Oliver went still for a moment, the surprise taking longer than usual to reach him through the accumulated exhaustion. Dax looked him up and down, once, no urgency at all in the gesture — the torn clothes, the side, the careful way Oliver was still breathing — then went back to whatever he'd been watching in the distance, as if the information were already filed away and needed no comment.

Several minutes passed like that, each of them on his own side of the wall, before Oliver found his voice.

"You're alone?" he asked.

"Yes."

Nothing more. Oliver waited, and when it became clear nothing more was coming without pushing, he pushed.

"Why?"

Dax took his time answering, his eyes still fixed on something Oliver couldn't make out among the rooftops.

"Same reason as you, I'd guess," he said, finally. "Though I don't know if it's the exact same reason."

"Is that a problem?"

"No." Dax shrugged, a minimal gesture. "It's strange. You people always moved together. You more than anyone."

It didn't sound like a reproach. It sounded like someone noting a fact that didn't quite fit the pattern he kept stored away.

Oliver looked at him a moment longer than necessary. Dax had been wearing the same clothes since the first day, with no one to split watch shifts with, no one to guard his back while he slept. The solitude didn't seem to weigh on him the way it would have weighed on Oliver — or maybe it did, and it simply wasn't something Dax let show.

"Why haven't you attacked me yet?" Oliver finally asked, the question that had been circling him since he'd recognized the shape.

"I haven't needed to attack anyone in days," Dax said, nothing like pride in his voice, just the bare fact. "I've kept myself visible. No one's dared try twice, and the first time, I haven't lost a single exchange."

"You want a rematch?"

It was the first time in the whole conversation that something like interest crossed Dax's face.

Oliver got up, every muscle in his side protesting at once, and readied himself anyway — not because his body was ready, but because saying no in this state felt worse than the pain.

Dax shook his head.

"I'm not fighting you like this."

"What?"

"Another day." Dax looked back at the rooftops, closing the subject. "There's no point beating you half-dead from a fall. Take first watch if you want, or sleep. I've got it."

Oliver didn't fully understand what had just happened, but his body, relieved, decided for him before his head finished arguing about it.

"I'm not touching your badges," Dax added, unprompted, his eyes still fixed on the distance. "They're in your backpack. They stay there."

Twenty-one. Oliver counted, already half-asleep against the wall, everything gathered since the first night through the four stolen hours earlier at Scalo Farini — twenty-one badges, a number that days earlier had seemed nearly impossible to reach alone, and that now weighed less than the exhaustion sitting on top of him.

---

Eighteen. That was the number Asha had had written down since the night before, and it hadn't changed all morning.

Not for lack of trying. They'd followed Pier's plan to the letter since Oliver jumped out the window — abandoning the original idea of chasing the route Asha had seen Johanna's group take, and instead staying still, discreet, gathering information about who moved where instead of risking their necks hunting live. It had worked halfway. Eighteen badges among the five of them wasn't much, but they hadn't lost any either, and they knew, better than almost anyone else in the exam, where every group still standing slept.

It was the sixth day. They gathered that morning around the device, as they did every morning, and this time the screen took longer than usual to settle on a number.

"Seventy-one," Nadia said, when it finally stabilized. "Fewer than we calculated."

No one said anything for a moment. Seventy-one meant the drop over the last two days had been more brutal than any of them had directly witnessed — people vanishing from the list in silence, without Valais having seen even a fraction of those falls.

"Look at the positions," Asha said, sliding her finger across the screen.

Dax was still at number one, untouchable. Johanna had climbed to sixth, her whole group holding almost intact on the board since the last time they'd checked. Of the fight club that had spent the whole week hunting in a pack, almost nothing was left — Milan out, the ones Oliver had run into at Scalo Farini out too, though they didn't know the details yet, almost all of them out except Yegor, who still hung on at a low rank, unharmed somehow in the middle of everything that had happened to his own group.

"And us?" Riku asked.

The numbers came in one by one, none spectacular, all sustainable — the price of having played it safe all week.

It was Dara who found Oliver's name, and who stayed staring at the screen long enough that the others came closer to see what she'd found.

"That can't be right," she said.

Four. Oliver Webb, alone, with no one covering his back all week, sat at rank four of seventy-one — ahead of all of them combined, ahead of every other group except Johanna's and Dax himself.

It was right.

"Then we do what we've been avoiding all week," Asha said, once the silence had started weighing more than the number itself.

No one asked what she meant. Everyone knew which group she was talking about — twelve people sleeping together, with nothing like a one-roof-per-pair rule dividing them, settled for two full days now in an underground parking garage near Sant'Ambrogio. Reber had said it the first day: no one would disqualify them for breaking that rule, but twelve bodies under the same roof left a trail no individual discipline could fully erase — they'd traded discretion for strength of numbers, and that trade was exactly what made them findable. It was also a building Valais happened to know better than they did: Pier had marked it in his notebook the first night, back when they were still looking for somewhere to sleep themselves, and had ruled it out as too big to stay unnoticed in.

"Twelve is a lot," Riku said.

"Twelve in a fixed spot, with routines, is a lot less than twelve on the move," Asha said. "We whittle them down before we go in. Gradual. Every time someone leaves alone or in pairs, they go down. By nightfall, we go in for whatever's left."

It worked better than anyone expected. Pier and Petra — no, Pier alone, Asha corrected herself in her head, the habit of naming them together not yet fully worn off — intercepted two who went out for supplies mid-morning, clean, no noise. Riku and Nadia took down another one standing watch at the exit ramp — a loaded hit straight to the side, the charge driven into the point of contact before the man could even finish turning around. Asha and Dara waited almost four hours for a fourth to get far enough from the main entrance not to be heard, and when he finally did, there was no need to fight — Dara already had a Double waiting for him on the other side of the alley before he even knew there were two paths, not one.

Four out before the afternoon fell, though only two of those four gave up their badges before crawling off toward streets Valais didn't bother following — no need to risk anything finishing off someone already limping away, badge or no badge. Eight remained inside when the sixth night finally closed over the city.

---

The parking garage stretched three levels under the street, concrete columns every few meters, cars abandoned by their real owners weeks earlier covering much of the second level, emergency lighting flickering in irregular stretches where the system had gone too long without maintenance.

They came in through the pedestrian ramp, the five of them together for the first time in days.

Riku extended the field before anyone took a step past the ramp — not to hit anything yet, only to feel. Eight signatures spread among the columns and abandoned cars, none of them fully awake, a complete map of the second level no human eye could have pieced together in that same darkness.

The first to react was a heavyset man asleep against a pillar — awake all at once, both hands opening toward the ground, and the concrete itself under his feet started to vibrate, a low note climbing the columns and rattling the windows of nearby cars. Echo, Riku understood, almost by instinct — it didn't destroy anything directly, but the vibration disoriented, threw off the balance of anyone who didn't know where it was coming from.

Riku didn't give the note time to finish building. He didn't release anything into the air — he hadn't fought like this in months, not since the one time it had actually done him any good. He loaded Static into the sole of his foot and took the first step, the push against the concrete itself timed to the exact instant of contact, the same trick with the floor's physics he'd once been forced to learn. He closed the distance faster than his body alone would have allowed, and when the fist connected, it wasn't only the fist — the loaded hit drove straight into the point of contact, the same principle, applied by him now instead of suffered.

The man doubled over but didn't fully go down — he held the vibration with a stubbornness Riku hadn't expected, hands still open toward the floor, trying to rebuild the interrupted note. Riku didn't give him the second he needed. He let his own counterattack arrive — a thick forearm going for his throat — and instead of dodging it, he took it with an open palm, closing his fingers around the exact point of contact before the grip could finish forming. Active Reception: the charge traveled down the same channel the man had just opened himself, and this time there was no stubbornness left to hold anything up. He dropped entirely, the vibration dying at once, his own body's pulse cut off before it could finish organizing itself.

Two more came out from among the cars at the same time — a woman with her palms glowing a white light too intense to look at directly, and a third running at Asha with something loaded in his fists, Focus concentrated and direct. Asha didn't back off. She reached her hand toward the tension cables holding up one of the hanging light fixtures overhead, and squeezed them from within, without touching them — the whole cable snapped with a dry crack, the entire fixture dropping right into the third man's path, forcing him to break off the attack or be crushed.

The woman's light dimmed by half when Dara entered the exchange.

Not one Dara. Three.

The first took the full flash, sacrificing herself with no words needed; the second closed the distance while the woman was still blinking, dazzled by her own light bouncing off the concrete; the third, and the real Dara behind all three, coordinated a pincer move no single woman could have read in time. She went down between the three of them, none of the copies needing more than one hit each.

"I've never seen you do that," Riku said, breathless, not looking away from the chaos he himself was still holding together.

"Neither have I," Dara said, and it didn't sound like a joke.

The Focus one recovered his balance and charged again, this time at Nadia, who up to then had held the rear. She didn't need to close in — Impulse in a straight line, right to the chest, and the man flew back into an abandoned van, the sheet metal caving under the impact with a crash that echoed through all three levels.

Something different arrived from deeper inside the garage — the concrete starting to crack in fine lines around a man kneeling ten meters from Pier, concentrating something directly into the slab under his own knees, not with Milan's brute force but something slower, more methodical. Pier let him finish the job. Right as the slab gave way under the man's own effort, Pier weighted the whole area at once, the mass multiplying over the freshly opened gap, and the man sank to the knee in his own handiwork before understanding the floor was no longer his to control.

They went down, one by one, none of the five needing to shout orders — years of training together speaking for them. When only three were left standing, someone shouted something in a language none of Valais recognized, and the three remaining ran for the nearest exit ramp, abandoning the fallen without looking back.

No one chased them. There was no need.

They collected the badges from those left on the ground, one by one, unhurried now that the noise had mostly died down. Four, they counted among the garage's fallen — the one with the cracked concrete had lost his under the rubble of the very floor he'd tried to break, unrecoverable without half an hour of clearing debris none of the five had any interest in touching. Even so, it was the best single haul Valais had pulled off all week, added to the two from the afternoon.

"Twenty-four," Nadia said, keeping the tally as always. "Between the five of us."

No one celebrated much. But for the first time in days, the silence that followed didn't weigh like defeat.
