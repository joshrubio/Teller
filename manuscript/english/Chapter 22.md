# Chapter 22 — First Contact

The sector two team was three people.

They'd gone out at 02:15. Seo up front, Kim and Bak behind, three meters of spacing between each — the standard distance for dense vegetation in the dark. Seo's reader marked a slightly elevated base density, which was the usual baseline for that zone. Twelve weeks on the island had taught her to tell the normal weight of sector two apart from the weight of something active. Tonight was normal weight. They advanced.

Twenty minutes in, Seo raised her fist.

The gwisin was in the clearing, about twelve meters off. Standing, still. Seo had been seeing gwisin in Korea since she was nineteen and had spent twelve weeks on this specific island, and there was still something about the ones in sector two that took her a second to absorb: the face was too much of a face. Not an approximation of a human face — the features of an actual person, the specific shape of the nose, the lines around the eyes, the particular weight of the jaw. It wore clothes from the fifties, dark wool fabric, and the clothes were also too much clothes: the folds fell where folds fall, the hem had the fraying of use. The first time Seo had seen one in this zone, it had taken her three seconds to activate the protocol because her brain was trying to find the person behind the shape. There was no person. That was exactly the problem.

The gwisin didn't move. It watched.

Seo pointed at Kim, who had the containment unit. Standard process: Kim advances, deploys the field, Bak covers the flanks, Seo controls the distance. They'd done this dozens of times. Kim opened the unit and the field began to extend —

The second one appeared to the left.

Fifteen meters, between two trees. The same fifties clothing, a different face — this one a man's, older, hands hanging at his sides. It wasn't looking at the first gwisin. It was looking at them.

Bak saw it while Seo was still counting. Sector two: territory of individual gwisin, a known presence pattern. Two in proximity was rare but not unheard of if they shared a zone of origin. Seo was about to give the signal to continue with the first one when the reader beeped.

The third one was behind them. Thirty meters. Between the team and the way back.

There was no signal between the three. They hadn't interacted — no visible communication, no movement of mutual approach. They were positioned at angles. Seo took exactly as long as it took to process the angles to understand this wasn't a coincidence of territory. She didn't know what it was. She knew she had to get out of that geometry.

"First one," she said, and Kim finished the containment of the first gwisin before the other two had finished repositioning.

The unit closed. Reading on the screen: Level 3.

The sector marked Level 2.

The other two moved. Not fast — a gwisin's movement was that of something that shifts position without using legs to shift position, presence changing place more than body advancing. But the direction was toward them, and the angles Seo had wanted to break were closing anyway. Bak put his body between Seo and the closer one — the older man, the one with the hanging hands — and the gwisin passed through the space Bak occupied without stopping.

It passed through the space Bak occupied.

It wasn't a blow. Seo had no words for what it was, and twelve weeks later she still didn't. The gwisin's Odem field, crossing through the area where Bak stood, did something to the volume of that space — compressed it or folded it or replaced the air with something Bak's body didn't know how to handle. Bak didn't fall. Bak stopped being oriented. He kept standing, kept breathing, but for the next several seconds he didn't know which way was up. In the dark with trees three meters off in every direction, that was enough.

"Extraction," Seo said.

Kim grabbed Bak. Seo covered the retreat, reader in one hand and the modified weapon in the other, though she knew the weapon wasn't going to do anything useful if the older man's gwisin decided to come back. It didn't — it withdrew as they started moving, the usual territorial behavior. The third one, the one blocking the way back, didn't move until they were five meters off, and then shifted aside with no apparent logic. Or with a logic Seo didn't have the category to read yet.

They got out.

Bak regained his orientation before reaching the perimeter but walked with a hand on the tree trunks, touching surfaces as if he needed to confirm the world had the right dimensions. Kim carried the containment unit. Seo noted the weight — more than it should have been for a Level 2 fragment. She noted it and wrote it in the notebook even though she already had it in the reader, because it was the kind of number she wanted logged twice.

They reached the perimeter as the sky began shifting from black to blue.

---

Park hadn't heard the team arrive. What he heard was the shift in tone of voices he couldn't make out from the cabin — not alarm, the lower register of something that hadn't gone as it should. He came out before there was enough light to not need a flashlight.

Ji-ho was already at the operations center.

Not freshly woken — he'd been standing for a while, or he hadn't slept, or he slept the way some field agents learn to sleep: available. He stood by the reading table, the sector two containment unit in front of him. He wasn't looking at it. He was looking at the agent who'd brought it in.

"Sector two was flagged," he said. Not loud. In the register of someone who's already said the same thing enough times that it no longer needs volume.

"The night reading showed stable level two," the agent said, the team's second, not Seo. "Protocol authorizes a sweep at stable level two."

"Sector two doesn't have stable level two. It has level two on the readers, which isn't the same thing."

Silence. The agent didn't answer because there was no answer that would add anything useful.

Ji-ho looked at the unit. Opened it. Park, from where he stood, couldn't see the contents — only the reading on the side screen. Level 3, partial fragment. Ji-ho closed the unit.

"Where's Seo?"

"Medical review."

"The other one."

"Same."

Ji-ho nodded once. The kind of nod that doesn't indicate agreement, just files the information and moves on. He crossed the tent without another word and went out the other side.

He came back twenty minutes later with the maps.

Not the same maps from the briefing — these had annotations over annotations, the palimpsest of previous rotations corrected and re-corrected. Ji-ho spread them on the central table and started working in silence. He didn't call anyone in. The agents who needed to be there kept appearing on their own over the next forty minutes, as if there were a frequency Park wasn't tuned to.

What Ji-ho did with the maps wasn't reorganizing the operation in any formal sense — there were no orders, no assignment table. It was more like reading a text that had layers and marking what each layer said. He pointed to zones, traced alternate routes, erased an earlier annotation and wrote another over it. The agents who came up looked where he pointed, nodded, left. The whole process took two hours, and at the end there was a different map than the one that had existed before, without anyone having held a meeting.

Park watched from the edge for a while. He didn't come closer. What he was seeing wasn't a process that improved with questions — it was someone who'd spent enough time on this kind of terrain that the map was a translation of something he already knew, not the source of the information.

At some point Ji-ho looked up and found him.

"The northeast sector," he said. "Second evaluation zone. Don't go in until the reader marks first."

He didn't wait for an answer. Looked back down at the map.

Park filed the instruction and didn't ask why.

He walked up to the table. The containment unit was still there. He looked at it without opening it — the side screen was still lit, reading stable at Level 3 for what should have been Level 2.

"Are you going to open it?"

Kiki was behind him, phone in hand but not looking at it. She'd arrived without Park hearing when.

"No."

"I did open it." A pause. "It's got too much face."

Park looked at her.

"How defined?"

"Like you know who it was. You don't, but you feel like you should." Kiki looked at the unit for a moment. "The ones in sector two have been like this since I got here. I asked Lee what goes in the report when they have more form than the form accounts for, and he said it says 'high legibility, see appendix.' I asked what goes in the appendix. He said he leaves it blank."

Park filed that away. Sector two with a stable Level 2 reading and Level 3 gwisin. Forms with blank appendices. Ji-ho marking the sector off-limits and the team going in anyway because the protocol didn't account for the distinction between what the reader said and what the terrain was.

None of this was new to the island. It was what had been going without the right box to check for seventy years.

---

The pre-operation medical checkup was at nine. The noon operation hadn't been cancelled — reorganized. Ji-ho had spent two hours in the control center with the maps and had redistributed the sectors without formally calling anyone together; the agents who needed to know found out because someone told them, and that was that.

The field doctor was a man of about forty who worked in silence. He checked Park's left arm — range of motion, resistance, temperature of the recovering area — and didn't say what he thought of what he found, which was its own way of saying it.

The Class-R came in rolls. Fabric compressed in dark indigo — nearly black in shadow, a deep blue when light crossed it head-on — with a weight that didn't match its volume. The doctor cut specific lengths without measuring.

"Used this before?" he said.

"No."

"Left arm and right leg is the standard for your Domain profile. The material activates with body heat — in thirty seconds you're going to feel like the joint has more support than it should have from the outside. That's correct. Don't take it off in the field even if it feels like you don't need it."

He applied it without further explanation. The arm first: from wrist to elbow, two layers, steady pressure. The first twenty seconds were texture and neutral temperature. Then the Odem in the material activated — the edges of the wrap turned slightly translucent, as if the material and the skin were deciding where one ended and the other began. Through the translucent edge you could see, briefly, the muscle structure underneath — the radius, the tendons, the tissue that had been compensating for weeks. One second, two. Then the translucence settled into something more opaque and the arm was left with the support coming from inside, not outside.

Park looked at it for a moment.

"Normal," the doctor said, without looking up.

The right leg, from ankle to mid-thigh. The same process — the translucent edges, the visible structure for two seconds, the settling.

Park came out of the checkup in sleeveless tactical gear — the dark indigo of the Class-R visible on his left arm, uncovered. Crossing the central area toward the rally point, he noticed most of the unit's agents wore the same material on some limb, at different degrees of activation depending on how long they'd had it on: the ones who'd equipped earlier had fully opaque, settled edges. The recently applied ones were still translucent at the rim.

Kiki was waiting for him at the rally point with the gear. She wore the Class-R on both arms, both of them — not standard, the doctor would have given her the same argument about Domain profile. Kiki wouldn't have changed her mind because the doctor asked her to.

"How's the arm?" she said.

"Fine."

"How fine?"

"Like nothing happened."

Kiki considered that a second.

"Don't get used to it," she said. "The material works with what you have, not instead of what you're missing. If the arm's at seventy percent, the Class-R gives you seventy with better support. Not a hundred."

Park hadn't thought it was anything else. But it was useful to know Kiki had thought about it enough to say so.

The unit formed up. Twelve operatives plus Park and Kiki. Ji-ho wasn't in the formation — he stood off to the side, the dagger in his left hand the way someone holds their keys: without deciding to. Supervision, not field.

Park looked at him for a second. Ji-ho didn't return the look — he was looking at the island map spread on a folding table, tracing something with a finger that left no mark.

The unit headed out toward the sector.

---

The interior vegetation of the island was dense in a way the maps didn't convey. It didn't block the path — there were trails from previous operations, ground trodden by decades of agent rotation, a network of paths that weren't marked but existed because the weight of hundreds of boots on the same soil over seventy years leaves a kind of imprint vegetation doesn't fully recover from. The trails were a body and a half wide. Enough to advance single file without branches brushing, not enough for more.

To the sides, where boots hadn't reached, the green was opaque. At ten meters, the vegetation absorbed the background and what lay beyond was guesswork. At twenty, nothing. Park had read that the interior hadn't been touched in decades except by the operations — no agriculture, no industry, nothing keeping the terrain open. Seventy years of vegetation that had grown around the one thing the island had: the operation, and what the operation contained.

The trail showed signs of recent use in some stretches — turned earth, a broken branch that hadn't fallen yet. At one point, a mark on a tree trunk at eye height: three horizontal lines in ink, the kind of signal that means something to whoever put it there and nothing to anyone who wasn't there. The unit passed it without anyone pointing it out.

Park walked in the second line. The agent at point carried the Odem reader — real-time readings, northeast sector, advance zone. Base density: high. AMAT-KR's reader marked on a scale AMAT-NA's wouldn't have had the gradations to represent. Park carried his own in his pocket but hadn't taken it out — the point agent's was enough, and duplicating the reading added no information.

Kiki was to his right, one step back. She didn't talk. She carried only field gear and the small case on her belt, her right hand near it without touching it. Park had noticed the case at the rally point. He hadn't asked. Kiki hadn't offered.

The rest of the unit's operatives moved with the specific silence of people who've done this enough times on this exact terrain to not need to talk. None of them looked to the sides more than necessary — not because there was nothing there, but because they knew what was there didn't move until it moved, and looking at it beforehand didn't change that.

Forty minutes of advance. The northeast sector had four zones marked on Ji-ho's redistributed map — two for standard clearing, one for evaluation, one with no label that the agent carrying the map had pointed to and kept without comment.

In the evaluation zone, the reader climbed.

Not a jump — a steady increase, the kind that indicates active presence at reduced distance. The point agent raised his fist. The unit stopped.

The silence of the vegetation. The island's background noise — insects, wind through leaves, the sea in the distance — stayed the same. What changed was the reader.

The point agent looked back. Looked at Park.

Then the shooting started up ahead.

Not at the point agent's position — further left, twenty meters, where the second advance line had been moving in parallel. Two shots. A pause. One more. The cadence of someone who's seen something and decided in the interval between shots.

The unit redistributed without a verbal order — training or field experience, the movement was coherent. Park moved toward the sound.

What he found in the clearing wasn't what the reader had predicted.
