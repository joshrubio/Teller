# Chapter 105 — Dead Weight, Again

---

Riku had spent several days counting the hours Oliver slept without telling anyone, not even himself entirely — a habit that had started without his meaning to, the same night he heard the door of room twelve open at past two in the morning and not close again until the sky was already lightening over the valley.

It wasn't just the sleep. It was everything else around the sleep. Oliver showed up late to meals or didn't show up at all, and when he did he ate fast, without conversation, his gaze fixed on a point that wasn't on the table. He had new bruises every few days, in places that didn't match any scheduled exercise — knuckles, forearm, once the jaw, as if he were fighting something only he could see. And when someone spoke to him, he answered with exactly enough courtesy to end the conversation sooner, not the kind that invited it to continue.

Riku knew what was happening, in the broad sense: Oliver trained at night, alone, something that had started the night of the evaluation with Maren and Anka. What he didn't know was how to name the other part — the one that had nothing to do with the Registry, or with any specific Domain, but with the face Oliver wore when he thought no one was looking. Riku recognized it without being able to fully describe it. It was the same face he himself had worn for weeks after the fight club, before anyone taught him to call it something else.

He had thought about telling someone else — Pier, Asha, even Maren — and each time he had stopped himself at the last moment, unable to find a sentence that didn't sound like an accusation or an exaggeration. "Oliver is training too much" didn't capture anything real. What was real was harder to say out loud: that Oliver had spent days now with the look of someone who had decided that resting was just one more form of waste, and that no one around him knew how to argue him out of it without sounding like they were asking him to fail all over again.

Once, crossing the east wing hallway at dawn on his way to the bathroom, Riku had seen him from a distance — a silhouette going down the stairs toward the secondary training rooms, barefoot, turning on no more light than he needed. He hadn't followed. He'd stayed in his own doorway, listening to the footsteps recede, feeling that following would have been an intrusion Oliver hadn't asked for and probably wouldn't have thanked him for.

---

The order arrived stuck to the door, same as always: dead lift, full repetition, the whole center at once.

The esplanade by the east wall, when they got there, was already fuller than Riku remembered from the first time — the artificial slope with the cones already set out, sandbags stacked in double rows, and candidates in gray uniforms stretching alongside those in regular training clothes, the same space shared without announcement between groups that normally crossed paths only at a distance. Riku recognized, without meaning to, Dax's broad back near the north end, and further off, the orderly line of Johanna's group, each stretching at their own pace but all finishing almost to the second.

"Same exercise as the first time," Reber said, not needing to raise his voice much for everyone to hear. "No Domain. Sprints, then sandbags, up and down, no announced limit. This time with a reference point: you'll know how many reps you completed the first time, and how many you complete today."

He gave no further instructions. No one needed them.

It started with the runs, same as always, each one shorter than the last but with less rest between them, until the rest stopped existing altogether. Then came the sandbags — pairs spread out along the whole east wall, going up and down the slope again and again, the boxed-in air of the compound with no intention of cooling anyone. Riku lost count of his own reps somewhere around the sixth, somewhere past the half-hour mark of continuous exercise, his legs turned into a weight that wasn't his own anymore, obeying only out of habit.

Oliver had placed himself at the front of the line without saying anything to anyone, his eyes sunken, his hands already shaking before he loaded the first bag. Riku, a little further back, felt the urge to say something to him — anything, some empty encouragement — and didn't, because something in Oliver's posture made it clear he didn't want words.

By the fourth rep, Riku had already lost visual track of him. Not because he'd finished, but because the rhythm at which he went up and down the slope didn't match the rest of Valais — none of the visible effort of the others, no stumbling, just the bag going up and down with a regularity that seemed to cost him less the more it should have been costing him. It matched, instead, the rhythm of the military candidates working the same slope a few meters over, and at some point past the hour mark, Riku lost sight of him completely among the gray uniforms, sweating at the same pace, indistinguishable except for his hair.

"Fifteen reps," someone said, passing the number quietly down the line when Oliver finally sat down, long after the rest of Valais. "Best of the whole exercise did sixteen."

"That one's yours, isn't he?" said a candidate from Dax's group Riku didn't know, tipping his chin toward where Oliver had collapsed, too breathless to speak. "Thought you were the ones who came in with no prior training."

"We are," Riku said, with nothing more to explain.

He stood watching Oliver, still flat on the ground, force himself up before anyone could offer to help, further along than anyone else in Valais and a single rep short of the best time in the whole exercise, and felt something that wasn't quite pride — closer to watching someone run toward a cliff at an impressive speed.

---

At the dining hall afterward, the Valais table was nearly full — Asha, Dara, Nadia, Riku — talking about what had happened since the big exercise, the weeks on top of it already starting to settle into something like routine again.

"I still can't believe the two Doubles thing," Nadia said, not for the first time that week, looking at Dara with something between admiration and honest envy. "I've spent months trying to hold the impulse inward and outward at once and I still can't fully control it."

"I don't believe it either," Dara said. "I still get a nosebleed if I think about it too hard."

"And how's the pending rematch coming?" Asha asked Riku, a hint of a smile that didn't quite land as mockery. "Noa's still out there, free, without you having returned the armlock."

"No rush," Riku said, though the idea had been circling him more than he'd admit. "Still working out how not to lose again."

Someone mentioned Petra's shoulder, whether she'd taken the sling off completely yet. No one had an updated answer. Someone else brought up the leadership contrast, Pier following the descent without stopping versus Johanna halting her whole team, whether that said anything real about which of the two was right or whether the two styles were simply different. No one reached a clean conclusion.

Pier wasn't there. Neither was Oliver.

"Has anyone seen Oliver today, besides the exercise?" Asha asked, at some point, not addressing anyone in particular.

No one had.

Riku didn't say what he'd been thinking through the whole meal — that Pier wasn't there either, and that the two absences probably weren't a coincidence.

---

He found Pier on the way to the secondary training room, the same one where a couple of weeks earlier Petra had let him see, for the first time, that Oliver wasn't starting from zero. The door was ajar. Pier stopped before going all the way in, and Riku, without anyone asking him to, stayed a step behind him, in the gap of the doorway, watching without being seen.

Oliver was in the center of the space, alone, moving in a way Riku had never seen from him — not the usual technical work, nor the calculated choreography of a real fight. Something closer to shadowboxing, punches thrown at the air in repeated sequences, but with something loaded inside each one, a weight that built and released in the same gesture, the air around the fist shifting in a way that wasn't only physical. Every three or four punches, his whole arm shuddered an instant after impact against nothing, as if the body had to absorb something the gesture alone couldn't fully release.

The bare bulb in the center threw a single long shadow, and Riku, from the doorway, realized he'd gone several seconds without breathing at all, hypnotized by the repetition — it wasn't elegant, it wasn't the kind of movement anyone would teach in a formal session, but it had the brutal honesty of something Oliver had spent days polishing alone, with no one there to correct his form.

"Oliver," Pier said, stepping all the way in.

Oliver didn't stop. He chained another sequence, sweat already soaking his shirt, his breathing working like an engine that had gone too long without shutting off.

"You haven't slept well in a week," Pier said. "Riku's worried. So am I."

"I'm fine."

"Riku hasn't said a word about it at the dining hall, if that makes you feel better," Pier added, with something that was almost humor, though it didn't quite land. "He's been keeping it to himself out of respect. I don't have that upbringing."

Oliver didn't respond to the comment. He chained another sequence.

"You're not."

Oliver stopped, finally, one full second, his gaze fixed on a spot on the wall that had nothing special about it. When he spoke again, his voice came out lower than usual.

"I have things people gave me that I let rot without realizing it," he said. "I don't know how long it's going to take me to fix that. I'm not going to waste one more minute than I've already wasted."

"That's not sustainable."

"Nothing I do is," Oliver said, and started the sequence again, the same loaded punch, again, and again.

Pier stayed a moment longer, without pressing, and left without having gained anything Riku could identify as a victory. In the hallway, already far from the door, he put a hand on Riku's shoulder, brief, wordless — the same kind of gesture Riku already knew, the one that meant someone had seen the same problem and didn't have the solution either.

"Give him time," was all Pier said, though it sounded more like hope than certainty.

---

The general summons came that same afternoon, all of St. Gotthard gathered in the main courtyard, something that hadn't happened since the first day. Reber spoke from an improvised platform, without a microphone, in the same voice that carried just as far regardless.

"Today marks the month," he said, without preamble. "This morning was one more exercise. Nothing special, despite what some of you seem to have decided to believe."

He paused, briefly, letting the sentence settle before going on.

"Tomorrow, free," he added. "The whole center. No one trains, no one evaluates anything. Rest, or don't, it's your decision, but use it for whatever you need before the day after tomorrow."

"What's the day after tomorrow?" someone asked, two rows ahead of Riku, their voice coming out sharper than they meant it to.

"The final evaluation," Reber said, without drama, like someone confirming a fact everyone already knew. "Real, in the field, supervised but real. The Board stops being a snapshot of how you train. It starts being a snapshot of who AMAT can count on for what comes after this. It's not the same thing."

Reber gave no details about what kind of evaluation, or what margin of error would be tolerated, or what happened to whoever didn't rank — he only made clear, through the very absence of those details, that the vagueness wasn't carelessness. It was the way the institution protected information until it was needed.

A different kind of silence moved through the courtyard — not the nervous silence of one more test, but something with real weight behind it, the sense that the countdown that had spent days being abstract had just become a concrete date, with a name and everything: the day after tomorrow. Riku glanced sideways at Oliver, two rows over, and saw that he was one of the few people in the whole courtyard who hadn't reacted at all — as if he'd already spent days operating under his own version, more urgent, of the same countdown.

"The Board updates this afternoon with the dead lift results," Reber added, before stepping down from the platform. "Look it over carefully. It's the last lab snapshot you're going to see."

---

The Board was projected in the dining hall that same afternoon, the whole list scrolling on the screen while the Valais group crowded in to see it up close. Asha #27→#24 (+3). Dara #142→#134 (+8). Nadia #170→#162 (+8). Riku found his own name, #158→#150 (+8), and felt the usual flash of satisfaction before looking for the name he actually wanted to see.

Oliver #68→#45 (+23).

Three spots below Pier. The biggest jump of the whole group, again, the same pattern that had been repeating since Val Tremola — Oliver breaking his own ceiling every time the personal cost was higher than reasonable.

"There's the price of not sleeping," Dara said, half joking, half not.

It was Nadia who noticed first, running her finger down the list on the screen, looking for a specific name without saying which.

"Petra's missing."

The whole dining hall seemed to take a second to register the sentence. Riku looked at Oliver, and saw the exact moment the information finished landing on him — not confusion, but the speed of someone who already had the piece, only missing the gap where it fit.

"That's what I was trying to tell you this morning," Pier said, appearing behind him, his voice softer than Riku had ever heard him use. "Petra's gone, Oliver. Yesterday afternoon. She didn't want goodbyes."

Oliver said nothing. He stayed looking at the screen, at the exact gap where Petra's name should have been, as if looking at it long enough could make it reappear.

Riku thought of the secondary training room, of the loaded punch repeating over and over against the air, and understood, too late for it to do any good, that Oliver had already known. Not the details — yesterday afternoon, no goodbyes — but the general shape of the loss, the same one he'd spent whole days training against something that still didn't have a name. Now, finally, it did.

No one at the table said anything else. The Board kept scrolling on the screen, indifferent, raising and lowering names that by then mattered to no one.
