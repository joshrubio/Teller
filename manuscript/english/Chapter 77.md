# Chapter 77 — Cold Trail

---

He'd gone four days without sleeping more than three hours straight, and his body hadn't collected on it yet. Yusuf knew it would — it always did, somewhere between the fifth and seventh day, a collapse that arrived all at once and gave no warning — but for now discipline was still holding him up, the same discipline that had carried him through thirty years of worse jobs than this one.

The café at Lausanne station had seven occupied tables at that hour, and Yusuf had picked the one that gave him full sightlines on both platform entrances without needing to turn his head. Coffee untouched in front of him, already cold. He wasn't drinking it for the coffee. He was drinking it because a person sitting alone with nothing in front of them draws attention, and a person sitting alone with a coffee becomes part of the furniture.

He hadn't activated his Domain once in four days.

It wasn't a rule anyone had imposed on him. It was his own, older than AMAT, older than the Valais Center — Density reinforced hands, forearms, ribs, made the body more resistant to localized impact, and it was absolutely useless for what he was doing now. Tracking wasn't a matter of taking a hit. It was a matter of seeing what other people didn't bother to see, and for that the Domain neither helped nor hindered. It was simply irrelevant. Using it here would have been like wearing a bulletproof vest to a chess match — not a mistake, exactly, but a way of not understanding the problem.

The problem, four days out of the Valais Center, remained the same: Kaspar left no trail because Kaspar knew exactly what counted as a trail.

Yusuf had started with the obvious and ruled it out in a day. Cameras around the Center — nothing, not a single frame in forty-eight hours of footage that could correspond to a man one meter eighty-four crossing on foot or by vehicle. Car rental records within a hundred-kilometer radius — a dozen names that led nowhere, none with the right profile. Kaspar had spent forty years avoiding exactly this kind of search. He wasn't going to get caught by a traffic camera in his first week back on the surface.

So Yusuf had changed methods. He'd stopped looking for a man and started looking for a constant.

Kaspar's Appearance Domain changed age, hair, skin tone, the surface structure of the face. What it didn't change — what Voss had confirmed in the file, with the same cold certainty she confirmed everything else — was height, body mass, and the eyes. Blue-gray, pale, the one feature forty years of Domain use had never touched. A man one meter eighty-four with those eyes could call himself anything, wear any face, and would still be, to anyone who looked closely enough, the same person.

The problem was that almost nobody looked closely enough.

Yusuf had spent three days showing a description with no photograph — he couldn't show a picture of something that changed faces — to hospitality staff, ticket clerks, a couple of taxi drivers who worked the night routes toward the border. Most remembered nothing, and the ones who remembered something usually remembered it wrong: a waiter in Sion had sworn for ten minutes that a man matching the description had paid in cash two nights running, until Yusuf checked the dates and found the waiter had been confusing his days since a shift change. A hotel receptionist in Martigny remembered light eyes, but the height didn't fit — fifteen centimeters off, too much for it to be the same man under any version of Appearance Kaspar could sustain without changing his own body mass, something Voss's file swore he couldn't do.

Two people remembered something that didn't quite fit. And this morning, a left-luggage attendant at the station — an older man, with the kind of memory trained by decades of looking at faces that only pass through once — had told him something Yusuf had spent three hours turning over.

*A tall man. I don't remember the face. I remember he never stopped looking me in the eye while he talked. That's not normal. People look at your hands, the ticket number, anything but the eyes.*

Yusuf had asked about the color.

*Light. Almost gray. Like cold water.*

The date matched: the same day Kaspar had left the range of Oliver's Registry, minute sixteen, seventeen. The left-luggage office was forty minutes by car from the point where the signal had been lost. It fit. It fit too well for four days of work that had produced nothing but dead ends.

Yusuf stirred the cold coffee without drinking it, looking at the platform without looking at it, and let the part of him that had spent thirty years doing this work process what that meant.

A man who'd gone forty years without showing his face to a single traffic camera didn't leave a clean trail by carelessness. Either Kaspar had made the first real mistake of his career — possible, nobody stays perfect forever, not even him — or someone had left that trail on purpose, knowing that sooner or later someone patient enough would find it.

Yusuf didn't know which of the two he preferred to be true.

He stood, left exact change on the table, and left the café without hurrying. The left-luggage office had an internal security camera — old, mediocre image quality, but a camera all the same — and if Kaspar had let an employee look him in the eye long enough for the detail to lodge in memory, there was a chance, remote but real, that there was also a frame in some archive nobody had thought to check yet because the date didn't match any reported incident.

Nobody knew he was here. He hadn't requested backup, and it wasn't a lack of humility — he knew perfectly well he'd need it at some point in this, that one man working alone hit a low ceiling against someone with a forty-year head start. But requesting backup meant explaining the method, ceding time to coordination, accepting that someone else made decisions about a trail only he had spent three days reading up close. Not yet. When he had something solid, something worth putting on a table, then yes.

For now, the only solid thing he had was a description of eyes someone remembered too clearly, and the feeling — impossible to justify to anyone who asked him to justify it — that the clarity wasn't an accident.

The mid-morning sun came in at an angle through the station's glass walls, lighting the dust suspended over platform three, where a regional train waited to depart. Yusuf crossed toward the station security office with the walk of someone who'd been doing this his whole life, no visible hurry, nothing in his posture to suggest the next few hours might confirm whether the man he'd spent four days chasing knew exactly where he was standing right now.

The credential he carried didn't say AMAT. It said something much more boring — a rail transport authority that existed on paper for exactly this kind of situation — and the station's security guard, a young man whose uniform was still too new, looked at it just long enough not to question it.

"I need the left-luggage office's internal footage, five days back," Yusuf said. "A specific day. Shouldn't take long to find if the system's indexed by date."

"We usually need a formal request for this," the guard said, already turning toward the terminal.

"Usually," Yusuf said, offering nothing more. Sometimes the absence of justification worked better than any justification he could invent — people filled the silence with the explanation that raised the fewest questions for themselves.

The guard typed in the date. The system took nearly a minute to load, an hourglass spinning in the corner of the screen while Yusuf waited standing, hands still at his sides, without the visible impatience someone else would have let show.

When the file list appeared, Yusuf read it before the guard finished turning the monitor toward him.

There was a gap.

Not a corrupted file, not an indexing error — the kind of thing Yusuf had seen hundreds of times in poorly maintained surveillance systems. This was different: the files before and after the gap had consecutive names, continuous timestamps, no jump in the numbering that suggested a technical fault. Fourteen minutes were simply missing — exactly the fourteen minutes that included the time the left-luggage attendant remembered talking with a tall man with light eyes.

"Is this normal?" Yusuf said, pointing at the gap without touching the screen.

The guard leaned in, frowning.

"It shouldn't be. The system records in twenty-four-hour loops. There shouldn't be gaps unless..." He stopped, typed something else, checked a second database. "Unless someone deleted it manually. But that needs admin access, and only two people have it."

"Can I talk to those two people?"

The guard hesitated, and in that hesitation Yusuf read something he'd already suspected before asking.

"One of them doesn't work here anymore," the guard said. "She left..." He checked again. "Five days ago. No notice. HR's still processing the resignation."

Five days. One day before Kaspar spoke to the left-luggage attendant, looking him in the eye long enough for the detail to lodge in a memory no camera could correct afterward.

Yusuf said nothing for a moment. The part of him that had spent thirty years doing this work was rearranging every piece he had — the trail too clean, the employee who remembered just enough and no more, the gap in the footage erased by someone no longer there to explain it — into a shape he didn't like at all.

This wasn't a trail he'd found. It was a trail someone had left, with enough care that it looked like a discovery, and with enough care, too, that Yusuf couldn't be sure whether it had been left for him in particular or for anyone patient enough to get this far.

"Do you have the name of the person who left?" he said, finally.

The guard did. Yusuf memorized it without writing it down anywhere, thanked him with the economy of someone who doesn't want to be remembered in detail, and walked out of the office toward the platform, where the regional train had already left and the next one wouldn't arrive for another half hour.

He stood by the empty track, in no hurry to move, weighing something he had no way to say to anyone yet because he had no way to prove it: that at some point in the last four days, he'd stopped tracking Kaspar on his own and started following a path that Kaspar — or someone working for him, or with him, or around him in a way Yusuf still didn't understand — had decided he should find.

The address on file in the station's HR records was twenty minutes on foot, in a 1970s apartment block near the lake, graceless concrete facade, rusted metal mailboxes at the entrance. Yusuf checked the name on the third-floor box — still there, the label unremoved, which was, in itself, a data point: someone who really leaves, with no intention of coming back, usually takes care of things like that before they go. Someone who leaves in a hurry doesn't.

He went up without buzzing the intercom. The lock on the street door was old, the kind that gave way to the right patience more than to force, and Yusuf had both. The apartment was on the third floor, door at the end of the hall, a thin layer of uncollected mail piled on the mat — mostly advertising, but also two envelopes with official letterheads Yusuf recognized by the format before reading the sender: bank notices, the kind sent when someone stops paying something without telling anyone.

He knocked anyway, not expecting an answer. None came.

The apartment door lock was better than the street door's, but not good enough to hold him for long, and Yusuf allowed himself, during the seconds it took to open it, the vague unease of crossing a line that in any normal operation would have required a warrant, a partner, a record that this was happening. He had none of that. He only had the certainty that if he waited to have it, whatever was inside would take that much longer to disappear too.

The apartment was empty in a way that wasn't the emptiness of someone moving out calmly. The furniture was still in place — a sofa, a table, bookshelves still loaded with books — but the drawers were open, some tipped over, as if someone had searched for something specific without worrying about leaving a trace of the search. In the kitchen, a plate with food scraps already dried had been sitting there, Yusuf calculated, at least five days. The freezer was still running. The refrigerator wasn't — someone had unplugged it, maybe so it wouldn't smell, maybe for no reason Yusuf could reconstruct yet.

In the bedroom, on the unmade bed, he found the one thing that seemed to have been left on purpose rather than simply abandoned: a photograph, face down, unframed. He turned it over.

It wasn't the tenant. It was an older man, white hair, light eyes that not even a badly printed ID photo could fully disguise — a file image, the kind AMAT generated automatically for active cases, with a code printed in the bottom corner that Yusuf didn't need to decode to recognize the format.

It was Kaspar's natural face. The one he'd gone forty years without using in public.

Yusuf stood looking at the photograph longer than he should have, calculating what it meant that someone had left it there, face down, on the bed of an employee who'd disappeared one day before Kaspar spoke to her coworker, looking him in the eye long enough to be remembered.

It wasn't carelessness. Nothing he'd found in the last four hours was.

He pocketed the photograph, left the apartment with the same care he'd used to get in, and went down the stairs thinking, for the first time since he'd left the Valais Center, that maybe he should have called for backup before getting this far alone.
