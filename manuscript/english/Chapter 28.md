# Chapter 28 — Altitude

Voss took his time answering.

It wasn't the kind of silence Park recognized from interrogations — the silence of someone waiting for the other person to speak first, or calculating how much to reveal before the conversation ran dry. It was the silence of someone who had found the right answer and was checking whether it was also the honest one.

"The three previous cases," he said finally, "activated under threat. Direct pressure, survival instinct, the organism doing what it does when it has no other option." He moved toward the doorway, unhurried. "Mr. Webb opened a notebook that wasn't his out of curiosity."

Park didn't answer. He let the information settle into the dark space between them.

"The three cases copied what threatened them." Voss stopped in the back door's frame. "Mr. Webb copied what he found interesting." Voss looked at the counter. "Given enough time, Agent Park, he'll copy everything he finds interesting. Everything."

Park looked at him, waiting.

"He wrote two words in that notebook, by hand. Nobody taught him to, and nobody was meant to see it." Voss said it the way he said everything — flat, exact. "I only have it because Maren pulled it out of him during evaluation, weeks into it. He wasn't trying to tell anyone anything at the time." He let that sit for a moment. "*REGISTRY SETTLED.*"

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked.

Voss looked at him with the specific attention of someone confirming the question was sincere before answering it.

"Because you went to Hartford instead of the airport." Voss looked at him directly. "And I need to know if there's someone paying attention."

The back door closed slowly behind him.

Park didn't move for a moment. The shop had been closed for weeks — the only sound was the ventilation system keeping itself running on inertia, and outside, far off, the kind of traffic that exists in Grayhaven at five in the morning. Light was starting to filter through the tall windows on the floor. It wasn't light that illuminated anything yet — only the difference between total darkness and darkness with edges.

Park looked at the counter for several seconds.

Then he turned and went down to the parking lot.

He didn't go to the airport. He went to the car. He drove.

---

*Six weeks later.*

---

The nine o'clock session opened with the same question it always did.

"How did you sleep?"

"Fine," Oliver said.

Maren Steiger noted something on her tablet without looking up, with the kind of concentration Oliver had learned to read over the six weeks he'd spent at the center: not indifference, but distributed attention. Maren paid attention to several things at once without any of them looking neglected. It was a trait Oliver had mentally filed as useful, because it meant he could say true things while Maren was writing and not have to manage them right away.

"Fine as in uninterrupted, or fine as in rested?"

Oliver considered the distinction.

"Uninterrupted," he said.

Maren nodded. Wrote it down.

The session room had a window that looked out on the mountains. Oliver had learned, in the first two weeks, not to look through that window for more than three seconds at a stretch, because the altitude gave the horizon a clarity the brain read as distortion. The center sat in a valley fourteen hundred meters above sea level — a stone building that had been something else before the European branch converted it, though nobody had answered exactly what when Oliver had asked. The structure still had the functional solidity of something built to last, with more recent additions that didn't try to blend in, only to work.

It was a place that didn't try to look like anything it wasn't. Oliver had decided that in the first week and had had no reason to change his mind.

"Have you noticed anything different in your perception this week?"

The usual question. Oliver answered it with calibrated precision — enough for Maren to have data, not enough for the data to outrun his own process of understanding it.

"The contours are sharper in the mornings."

Maren looked up.

"The contours of what?"

"Of everything." Oliver chose his words carefully. "Like calibrating a monitor and adjusting the contrast. The difference between what has Odem and what doesn't. It's more pronounced in the mornings."

"Since when?"

"Since about the third week. It was gradual."

Maren wrote longer than usual. Oliver waited without filling the silence — he'd learned that here too.

"And does it cause you any practical difficulty?"

"Not yet."

Maren looked at him for a moment. It wasn't the look of someone calculating how to respond to an implied threat — it was the look of someone who'd heard one thing and was deciding whether the next thing they said would bring them closer to the other person or push them further away.

"Good. Let's continue."

---

The center had nine subjects when Oliver arrived. He made ten.

It wasn't a number anyone had mentioned directly — Oliver had worked it out on his own, counting trays in the dining hall, schedules written on the main corridor's whiteboard, specific footsteps he recognized from people he'd never seen face to face. The center operated with the discretion of something designed for subjects who already knew exactly what they were, with no need to manage their disbelief or protect them from themselves.

Most of the subjects had been there longer than Oliver. It showed in the way they moved through the common spaces — not relaxed, exactly, but adjusted. Knowing how much space they needed and how much they yielded.

Oliver had learned not to look at them too much.

Not because of protocol. For the specific reason that looking at them too long made the contours Maren had asked about grow sharper, sharper with more detail than Oliver knew what to do with yet. Every subject had a signature — not visual, not exactly auditory. Something Oliver could only describe as the difference between reading a text file and reading an executable one. Same hallway, same breakfast, same space. But some of them did something at the edge of what Oliver could perceive that was different from the rest. Like an extra frequency the rest of the world didn't broadcast.

He'd gone three weeks without telling Maren any of this.

Not to hide it. For the same reason he applied to all his new data: he needed to understand it before turning it into information, because at the center, new information cost something before he could assign it a value. He'd learned that here too.

Asha had the clearest signature.

Twenty-eight, short hair, functional integration according to the whiteboard code Oliver had cracked in his second week. They'd never had a long conversation — subjects in functional integration ran on different schedules from those in active evaluation, and the overlaps were brief. But they crossed paths at breakfast, and Asha had opinions.

Three weeks earlier, in front of the Italian coffee machine the center had installed the year before, Asha had pointed at it with the gesture of someone who'd had this conversation before but never found the right person to have it with.

"The pressure isn't regulated."

"Does that matter for coffee?" Oliver had asked.

"It matters for everything." Asha hadn't taken her eyes off the machine. "If you can't control the input variable, you can't predict the output. The result depends on the process, not the product."

Oliver had considered whether that was a metaphor. He'd decided it probably wasn't, or that if it was, Asha didn't know it.

"Basic," she'd added, in the tone of someone closing an argument the other person hadn't gotten around to disputing.

"Basic," Oliver had repeated.

Asha had looked at him then, for the first time since they'd started talking, with the quick attention of someone gauging whether agreement was real or just social protocol. She'd decided something in that second, though Oliver hadn't known exactly what.

"How long have you been here?" she'd asked.

"Two weeks."

"It passes." She'd taken her cup and left.

Oliver hadn't told her that pressure regulation was exactly what his Domain did. He'd taken his coffee and kept the image: Asha looking at the coffee machine with that specific concentration of someone who sees variables where other people see appliances.

Oliver recognized it.

Asha's signature was denser than the others'. Not more intense — dense. Like more matter packed into the same volume. And in the mornings, when the contours were sharpest, Oliver could feel her from the corridor before he even reached the dining hall.

---

Between the morning session and the afternoon one, Oliver had two hours with no protocol.

The center called it free time. Oliver used it the way he'd learned to use the silence in his sessions with Maren: without trying to produce anything with it. He walked the perimeter of the building when the cold allowed it, which in the Alps in October was rare. He read the technical books in the center's library — network structures, systems theory, nothing he hadn't read before, but here reading had a different quality. Like rereading a manual after you've already used the tool.

Sometimes he sat in the main corridor, on the stone bench facing the window that looked out on the valley, not doing anything in particular.

It was in those moments that the other subjects' signatures were easiest to register. Without a session's conversation to manage, without Maren's tablet as a reference point, Oliver could just notice what he noticed. Lev, in his forties, Heat Domain — Oliver felt him as a gradient that shifted slowly with the time of day, always warmer toward noon. A woman whose name Oliver didn't know yet, in formal collaboration, whose signature was so stable and low that Oliver sometimes had to actively search for it to confirm she was still there.

And Asha.

Asha was constant. Present no matter where she was in the building.

That afternoon's session ran longer than usual.

Maren had added a new test to the protocol — Oliver with his eyes closed, describing what he perceived in the room with no visual support. It wasn't the first time they'd done this, but the focus was different: Maren wanted to know whether the signature Oliver perceived from people varied with distance.

It did. Oliver described it with the terminology he'd built over the previous weeks: contours, density, frequency. Maren wrote it all down without interrupting.

At the end of the session, when Oliver opened his eyes, Maren closed the tablet with a deliberate gesture. Which meant whatever came next wasn't part of the standard protocol.

"There's an observation session scheduled tomorrow," she said. "Cross-evaluation protocol: you observe from the secondary space while another subject runs an exercise with their Domain active."

Oliver waited.

"It's Asha. Controlled tension exercise, level four. Roughly twenty minutes." Maren let that land. "The goal is to record your response to the presence of another Domain in direct activation. You don't have to do anything. Just be there."

"Just be there?" Oliver repeated.

"Just be there."

Oliver nodded.

What he didn't say was that he could already feel Asha's Domain from the corridor, and that if in tomorrow's session Asha activated it for real, and Oliver was in the same building, *just be there* might not be as simple as Maren was making it sound.

But that was new information. He didn't understand its value yet.

"Understood," he said.

---

Oliver's room had a window facing north, toward the irregular profile of the Alps in the distance. At night, with the valley lights off, the mountains' outline stood visible against the sky — darker than the darkness, present as a mass before it was ever a shape.

Oliver had picked up a new habit in his second week: sitting on the edge of the bed with the window cracked two centimeters, the cold just enough to keep his body from fully settling into sleep, and thinking without trying to produce anything with those thoughts. He'd never had that habit in Grayhaven. In Grayhaven there had always been something to do or something to ignore.

Here there wasn't much he could ignore.

Gary had gotten the cover story. A call from some parking lot near Cleveland, a Thursday in the third week, short like all of Gary's calls — just long enough for Oliver to know his father believed the fellowship story, and believed it without suspecting anything, which was exactly the specific kind of relief Oliver hadn't known he needed until it arrived. Gary had said he was proud and that the international part wasn't really his thing, but if that was how it was, fine. Oliver had said yeah, fine.

He'd hung up and sat staring at the phone for a moment before handing it back to Maren.

He hadn't asked about Park.

Not because he didn't want to know. For the same calculation he applied to everything else: the question had a cost Oliver was still working out. If Park was on another assignment, that would be the answer, and Oliver wouldn't have any more information than before. If Park had tried to ask about him, knowing that didn't change anything from here either. And if Park hadn't asked — that possibility, Oliver left exactly where it was, still unopened.

There was time.

He lay down without fully closing the window.

Asha's signature reached him from the corridor across the way with the same constancy as always — not intrusive, not bothersome. Just present, like the background hum of a system somebody had left running. Oliver perceived it with the part of himself that had learned to read Odem without looking for it, the same way he used to perceive the hum of an overloaded transformer without needing to stand in front of it.

Tomorrow, Asha would activate her Domain for real. Level four, Maren had said. Controlled.

Oliver didn't know what that would do to the signature.

He didn't know what it would do to him.

He closed his eyes.

The mountains were still out there, in the dark, not moving.
