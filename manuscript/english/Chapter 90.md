# Chapter 90 — Val Bedretto

---

The van dropped them at the end of a gravel track the official St. Gotthard map didn't even bother naming, and the cold that met them when they got out had nothing to do with the controlled cold of the complex — real wind, cutting, dropping straight off ridgelines that still held snow on their north faces despite weeks of late spring already behind them.

"Val Bedretto," Reber said, no more preamble than usual, pointing at a valley opening west between two walls of gray rock. "Outside the building. There's no net under you this time, no mat, no instructor a meter away ready to cut the exercise if something goes wrong. If someone gets hurt here, they get hurt for real, and the helicopter takes twenty minutes to arrive even if we call it right now. Act accordingly."

Nobody asked anything. The group's own silence, different from the silence of any earlier briefing, already said the message had landed.

"The exercise is simple to explain, not simple to execute," Reber went on. "A simulated target, marked with a beacon you won't be able to see until you're close, hidden somewhere in that valley. Locate it, secure it, bring it back to the starting point. Rotating command — each team picks who leads, and that person leads for real, not just in name. I'm not stepping in unless someone's in real danger."

The Valais group stepped a few meters away to decide, and the decision came faster than Oliver expected.

"Asha," Pier said, and nobody argued it. "She already organized us well in the archive. And she's not going to lose her head if the terrain turns ugly."

Asha didn't protest, and she didn't acknowledge the nomination with any special gesture. She just looked at the valley for a moment, calculating something only she could see so far, and started assigning positions with the same economy she brought to everything else.

"Pier and Riku, north flank, following the treeline. Petra and Dara, center, open ground — more visibility but more exposed. Nadia, with me, we take the south flank where the rock gets worse. Oliver" — she turned toward him a moment — "you move between all three groups. Nobody else on this team can read what you read. Use it."

Nobody argued the assignments, though Oliver noticed, as they split off toward their positions, that Pier gave Asha a brief look, almost professional approval — the kind of gesture a man with his years of experience didn't hand out easily.

"Twelve minutes to the first position check," Asha said, already moving toward the south flank, Nadia keeping pace beside her. "If anyone loses visual contact with their partner for more than thirty seconds, stop and shout. There's no shame in getting lost out here. There is in not saying so."

The north flank disappeared into the trees almost immediately, Pier setting a steady pace that Riku, still adjusting to the extra weight of the field pack, took a couple of minutes to match. In the center, Petra and Dara moved slower, fully exposed on the open ground, each scanning a different semicircle without needing to coordinate it out loud — weeks of living under pressure together had left behind the kind of silent sync no formal exercise ever taught as well as time did.

---

The terrain, barely fifteen minutes in, made it clear why Reber had bothered warning them.

The slope on the south flank hadn't looked steep on Asha's map, but on the actual ground it turned out to be a run of loose rock plates, every step a negotiation between moving forward and not setting off the whole hillside underfoot. Nadia, ahead of Asha along a stretch where the trail narrowed, felt the stone give before she saw it give — the weight badly distributed, the surface that had spent centuries waiting for exactly that point of pressure to move.

There was no time to think it through. The Impulse came out of her almost on instinct, not aimed forward the way it would be in any combat exercise, but sideways — a short, controlled push against the very rock under her right foot, enough to redistribute her weight a fraction of a second before the slab gave way completely.

She fell anyway, but she fell controlled, rolling into the hillside instead of toward the drop opening up thirty meters below, and Asha reached her before she'd finished stopping, one firm hand on her good arm, pulling with just enough force to keep Nadia from sliding further.

"I'm fine," Nadia said, her voice more ragged than her words let on. "My foot's not. But I'm fine."

"That was fast," Asha said, already kneeling beside her, no panic in her voice, just observation. "The Impulse. I didn't know you could use it that way — inward instead of outward."

"Neither did I," Nadia said, still catching her breath. "It just came out on its own."

Asha didn't waste another second on unnecessary comfort. She checked the ankle with her hands, quick, professional, and made the call before anyone else could offer an opinion.

"You can put weight on it, you can't run on it," she said. "You're staying with us, but you set the pace, not me. If you say we stop, we stop. That's not a question."

Nadia nodded, and something in the way she did it — without her usual quick, fake smile, without needing to pretend everything was better than it was — told Oliver, watching from a few meters up the slope, that this time the "I'm fine" came closer to true than any of the earlier ones had.

---

Oliver found the target almost by accident, or so it seemed at first — an irregularity in the outline of a rock that, on a second look, turned out to be the beacon, disguised under a thin layer of artificial moss. But when he moved closer to confirm it, trying to read whether there was anything more than a simple marker waiting there, the Registry handed him something he didn't know how to interpret: a violent spike of contrast, almost painful behind his eyes, followed by nothing at all, as if the signal had cut off mid-transmission. The beacon, he understood afterward, had some kind of AMAT emitter inside it, raw Odem stuffed into an object with no Domain behind it — exactly the kind of thing the Registry didn't know how to process cleanly, for lack of structure to read, not from an excess of it.

He stood still a moment, waiting for it to settle. It didn't, not fully — it spiked and dropped twice more, with no pattern he could recognize, before leveling off into something close to normal. He didn't mention it to anyone. There was nothing to mention yet — only the suspicion, uncomfortable and shapeless, that trying to read an object with Odem stuffed inside it but no Domain organizing it produced exactly that kind of echo: a discharge with no structure, impossible to turn into anything useful.

He marked the position for Asha and helped secure the beacon without saying another word about it.

When Reber finally spoke, it was back at the starting point, with the whole group gathered.

"Valais team, target secured within acceptable time, one minor injury handled without stopping the exercise or compromising the group," he said. "Command well exercised." He looked at Asha a second longer than strictly necessary. "Take note, those of you who still think leading means talking louder than everyone else."

Nobody in Dax's group answered that, but Oliver noticed, out of the corner of his eye, that at least one of them tucked it away to think about later.

Back in the van, with the valley already disappearing behind them in the rearview mirror, Riku dropped into the seat next to Oliver with the dead weight of someone who'd spent hours holding exhaustion off through sheer will.

"You know what's strange?" he said quietly. "Today nobody argued about who was in charge. Not once. Asha would say something and we'd all just do it, just like that."

"Because she was right every time she said it," Dara said, from the seat up front, without turning around. "It's easier not to argue orders when the person giving them has earned being listened to."

Nadia, her ankle already bandaged and propped on another teammate's pack, didn't say anything, but Oliver saw her smile out the window — something small and private that didn't look built for anyone else to see.

The hangar screens, that afternoon, took longer than usual to update — the system, Oliver guessed, processing something more complicated than the simple time and weight of an individual exercise. When the numbers finally appeared, the whole Valais team had climbed, not just Asha: she went from #41 to #34, Pier from #59 to #51, Petra from #105 to #95, Dara from #168 to #157, Riku from #173 to #163. Oliver climbed from #110 to #93 — seventeen spots, more than anyone else. Nadia climbed more than anyone: from #213, dead last on the whole list, to #192.

"The Redoubt," Reber said, catching the collective surprised faces before anyone had to ask out loud, "doesn't just score each of you separately. A team that works well together is worth more, together, than the sum of what each of you would be worth alone. Today you worked. The number notices, even if you have trouble believing it."

Nadia stayed looking at her own number longer than the rest of them, her face still without the manufactured smile from other times. Twenty-one spots didn't pull her all the way out of the bottom of the list, but it was, by a wide margin, the biggest jump anyone in the group had made in a single exercise.

Oliver thought, watching his own number climb by more than any single piece of the day's data explained on its own — finding the beacon shouldn't have been worth that much by itself — that maybe the Board, for once, was also measuring something he couldn't name yet himself: what it cost, and what it was worth, to move between three groups reading what nobody else could read.

---

That night, long after dinner, Oliver took Petra to the secondary training room for the first time.

He hadn't fully planned it — when she'd asked him, almost in passing, where he disappeared to some nights, he simply hadn't found any real reason not to tell her. He led her down the same unmarked corridor, to the same door with no sign, and let her go in first.

"This is where you train Mantle," Petra said, looking around at the stacked mats, the long mirror covering the back wall. "Alone."

"Most nights," Oliver said. "Still failing a different way each time."

Petra didn't say "teach me." She said something closer to "show me what name they gave it," because the moment Oliver demonstrated it — his own Odem held tight against his ribs, Focus closed down on the exact point of his forearm Anka had taught him to use as a block — something crossed Petra's face that wasn't surprise. It was recognition.

"I already do this," she said, trying it herself against Oliver's open palm, her first attempt already cleaner than his had been in weeks. "Or something very close to it. Two years negotiating with people who wanted to hurt me in ways that weren't always physical taught me to hold before I respond, without anyone ever putting it in these terms. I didn't know it had a name. I didn't know it was the same thing the soldiers who've been beating us at everything have been doing for years."

"So what do you need from me, then?" Oliver said, genuinely not knowing the answer.

"Tell me how far this really goes," Petra said. "I know how to hold. I don't know yet if I can hold enough to stand up against someone like Iker, or whoever it was that fought Dara. I want to find the real limit, not the one that's enough to survive a negotiation."

What followed wasn't a beginner's lesson. Petra already came in with a real foundation, more solid than Oliver's had been the first night he'd tried this alone, and what she was after wasn't learning the gesture but pushing it past where her own experience had left it — closing the block faster, holding it against harder hits than Oliver could give her, finding the exact point where holding stopped being enough and something more was needed. She failed, but she failed high, every attempt already starting from a level most of the group would have taken weeks to reach. By the fifth or sixth try, the block she held against Oliver's palm withstood a hit that, days earlier, would have sent her whole arm reeling back.

The sound of the hits — palm against palm, the dry echo of a block set well — carried out into the hallway outside in a way neither of them noticed at first, too caught up in what they were doing.

Dax noticed.

He was passing through that stretch of the complex on his way to somewhere he never got around to explaining, and he stopped by the closed door long enough to recognize the rhythm of what he was hearing — not a fight, not a supervised drill, something closer to two people pushing each other past a limit they both already knew well. In the increasingly steady spacing of the hits, he recognized the kind of progress that only comes when someone who already knows how to do something decides to stop settling for barely knowing it.

He stayed there longer than he could have justified if anyone had asked him, back against the hallway wall, listening to the echo of every hit without ever opening the door or saying a word. He wouldn't have been able to explain, if anyone had asked him directly, why he cared about listening to the end instead of just moving on. Maybe it wasn't curiosity about Oliver. Maybe it was simply the kind of sound nobody in his own group would ever have thought to make, and one he himself hadn't let himself make in years either.

When he finally left, neither of the two inside the room ever knew he'd been there.
