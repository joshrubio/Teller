# Chapter 36 — Interference

Asha's signature was different that morning.

Not more intense — organized. As if overnight something inside her Odem had made a decision about how to arrange itself. Oliver noticed it from the corridor, before reaching the dining hall, with the same clarity he used to notice when an account in Yamal's books didn't add up on first read. The information arrived before he went looking for it.

He ate breakfast alone. Asha didn't come down.

---

The secondary observation room was in the east wing of the center, separated from the main room by a thick glass panel and a steel door that stayed shut during active exercises. Maren was already there when Oliver arrived, two tablets on the table and the monitoring equipment on standby.

"Morning," she said, without looking up.

"Morning."

"Sit wherever you like. I'll just ask you not to get closer to the panel than you need to."

Oliver chose the most central chair, facing the glass. On the other side, the main room had a long table with several metal stands — rods and plates of different alloys, arranged with precision. The kind of order that isn't decorative but functional. Asha stood beside the table, her back to him, finishing setting up something Oliver couldn't fully see.

"What does it do exactly, at Level Four?" he asked.

"Controlled tension on test materials. Measured compression and release." Maren looked up briefly. "You'll see."

Oliver nodded. He looked at the glass.

With the blindfold on during transfers, Oliver had learned to notice when his perception of the Odem sharpened the moment it came off — like a contrast adjustment, the difference between seeing without light and seeing with it. Here, with full vision and Asha's signature more organized than ever, the contours were different. Not brighter. More structured. Like a file that had finished loading.

Asha turned and looked at him briefly through the panel. She nodded, no particular expression. Oliver nodded back.

Maren started the monitoring.

Nothing visible happened in the first thirty seconds.

Asha stood in front of the table, arms at her sides, unmoving, with the still concentration of someone doing something Oliver couldn't see yet from his angle. But the Odem was visible. Asha's signature — already organized differently that morning than on previous days — started to shift more explicitly: it condensed, took on direction, like a field orienting itself before the current passes through it. Oliver watched it happen in real time and found no better comparison than a system loading before executing. Preparation, not action yet.

Then Asha looked at the first rod, and the rod began to move.

---

Asha started with the small rods.

There was no visible movement at first. That was the first thing Oliver registered — no gesture, no particular posture, nothing indicating effort. Asha simply stood in front of the first rod and the rod bent slowly, following a clean curve, as if an invisible weight were pushing it from the inside. When it reached the angle Asha wanted, it stopped.

Oliver filed it away as data.

What he hadn't anticipated was what watching it did to the Odem.

Asha's signature, which had spent weeks being a constant at the edge of his perception — dense, present, identifiable as a background frequency — had become something else. Not bigger. More legible. Like the difference between recognizing a written word and being able to pronounce it. The pattern of her Domain now had a visible structure: layers, direction, the specific angle from which the tension was applied and the point where it anchored before transferring into the material.

It was information. Not sensation.

The part of Oliver that had spent almost two years squaring other people's accounts without looking for it recognized it before he could tell it to stop.

"You okay?" Maren asked, without taking her eyes off the tablet.

"Yes," Oliver said.

Asha moved to the next rod. This one was thicker. It took longer, and Oliver could see — not imagine, see — the whole process: Asha's Odem extending toward the metal, finding the internal structure, identifying the points of least resistance. Like a diagnostic. Like what Oliver used to do with Yamal's books, but in real time and on physical matter.

He tried not to watch it too closely.

He knew the condition. Deliberate attention triggered the copy. If he stayed in passive observation, if he didn't actively read the pattern's structure...

The problem was that the difference between observing and reading was smaller than he'd calculated.

---

He noticed his hands first.

Not pain. Tension — the specific sensation of something contracting without him activating it, but not in the muscle. In the air around his fingers. As if the space between his knuckles had grown denser, more resistant, with no physical reason for it.

Oliver looked at his hands. He opened them slowly, fingers extended.

Nothing visible.

Asha was on the fourth rod. Her Odem pattern was clearer than ever — Oliver could already tell the difference between when she applied tension inward and when she released it outward. They were two directions of the same process, and both were legible. And the Registry was reading them, because that was what the Registry did when the conditions lined up: it read without Oliver asking it to.

The window frame made a sound.

Not loud. The specific creak of metal under light stress — the kind of sound a structure makes when something pushes it from inside with little force but constantly. Oliver recognized it before he connected it to what was happening.

Maren looked up from the tablet.

A thin crack had appeared in the lower corner of the glass panel. Not spreading, not dangerous, but new. It hadn't been there twenty minutes earlier.

The chair legs beneath him gave off the same creak.

"Oliver," Maren said, in a tone that wasn't alarm but wasn't neutral either.

"I know," he said.

He hadn't decided this. That was what he still didn't know how to explain — that he hadn't made any decision, that he hadn't done anything but watch, and that something was happening anyway in the space around him that responded to Asha's pattern as though imitating it without instructions. Like a process that executes on its own because it finds the right conditions in the system.

He closed his eyes.

The pressure didn't leave. It stopped — stopped growing — but what had already built up was still there, undirected, unanchored, like charged energy with nowhere to go because nobody had given it a direction.

Maren activated the intercom.

"Asha, stop."

On the other side of the panel, the exercise cut off.

---

Asha came into the observation room through the side door. She looked at the crack in the glass. She looked at Oliver. Her expression wasn't anger or fear — it was the specific attention of someone assessing a variable that wasn't in the plan.

"Was that you?" she asked.

"I didn't decide it."

Asha processed that for a second.

"I felt it from over there." She gestured toward the main room. "Like someone was applying tension in this room from the inside, with no fixed anchor. That's not efficient."

"I know."

"I'm not criticizing you. It's a technical observation."

Oliver looked at his hands again. The dense sensation was gone, but something at the edges of his perception was still different — a residue. Like when a system process closes but doesn't release the memory properly. It was there. Not active. Just loaded.

Maren had stopped writing and was looking at both of them.

"Oliver," she said. "Describe what happened, from the start."

Oliver did it with the precision he'd learned in the sessions: from the organized signature that morning, the contrast adjustment when he saw the active Domain, the pattern that had become legible without him seeking it out. The tension in his hands. The frame. He didn't lie or leave anything out. This time he didn't weigh the value of the information before giving it.

Maren wrote it all down.

"Did you try to activate it?" she asked when he finished.

"No. I tried not to."

"And when you closed your eyes?"

"It stopped. But what had built up didn't go away."

Maren looked at the crack in the glass for a moment. Then she looked back at Oliver with the expression of someone who's just confirmed something she expected to find, but sooner than planned.

"You've been sensing her signature for weeks," she said. It wasn't a question.

Oliver waited a second.

"Yes."

"Since exactly when?"

"Since the second week. In the corridor, before the dining hall."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

Oliver had already thought about it. The honest answer was also the more uncomfortable one.

"I didn't know if it was relevant or just background noise."

"In your case," Maren said, with a precision that wasn't a reproach but wasn't gentle either, "everything is relevant. Especially the background noise."

Asha had taken a seat in the chair by the door, arms crossed, following the exchange with the quiet attention of someone who's been at the center long enough to know when a conversation isn't about her and when it is.

"Can I ask something?" she said.

Maren nodded.

"The copy." She gestured at the glass with a minimal motion. "Will it last?"

"No," Oliver said, before Maren could answer. "It's degrading. I can feel it."

Asha looked at him.

"How do you feel it?"

Oliver searched for the right comparison.

"Like a corrupted file the system is trying to read. It's still there but the format's wrong. Every time I access it, it loses something."

Asha considered that with the specific focus of someone who has a formed technical opinion and is deciding whether to give it.

"You need to practice it," she said finally. "If you don't exercise it, it disappears."

"I know."

"Do you know what that means?"

"That I'll have to do it on purpose."

Asha nodded.

"With time, and with something worth copying." Asha looked at the broken glass a moment. "It took me eight months to control the direction. And my Domain doesn't need to read someone else's first."

"Yours is yours from the start," Oliver said.

"Yes." Asha looked at him with the direct attention Oliver had learned to recognize in her — not evaluating, but leveling, as if she were speaking to someone at the same conversational tier. "Yours doesn't exist until it finds someone else's. That's harder or easier depending on how you look at it."

Oliver weighed the two options.

"Harder," he said.

"Probably." Asha stood up from the chair. "But it also means whatever you learn stays. Mine I can't lend out."

Oliver knew that. It meant next time he'd have to do it deliberately. With Asha present, with Maren monitoring, with the third condition active — reading the pattern's structure, not just seeing it. Choosing to copy instead of letting it happen without him choosing it.

The difference between the two versions was what separated an accident from a tool.

---

Pier was in the corridor when Oliver came out.

He wasn't waiting for him — he was simply there, leaning against the wall with his arms crossed and the expression of someone who's decided that's a good place to be for a while. Pier had that quality: he occupied space with the calm of someone who no longer needs to prove anything to anyone.

"The building?" Oliver said.

"The building," Pier confirmed. Not as a question or a reproach. As data. "Lasted about twenty seconds. Everything all right?"

"Yes," Oliver said. Then, because Pier wasn't the kind of person short answers worked on: "Involuntary."

Pier nodded with the slowness of someone who'd already heard that word applied to himself many times before.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we have breakfast early. I'll tell you what I know about involuntary."

He didn't wait for an answer. He pushed off the wall and continued down the corridor.

---

His room's window still faced north.

Oliver had the pen in his hand — one of the metal ones from the desk, silver, the center's logo engraved on one side. He'd picked it up without thinking, during the twenty minutes he'd spent sitting on the edge of the bed looking at the mountains without seeing them.

He tried to access what was left of the copy.

The residue was still there, smaller, more diffuse — a degraded signal on a low-bandwidth channel. Oliver focused his attention on it the same way he focused on an error log: not looking for the result, but looking for the structure. The pattern of how Asha applied tension inward. The direction. The anchor point.

The pen bent.

Not well. Not in the clean curve Asha made with her test rods. At an irregular angle, too much force concentrated at one point and none at the others, like an instruction executed with half the code. Oliver let go and the pen lay bent on the desk.

He looked at it for a moment.

It was nothing. It was exactly what had happened in Grayhaven when he unbound the Authority without understanding what he was doing — proto-copy, not a mature Domain. The difference between recognizing a pattern and understanding it well enough to reproduce it with intent was all the work he still hadn't done. Weeks of passive exposure had loaded the Registry without giving it structure. They'd given it fuel with no map.

Which meant he'd have to learn to read the map. Not Asha's pattern in the abstract — Asha's pattern in operation, with deliberate attention, with the third condition active. Seeing the structure and understanding it well enough to reproduce it with intent, instead of letting the Registry capture it just because the conditions were favorable.

There was a difference between a system that executes because it can and a system that executes because you tell it to. Oliver had spent weeks being the former.

Tomorrow Maren would change the protocol. Oliver knew it without anyone telling him yet — he'd seen it in the way she closed the tablet at the end of the meeting, the specific gesture of someone reorganizing what comes next. More joint sessions, probably. More supervision. More variables under someone else's control instead of his own.

He thought about Park for a moment. Not about anything specific — just that Park would have had something dry and precise to say about bending a pen by accident and calling it progress.

He put the bent pen in the desk drawer.

The mountains were still out there, in the dark, unmoving.

The protocol was going to change. And Oliver still didn't know whether that meant Maren trusted him more, or that she needed to control him better.

The difference mattered.
