# Chapter 35 — What the Machine Room Took

Rafa didn't go toward Mariana.

He crossed the room in two strides, straight at Báthory, and before anyone could say a word he was already at his side, both hands dropping toward the floor.

"No!" Mariana's scream arrived too late. The floor was already moving.

"I came alone," Rafa said, his voice tight with the effort, not looking at his sister. "Nobody brought me."

It wasn't a small warning tremor. It was the Vigília's whole one, compressed into a three-meter radius instead of half a city — the floor jumping under the feet of all four of them, a loose stretch of piping falling from the ceiling with a metallic clang that echoed through the whole room, and above their heads something in the elevator's old structure groaned, a sound that shouldn't come out of a building meant to hold for another hundred years.

Two threats, not one. Park didn't have time to choose which first: Báthory was already loading her right hand again, and Rafa was shaking the ground under their feet at the same time, the exact combination designed — or stumbled into by accident, there was no way to know — so that neither thing could be handled separately.

Park did the only thing that made sense: he stopped trying to dodge the tremor and used it.

It wasn't so different, at bottom, from taking a hit. The floor pushed against the soles of his feet with the same logic a fist pushes against a forearm — only distributed, only sustained over time instead of packed into an instant. Park did what he'd spent two years doing with any impact: he stopped resisting it and absorbed it, the tremor's momentum adding to what he'd already accumulated since walking through that room's door, instead of throwing him down.

Báthory released the detonation believing Park was still committed to his balance.

He wasn't.

Park pivoted on the foot the tremor hadn't managed to move, let the detonation pass a hand's width from his shoulder, and closed the distance to Báthory before Rafa could coordinate the next tremor with the next strike. It wasn't elegant. It was fast, and it came from an angle Báthory didn't expect, because nobody moves forward when the ground is shaking.

Half a step away, Park forced himself to do something more — something Mariana had taught him without meaning to teach it. He shut off, for a second, whatever was legible about him. Void, barely held for more than two breaths, but enough that Báthory lost, for that instant, the fine reading of where the blow was going to land. It didn't blind her. It cost her the half-second lead she'd carried the whole fight.

"Interrupt," Park said, almost to himself, his elbow already moving. "Again."

The strike connected with Báthory's forearm mid-charge, her concentration broken early for the second time that night — but this time Báthory was already expecting it, and turned her wrist at the last instant so the detonation, without releasing fully, spilled onto Park himself instead of dissipating against the motor. It was small. It was enough. Park felt the air compress against his chest, the dull impact pushing him two steps back, against the edge of the sparking electrical panel.

Mariana moved again, still without choosing sides, her hand stretched into the air between the three of them — but this time the drain arrived late. She couldn't sustain the Vigília's duel, contain Báthory, and protect Rafa all at once, and for the first time all night her body told her before her head did: a stab behind the eyes, her legs giving a degree under her own weight. Something had to give, and what gave was the fine control.

Rafa gritted his teeth and dropped his hands again. His arms were already shaking, the effort of holding something his body still didn't know how to fully close — but the ground obeyed anyway, longer this time, almost five seconds, a new crack running from the motor's mounting point to the oil drum already split by the earlier detonation.

Park saw it before anyone else: the oil starting to drip onto the panel's exposed wiring, a sputter that no longer went out on its own.

There was no time to get anyone out. There was time for one thing.

Park brought together the two variables he'd spent two years unable to hold together for more than one strike per action — the full momentum accumulated since walking through that room's door, plus everything he'd absorbed that night. Instead of leaving it spread across the whole arm the way it had been in Seoul, he drove it into a single point of contact, the size of two knuckles. Focus, if anyone in that room had had time to give it a name. Smaller than ever. More precise than ever. Not to destroy — to displace.

The strike went straight for the drum, not Báthory, not Rafa. It sent it flying toward the stairwell gap before it finished catching, a movement calculated in the exact second Park understood that putting out the fire was impossible and containing it was the only option left.

It wasn't enough.

The drum struck the stairwell frame and didn't break clean — it bounced, and the remaining spray of oil fanned out over the rest of the exposed wiring, over the second drum, over the floor itself where Rafa was still planted, hands still down, unable to let go of the tremor mid-hold without something worse giving way all at once.

"Let it go, Rafa, now!" Báthory shouted, and for the first time all night it didn't sound calculated. It sounded like someone watching something come that she could no longer stop.

The electrical panel gave out entirely a second later — a white discharge, a roar that wasn't an Odem detonation but something far simpler and far older, short circuit and oil and a room that hadn't been inspected in a century — and this time it wasn't a contained tongue of flame. It was a whole ball of heat that swallowed the corner where Rafa stood and kept growing, fed by everything that room had been dripping the whole fight.

Báthory threw herself at him instead of toward the exit — the only move all night she didn't calculate first — and the edge of the fireball caught them both before either could clear the radius: Báthory glancingly, throwing her against the motor with her clothes already catching on one side; Rafa full on, his body vanishing for one whole instant inside the white glare.

Park didn't have time to think. He grabbed Mariana around the waist and pulled her toward the service gap with all the momentum he had left, the heat passing so close that Park felt the skin of his neck burn before he crossed the threshold, the shockwave closing behind them like a door slamming an instant too late.

"Rafa!" Mariana screamed, already trying to turn back, her voice coming apart around the name repeated over and over.

Park didn't let go of her. The heat now pouring out of the room wasn't the heat of something you could cross twice. He knew it before he had to see it clearly, and by the way Mariana's body stopped fighting him, she knew it in the same instant.

Báthory came out of the glare stumbling a few seconds later, alone, her clothes smoking, one arm pressed against the side where the fire had caught her. She didn't look at Park or Mariana. She didn't look back, toward where she'd left Rafa. She walked past the two of them toward the service stairs as if walking were the only thing she still knew how to do.

There was nothing left to say in that room. The fire was already climbing the wiring toward the ceiling, and staying one more second was a second death nobody needed.

---

They came out through the service gap coughing, all three of them — Park holding up Mariana, who kept looking back, toward the smoke still rising from the stairwell; Báthory two steps ahead, limping, her clothes still smoking on one side, one side of her face reddened by the heat. The street air, cold with November, still carrying the distant sound of the stampede breaking against the corners of the Baixa, was the first thing Park felt as real since he'd walked into that room.

It lasted only a second. Park saw her step into the light of a streetlamp, turn left, her face unreadable — no victory, no horror, only the stillness of someone who hasn't yet decided what to feel. She was limping less than she should have been, for someone who'd just walked out of an actual fire. A crowd of fleeing people passed between them, dense, shoving, someone shouting a name that belonged to no one present.

When the crowd cleared the street, Báthory was gone.

Park spun around, searched both ends of the street, the side alley, the metro entrance with its gate half-lowered. Nothing. No shadow moving where it shouldn't, no echo of footsteps rising above the rest. As if the smoke had carried her off with the exact same ease it had carried off Rafa, two minutes earlier, in the same room.

Mariana didn't look for her. She didn't seem to even notice she was gone. She let herself drop to her knees at the edge of the sidewalk, hands open in her lap, the candle that hadn't been lit for hours still clenched between the fingers of one hand, without Park remembering when he'd put it back there. She stared at the smoke without seeing anything past it.

Further down, toward the Baixa, the first sirens were starting to sound — firefighters, maybe, or something AMAT-PT itself had set in motion to give the city a story for a fire nobody would ever be able to fully explain. The rest of the Vigília kept dissolving down the street, voices and footsteps and the odd loose shout, entirely unaware of what had just happened three floors underground. No one in that crowd would ever know the name of the boy who'd stayed down there.

Park sat down beside her, without saying anything. There was nothing to say that wouldn't sound empty next to what had just happened in that room — and he still didn't fully understand, sitting there on that curb with his chest burning inside and his arm not quite feeling his fingers, whether what he'd just done counted as having won anything. He'd interrupted Báthory three times. He'd displaced the drum before it finished catching. And the room had still found a way to collect what it wanted anyway.

No one else had seen what happened down there. That, this time, didn't feel like a victory either.

At some point he'd have to write a report. He'd have to decide how much of what he'd seen in that room fit into the language AMAT used for these things, and how much would be left out, nameless, like everything else Park had been carrying since Hartford. For now he stayed sitting on the edge of that curb, with Mariana, while the smoke kept rising behind them and the whole city still didn't know what it had just lost.

---

AMAT-PT headquarters at four in the morning had the kind of silence that only exists after an entire building has stopped pretending it's still night by choice.

Ferreira wore his shoulder bandaged under his shirt, the arm in an improvised sling Salgado had made from whatever was at hand. Costa had half his face swollen, ice wrapped in a cloth against his jaw, and hadn't said a word since arriving. Vitória and Rui sat on the same couch, shoulder to shoulder, not needing to speak to know the other was still there. Filipa had reopened her laptop without anyone asking, the screen's light the only clear source in the room besides the lamp on Ferreira's desk.

Mariana sat in the chair farthest from the door, wrapped in a blanket someone had draped over her without her seeming to notice. She hadn't said a word since the street.

"The hospital confirms twenty Vigília injuries so far, none critical," Costa said, his voice muffled by the ice against his jaw. "Eight civilian awakenings registered, contained. The official version is a short circuit in the Santa Justa elevator that triggered panic in the crowd."

"And Rafa?" Park asked.

No one answered right away.

"There's no Rafa in any report," Ferreira said finally. "Not until Mariana decides whether she wants there to be."

Mariana didn't look up from her own hands.

It was Filipa who broke the silence that followed, her voice careful, like someone who knows what she's about to say can't wait for a better moment because there isn't going to be one.

"I have something," she said. "Security camera from a taxi stand, Praça do Comércio, forty minutes after the incident. A man with visible burns on his face gets into a car that takes him straight to the airport. The name on the three-twenty flight manifest isn't Báthory. But the passport he used to check in comes from a document pattern AMAT-HU has had flagged for years — the same kind of identity he used in Prague, in Vienna, in Marseille."

"Where's the flight going?" Park asked, though he already suspected the answer before Filipa finished saying it.

"Frankfurt first. Connection to Budapest at nine in the morning, local time."

Ferreira stared at the screen for a long moment, the same silence with which he'd stared at the map of Europe hours earlier, in another life.

"He's going home," he said. "Injured, with none of what he came for, and he's still going home."

Park thought of Cartwright, of the question nobody had asked out loud that night yet: how much of what had just happened counted, for protocol, as a victory. He looked at Mariana, who still hadn't raised her eyes, and understood that question would have to wait for a day when asking it hurt less.

Four hours. That was all the lead Báthory had on them toward a city none of them knew yet.
