# Chapter 107 — Arrival / Rules of the Game

---

The vans had been threading between buildings for a good while before Oliver understood he'd stopped recognizing anything about the landscape.

Milan, Reber had said, with no further explanation, as they boarded — the name of the city and nothing else, delivered with the same economy he used for anything else. It wasn't the massif. There was no bare rock, no thin air, none of the particular silence of altitude. There was traffic — a constant background noise, distant horns, a tram crossing two streets over with a metallic screech Oliver felt in his teeth. A man argued on the phone on the sidewalk without lowering his voice. A woman pushed a shopping cart with two kids hanging off the sides, completely oblivious to the row of unmarked vans that had just passed her. It smelled of hot asphalt, of something fried from a street stall, of a real city — layers of smell that St. Gotthard, sealed inside its mountain, had never had.

"This isn't a field exercise," Riku said, his face nearly pressed to the window. "This is a real city. With real people. Who know nothing about us."

"That's the idea," Pier said, without looking away from the glass. "That it stays that way, whatever happens this week."

No one answered that. The silence that followed carried a different weight than the usual silence of the vans — not the silence of saving energy before an exercise, but the silence of understanding, little by little, that this time the terrain itself wasn't controlled by anyone they knew.

---

They were led into a converted warehouse near a rail freight yard, containers stacked outside, industrial neon inside, the floor still stained with machine oil from equipment that was no longer there. All of St. Gotthard fit inside — more than two hundred candidates, standing, silent, the same discipline as always applied to a space that looked nothing like the hangar in the massif.

Reber was already waiting on an improvised platform, needing no microphone, the same voice as always carrying just as far.

"One week," he said, without preamble. "Starts tonight, at midnight sharp. Ends in seven days, same hour, not one minute more. You survive in this city, under these rules, and at the end we count. Twenty-five slots. Nothing more."

"The general Board doesn't stop this week," Reber went on. "It keeps running, and this counts toward it. Every badge you take off someone else raises your points. Not the same amount always — taking the badge off someone above you on the Board is worth much more than taking it off someone below you. It doesn't matter what method you use to get a badge. If an instructor catches you, you're out. Evading an active pursuit — not just staying unseen, but being seen and escaping — scores higher than hiding well the whole time. Reaching the end of the week with your own badge intact scores by itself, even if you haven't touched anyone. The top twenty-five on the Board at close, pass. No tie counts — whoever ends up in twenty-sixth place doesn't pass."

A murmur ran through the warehouse, muted almost instantly by the same discipline that had always contained it.

"It's not just about surviving each other, or us," Reber went on. "It's about surviving the city itself without the city finding out you're here. No one draws attention. No one leaves a trace a civilian remembers twice. One roof, two people — maximum. The whole group under the same roof gets noticed, gets remembered, becomes the weird neighbor on the fifth floor before the week is out. Split up tonight, not tomorrow. Whoever gets discovered, gets discovered alone — there's no containment team behind you cleaning up what you leave loose."

An instructor started handing out small boxes, one per candidate. Oliver opened his over his palm: the badge with his name engraved, yes, but underneath, in ordered layers, much more. A flat device, card-sized, with a screen that lit on its own at his touch — the Board, in miniature, portable, already updating with his own name in some slot he hadn't looked at yet. A tiny earpiece, short-range communication. A wad of bills in a currency that wasn't his own. And, at the bottom, an ID card with his face and a name that wasn't Oliver Webb.

"This is a real deployment," Nadia said, her voice dropping without her meaning it to. "Not an exercise with a safety net. They've given us money. A fake identity. Like we actually have to pass as someone else the whole week."

"Because that's exactly what it is," Asha said, already inspecting her own card with the same attention she gave any structure. "Half the test is the hunt. The other half is that no one outside this room notices the hunt is happening."

"And this," Riku said, turning the badge between his fingers, the underside showing the same institutional summary as Oliver's: Domain, estimated rank, a line of assessment that sounded like a closed file, not a game, "is what's actually on the line. Literally."

"Keeping it until the end," Oliver said, "means you get to keep it."

"And losing it," Dara said, not needing to finish the sentence.

No one finished it for her. No one needed to. Losing the badge wasn't just losing points. It was, in a literal way no one had announced out loud, letting someone else keep your spot — or letting the system itself confirm you weren't going to need it anymore.

"Last year it was fifty," Johanna said, from somewhere in the line beside them, not addressing anyone in particular, her voice completely flat. "This year, twenty-five. Don't ask why. No one explains the cuts."

No one asked her. The way she said it — no surprise, no outrage, like someone reciting a fact digested long ago — gave her an authority none of the people present had had to earn yet. She'd already done this. She knew what she was talking about.

---

That was when the third one arrived.

Reber and Anka were already on the platform, but until that moment no one had mentioned anyone else. The man who climbed the steps wore a black cassock, a clerical collar, a rosary visible at his belt that didn't look ornamental — everything about him said Church before he opened his mouth, and yet no one on the platform treated him like a guest. Reber ceded the spot to him without ceremony, the way someone yields to a superior rank without needing to be asked.

The air changed before he spoke. Nothing visible — no light, no gesture — but Oliver felt his chest tighten a degree, as if the very space around him had gained density without gaining volume. Beside him, Dara raised a hand to her sternum without noticing, and she wasn't the only one — a small movement rippled through the whole warehouse, two hundred people adjusting their breathing at the same time without deciding to.

"Father Andrea Conti," the man said, the Italian accent marked even in three words. "AMAT-Italy. I'll be hunting, like the rest of you."

He gave no further explanation. He glanced down for a moment at the front row, as if choosing something without hurry, and for a second it seemed like he was going to add more — but he only crossed himself, a quick, almost automatic gesture, and stepped back. The weight in the air didn't leave entirely with him. It stayed, lower, like background noise the body learned to ignore without ever forgetting it.

Reber picked up a second later, as if the interruption had been procedural, and finished explaining the rest of the rules — city perimeter marked without a physical map, off-limits zones they'd recognize on approach, communication between candidates permitted, between candidates and instructors not.

"You start when you walk out that door," Reber said, finally. "Not before."

---

They left in small groups, spaced out, toward streets Oliver wouldn't have been able to name even with a map in front of him. The noise of the city swallowed them almost immediately — a street market two blocks over, the smell of food he didn't recognize, someone arguing in Italian over a parking spot without either of them knowing that a hundred agents in training had just crossed the same street.

Before losing sight of them entirely among the buildings, Oliver caught a glimpse of how the other groups scattered. Johanna led hers with the same calm authority as always, pointing directions without needing to repeat them. Noa, a little further off, spoke quietly with two of hers, her head tilted toward a mental map only she seemed to have. Further back, the group Riku recognized without needing a second look — Iker among them, Yegor not, the rest of the fight club moving with a cohesion they never had at St. Gotthard, as if the city had suddenly given them a shared purpose the massif had never fully offered. And somewhere between Johanna's group leaving and another Oliver couldn't identify anyone in particular from, Dax walked out. Alone — not because no one followed him, but because he let no one.

"They look really organized," Dara said, following the same gaze.

"They've all had more practice time here than us," Pier said. "Deciding fast. We're going to have to learn that today."

---

They gathered one last time before midnight, on the roof of a half-finished building Pier had located without explaining how, the whole city stretched out below in lights that never fully went dark. Still as a full group, one last time that night.

"We need a plan that doesn't rely on luck," Asha said, an improvised map already drawn by hand on the concrete floor. "Areas to hunt, areas to hide, sleep shifts."

"And a place of our own, each of us, or in pairs," Pier said. "No all six of us under the same roof. You heard Reber."

Riku proposed rotating pairs for daytime hunting — no one alone more than a couple of hours straight, though each of them would sleep apart. Nadia suggested logging the instructors' patterns, the same filing discipline she'd carried since the Classification Room, applied now to something that actually mattered. Dara offered to use the Double as a decoy for the first attempts, spending the minimum until they saw how serious this was going to be.

"There's six of us," Asha said, at some point, almost in passing. "If we get enough badges between all of us, no one needs to win their own separately."

No one argued that yet. No one did the full math that night — how many slots were left, how many of them there were, exactly how much it was going to cost. It was just a sentence, said with the same calm confidence everything else had been said with that night, and for now that was enough.

They split up without much discussion, but not at random — Asha had marked a radius of a few blocks on the map, far enough apart not to look like a single group, close enough to run to if needed. Asha and Dara took an apartment building with too many staircases for anyone to keep track of who came and went. Nadia and Pier, two streets over, in a cheap boardinghouse where no one asked names. Oliver and Riku, closing the triangle, with a room over a shuttered mechanic's shop five minutes on foot from either of the other two, the smell of old grease seeping up through the wooden floor.

With each of them alert in their own room, the communicator open, the designated hour arrived. And nothing. Everyone could hear, from their own spot, a sigh from Pier that sounded more like relief than disappointment.

"This is good, it means tomorrow really starts—"

Somewhere several blocks away, Dara laughed for real, and for a second it was almost audible how her body doubled over on the bed, until Riku, awake enough for it to sound more true than interruption, cut in.

"So, a card game, since we're at it?"

Across the room, by the window, Oliver caught sight of, several blocks away, the silhouette of a man crossing a street completely alone, no one at his sides. He'd been switching the Registry on and off since eleven, he told himself, just in case. That man was Dax. Alone, the same way he'd left the ship that same morning. And, for a moment, he panicked at the idea that his cover had been blown.

He switched the Registry fully on, not halfway this time, and held it a second longer than necessary over the silhouette until the structure gave him back what he already knew. Dax. No one at his sides, no one behind him, walking with his usual pace, as if a street in Milan at midnight was no different from a hallway in the massif.

"Guys," he said, and the communicator cut off Dara's laughter mid-sound. "I just saw Dax. Alone, on the street. A few blocks from here."

A brief silence, different from the last one.

"Alone-alone, or just how he always walks?" Riku asked.

"I don't know if there's a difference tonight," Oliver said. "He's number one on the Board. If anyone's already started thinking about big targets, there's no one worth more than him. And we're the only ones who've seen him."

"That, or no one else has said it out loud yet," Nadia said, after a brief silence, the same tone she used for ordering any other piece of data. "If it's true, we're not the only heads thinking about this tonight. Just the only ones who've already said it over the comm."

"Which doesn't change anything we can do about it from here," Asha said, cutting in before the idea took the shape of a plan. "It's our first night. We don't even know if anything's really happening, or if Dax is just out walking like everyone who knows him says he does. Going out to check, just in case, on our first night, isn't a calculated risk. It's a hunch with legs."

"The hunch sounds pretty well-founded to me," Pier said, with no real intention of arguing, more like someone putting it on record. "But Asha's right about the rest. If something's happening out there, the six of us showing up all at once doesn't fix it. It just adds witnesses."

Dara said nothing for a long while, and when she spoke it was only one sentence, so low Oliver had to turn up the earpiece volume to catch all of it.

"Nobody notices me if I go take a look."

No one answered that either, but from the way the silence changed, Oliver knew Dara's offer wasn't going to come to anything that night — not because no one believed her, but because even she didn't seem too convinced she wanted to use it. And that was enough — Asha's proposal settled over the rest without anyone else pushing it further, and one by one the communicators went quiet, until only the room over the shop was left, and Riku, standing by the window with him.

"I'm coming with you," Riku said, before Oliver had said anything at all.

"I didn't say I was going."

"You didn't need to say it." Riku was already pulling his boots on. "If I leave you asleep, you go anyway. I'd rather come with you than have you go alone. And besides, if the thing about the number one on the Board is true, I'm not letting you find him by yourself."

Oliver felt something like guilt move behind his ribs, fast and without warning, and for a second thought about saying no, that Riku was wrong, that he'd never do something like that behind everyone's back. What he said, instead, was:

"I wasn't going to go alone."

"Fine," Riku said, not looking at him, already with a hand on the door handle.

Oliver agreed to go with a quick gesture, almost before he'd finished thinking it through, and the first thing he did was switch the Registry back on, searching for Dax within the radius still reachable. There he was, further away now, but there. Only then did he notice how fast he'd reacted — the same speed with which someone prepares who already knew, beforehand, that they were going to say yes. And he couldn't help wondering, following Riku down the stairs toward the street, whether what Riku had said about him wasn't, after all, entirely a lie.

---

They followed without getting too close, guiding themselves more by the Registry than by sight — Oliver kept it on in short bursts, a flicker every few streets, using Void the rest of the time, just enough to confirm direction without spending more than necessary, without giving away his position. Riku copied him almost by reflex. Dax walked without hurry, never looking back once, as if the possibility of being followed had never crossed his mind, or as if it didn't matter to him if it had.

"He's heading for the park," Riku said, quietly, tipping his chin at a dark patch between the buildings, two streets over. "No lights there."

They crossed the rest of the way hugging the walls, and it was Riku who stopped them first, a hand on Oliver's chest, before reaching the park entrance. Under the trees, in a clearing where the only light came from a broken streetlamp flickering at intervals, there was more than one silhouette. Four, Oliver counted, maybe five, arranged with no apparent order but with exactly the right distance between them — a semicircle, closing slowly around a sixth point that wasn't moving.

Dax.

He recognized Iker in profile, the same relaxed posture as always, and two more from the fight club whose names he didn't have, moving along the edge of the clearing like people who'd done this before and didn't need to talk to coordinate. Milan wasn't there. Neither was Yegor.

"They've seen us," Oliver whispered, though no one had turned toward them.

"No," Riku said. "Not yet."

But Dax had. Not toward them — toward the semicircle closing in, with the same exact calm he brought to anything else, as if five people surrounding him in a dark park were, to him, barely a minor variation on any afternoon in the pit. He didn't step back. He didn't look for a way out through the trees. He tilted his head, a small gesture, almost curious, and for an instant it looked like he was studying Iker more than Iker was studying him.

"You took a while," Dax said, and his voice carried clearly to where Oliver and Riku stood, without needing to raise it. "Thought it'd be sooner."

No one in the semicircle answered. Iker took a step forward, his jaw tight, and something in his posture — the fraction-of-a-second hesitation, the weight distributed wrong between his feet — said what his face didn't want to say.

Dax moved first.

Not toward Iker. Toward the nearer of the two nameless ones, closing the distance in two steps that didn't look hurried and that, even so, arrived before the kid finished raising his arms. The semicircle broke apart at once, five bodies reacting late to something that had already started, and Oliver, still by the park entrance, understood with a cold clarity that they hadn't gotten there in time to warn anyone of anything.

They had found them. Not the other way around.
