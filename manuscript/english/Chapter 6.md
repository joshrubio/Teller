# Chapter 6 — What He'd Already Decided


It took him eight years to go from knowing the number to having a plan that could do something about it.

They weren't eight lost years, though sometimes, looking back, he had trouble naming exactly where they'd gone. He learned to fix things more complicated than radios — a secondhand computer a neighbor was about to throw out, then another, then the public library's entire network, which they let him touch on Saturdays in exchange for not telling anyone that a sixteen-year-old knew more about it than the librarian in charge. He learned, without anyone teaching him in those words, the difference between a system that was broken and a system that only looked broken because no one had bothered to look at it calmly enough. At fifteen, he fixed the register at a corner store that had been out of service for months, and the owner, instead of paying him, gave him free access to a stack of technical manuals nobody else in the neighborhood wanted. He read all of them, twice, sitting in the attic his father had never gotten around to finishing into a room of his own. He didn't tell Gary. He'd learned, without anyone telling him that either, that sharing something good with his father almost always meant watching him try to turn it into a new business plan — and they'd already had enough business plans for one lifetime.

His father, who'd gotten his trucker's license a year after closing the shop — the road paid better than anything else he knew how to do with his hands — and spent three or four nights a week away, never asked where the extra money came from that sometimes turned up in the jar in the kitchen. Oliver decided, without arguing it out with himself any more than he had to, that it was better that way for both of them.

Gary, in those eight years, turned into a different man almost imperceptibly, the way a stair tread wears down: still kind, still laughing at his own jokes before anyone else got them, but he'd stopped proposing new plans, stopped talking about opening something else with his name on the door. Oliver saw him, on the nights his father stopped home between routes, sitting at the kitchen table with papers that weren't the shop's — rent bills, route sheets — never quite doing anything with them, just looking at them, as if looking were already, in itself, an exhausting kind of work. Oliver decided, at some point in those years, that his father had spent all the precision he had on a single attempt, and had none left over for another. He, on the other hand, still had plenty to spare.

At twenty-one, with a folder of his own — his, not his father's, bought new on purpose — he showed up at the Dunmore shop on a Tuesday afternoon, the same kind of day his father had chosen twenty-two years earlier, though neither of them knew it yet.

---

Farid Yamal had aged the way men age who never let anything show visibly: slowly, on the outside, with more years inside than his face let on. He looked at the kid in front of him with the same old reflex — age, clothes, how much of a hurry he was in — and took a full second, something rare for him, to match the face to a memory he hadn't needed in years.

"You have a safe you never let me weigh," Oliver said, with no greeting first, because he'd rehearsed the line long enough for it to sound more natural than it was.

Something shifted in Farid's expression — not quite a smile, closer to the look of someone who recognizes, against all odds, a number that adds up on the first try. "Gary Webb's son."

"Oliver."

"What do you want, Oliver?"

"A job. And I want my father's debt to stop growing while I pay it off, without him knowing it's me he's paying it to."

It wasn't the request of a nervous teenager. It was a proposal, with the same economy Farid would have used to close any deal in a lifetime of practice, and Oliver realized, watching the man's face across the counter, that this was exactly what he'd hoped to convey.

Farid took less time to decide than Oliver had calculated. "Do you actually know how to keep books, or just fix them once they're already broken?"

"Both."

"Start Monday. And you're not going to tell your father where you work."

It wasn't a question.

"Two hundred a week," Farid added, before Oliver could ask. "Forty goes against your father's debt, every week, no exceptions. The rest is yours, to do with as you want."

"How long would that take, at that rate?"

Farid did the math in his head, at his usual speed. "A little under three years, if you don't miss a single week."

"I won't miss one."

Farid didn't answer that. He didn't need to — he'd already decided, sometime in the last thirty seconds, with the same cold certainty he used to decide anything else worth deciding fast.

---

He worked the books for three different locations, rotating week to week, with no one but Farid fully knowing the real reason a twenty-one-year-old kept accounts with a precision that embarrassed employees twice his age. Part of his pay went into his own pocket, ordinary and accounted for; another part — set by Farid, no room for negotiation — disappeared straight against his father's six thousand dollars, with neither figure ever appearing on the same piece of paper. Oliver kept the parallel account in his head, updated every week, the same way he kept anything else that actually mattered to him in memory.

Silas, who by then ran the biggest location in the business and held a quiet authority over the younger employees, crossed paths with Oliver in the Foster Street storeroom one afternoon and looked at him longer than it took to ask him to pass a box.

"Yamal doesn't usually pay this much attention to somebody new," he said, apropos of nothing. "Let alone trust him with the books for three locations in his first year."

"I'm good at it," Oliver said, and left it there.

Silas nodded, like the answer confirmed something he'd already suspected rather than something that surprised him. "So was I, in the beginning." He didn't explain what he meant by that, and went back to work. Oliver was left with the sentence hanging there, not yet knowing that Silas had spent years piling up his own suspicions on a piece of paper no one else had seen.

Park Jun-ho showed up fourteen months later, on a summer Monday, with the kind of calm Oliver would only much later recognize as trained, not natural. They put him through the same thing Oliver had done his first year — minor appraisals, running between locations, the kind of work nobody fully explained until you got it wrong once and learned why the rule existed. They shared shifts, at first by chance, then because Farid, without ever saying why, started scheduling them together with a regularity neither of them questioned.

Park talked little, and when he did, he said less than it seemed. He had a way of checking the street before crossing it that didn't match anyone his age Oliver knew, and a way of standing completely still — none of the usual restlessness of standing around doing nothing — that Oliver, without a name for it yet, started noticing the way you notice things that don't fit a pattern you recognize.

They got along the way people get along who expect nothing from each other. Oliver made technical jokes nobody else understood; Park listened to them with a serious attention Oliver took months to recognize as the only way Park had of laughing at something.

One night, closing up the Dunmore shop, Oliver explained to Park, unprompted, why the old register always jammed at the same hour every afternoon — a capacitor losing charge in the heat, nothing fifteen dollars and half an hour couldn't fix, if anyone actually cared to fix it instead of hitting it whenever it stuck. Park listened to the whole thing without interrupting, and at the end asked only:

"So why don't you fix it?"

"Because Yamal doesn't care about a capacitor that fails twice a month. He cares that the register balances at the end of the day."

Park nodded slowly, like the answer meant something more than Oliver had intended. "That's a business too," he said. "Only fixing what makes noise."

One afternoon, a client who'd been turned down for a loan got violent at the Foster Street location. Park stepped between the man and the counter with a calm so absolute that the man seemed to lose the momentum of his own fury halfway through, as if something in Park's stance had told him, without a single word, that the fight he was about to start had already been decided before the first punch. No one got hurt. Park never mentioned it again, not that afternoon, not any afterward. Oliver did file it away — along with the way Park checked the street before crossing and the stillness that didn't match anyone his age — in the same mental drawer where he kept everything that still didn't have a full explanation.

---

The motorcycle showed up the same afternoon as the furious client in Foster. Oliver had stayed late at Dunmore, alone, cross-referencing on his laptop the closing numbers from the last two years on the three accounts Farid handled in person — a habit that had started out of boredom and had become, without his fully deciding it, almost a nightly ritual. They all closed the same way: never a hundred percent, always something very close to ninety, never further off that figure than rounding could explain. He was noting the third match on a separate sheet when a voice sounded right behind him, calm, almost casual.

"Doesn't that seem strange to you?"

"You almost killed me," Oliver said, closing the laptop faster than he needed to.

"You too. I thought everyone had already left." Park gave the barest shrug. "Where do you live? I'll give you a ride."

"I'll catch the eleven o'clock bus."

Park tilted his head, just slightly. "Buddy... it's eleven forty-five."

It took no more than that for him to accept.

The ride started without words. It was several streets later when Park spoke, without raising his voice, like someone thinking out loud more than talking to another person:

"Don't get fixated on things like that. Numbers fail all the time. Sometimes it's better to leave errors where they are."

"I guess." Oliver looked at the wet lights of the intersection, thinking about the sheet he'd just put away. "But it didn't look like a normal error."

Park took a few seconds. "In this business, almost nothing is. That's exactly why most people learn not to look too closely."

He stayed quiet for a whole traffic light after saying that, and when he spoke again it was in a different tone, lower, as if he were arguing something out with himself more than deciding it for Oliver.

"There are things in the world that don't have the explanation people think they have. People who see things they shouldn't be able to see. Things that happen that nobody ever files right, because they don't fit on any form that exists." He hesitated. "I shouldn't be telling you this."

"Why not?"

"Because it scares normal people without helping them any." The light changed. Park accelerated before finishing the sentence, as if he wanted the engine noise to carry off the last part. "And because people who aren't entirely normal — sometimes it changes them."

He didn't say anything else for the rest of the ride. Oliver didn't ask either, though the sentence kept turning over in his mind with the same insistence a badly closed system keeps demanding attention in the background long after you've stopped looking at it directly. He filed it where he filed everything that still had no place: in some mental drawer sorted by date, waiting for the rest of the context that would make it fit.

The motorcycle took the last curve and stopped in front of the house in Shelter 9 — two floors and an attic, aged brick, the faded GARY'S REPAIR sign that no one had bothered to take down.

"Repairs?" Park asked, looking at the front of the house.

"My father's old business. The usual story — it's easier to buy something new than fix something broken."

"And your father?"

"On the road. It pays more than this did."

Park looked at him for a moment from the seat. "You live somewhere quiet, and you've got a good job. That's a good life, Oliver. If I were you, I'd take it." Neither praise nor criticism — something in between that Oliver didn't know where to file. "Buy a motorcycle, get a girlfriend, and quit looking at columns that aren't yours to look at. See you tomorrow."

He'd disappeared at the next intersection before Oliver found an answer.

He stood a moment on the sidewalk, keys in hand, listening to the engine die away up the street. His father would be on the road that night, or already asleep if he'd stopped home between routes — no light was on except the porch light, which Gary had left burning out of habit for years now, not because he expected anyone to be waiting up.

His father had asked him, once, months back, what he was working at now that he wasn't home so many afternoons.

"Bookkeeping, for a couple of neighborhood businesses," Oliver said, not entirely lying — he'd learned from his own father, without meaning to, that same habit of explaining only the bare minimum.

Gary nodded, satisfied with the answer in a way that hurt Oliver more than he expected — not because his father was gullible, but because he'd gone years without asking too many questions about anything, as if he'd decided, sometime after the funeral, that looking closely at things only ever revealed how much worse they'd gotten. Oliver didn't correct him that night, or any other. Keeping the secret was, after all, the entire point.

---

He wouldn't know until much later how much of that night — the motorcycle, the silence, the half-finished sentence about people who see things they shouldn't — would turn out to be the real beginning of everything, more than anything that had happened at the Dunmore shop. For now, he was just a strange coworker who talked little, a man who could step between a furious client and a counter without his voice shaking, and an unfinished sentence that didn't fit any pattern Oliver recognized, no matter how much he turned it over.

That, and a number in his head that had already dropped below three thousand five hundred dollars — forty dollars a week, fifteen months down now, not one missed — which Oliver checked every night before sleep with the same exact precision his mother had once, without meaning to, taught him never to lose count of anything that actually mattered. He had, he calculated that night for the umpteenth time, a little more than a year and a half of work left before his father's debt stopped existing altogether — before he knew that the time he had left was never going to run out that quietly.
