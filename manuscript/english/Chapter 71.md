# Chapter 71 — The Old Man

---

The mission ended close to six in the morning, though Oliver had lost count before it did.

What remained after that: Riku at the center of the mass of worms, Park crouched beside him with the flashlight, Maren crossing the forest at a fast pace without anyone having to ask her to. And then the vehicles on the forest road, the second van's engine starting before they'd even shut the rear door, and the road home with its tight curves while the sky began to clear over the ridgelines — that early light that doesn't warm anything yet, only reveals. Yusuf drove. Oliver rode in the first van with Pier and Asha. Nobody talked.

Nobody had slept.

---

The village had a hundred forty permanent residents and a doctor who served as the regional emergency contact. Hana had called ahead from the field. The cover story was a training accident — vague enough not to need verification before seven in the morning.

Oliver didn't see Riku arrive at the clinic. He was on the other side of the street when they pulled the stretcher out of the van, and by the time he crossed, they'd already closed the inner door. What he did see was Riku's left arm as Park carried him past — the forearm bare, the glove lost somewhere in the forest, the skin showing no visible damage but with a quality the Registry caught before Oliver processed what he was looking at. The area from the elbow to the wrist had a different texture in the reading: the Odem there was quieter than it should be, like a channel that had given everything it had and hadn't refilled yet. Oliver looked away.

Maren went in with Riku. Hana followed her, phone still in hand. The rest of the team spread through the waiting room or stood by the door.

Pier found a chair in the corner and a cup of coffee and settled into that radius with the economy of someone who'd learned to wait in uncomfortable places. Dara fell asleep on a bench with her head on her crossed arms before Oliver finished sitting down. Nadia and Sabine talked quietly by the window. Asha stood, looking out at the street, arms crossed in the posture Oliver had come to identify as active analysis rather than cold.

Park wasn't in the waiting room. Oliver searched for him with the Registry — which worked, but with less resolution than usual, the radius costing more than it had at the start of the night — and located him somewhere in the building, still. Probably with Riku.

---

Voss arrived at nine.

Oliver watched him cross the street from the other side, no van in sight, with no one seeming to have brought him — simply present, as if the village had been part of his itinerary before the mission even existed. He went straight into the inner room without passing through the waiting area. Four minutes later he came out, spoke with Hana for three more, and then crossed the waiting room toward the exit.

Halfway there he stopped and looked at Oliver.

He didn't say anything. The look lasted two seconds — long enough for Oliver to understand that Voss had read Maren's preliminary report and had filed the fourteen-minute figure in the same folder as Riku's Stage 3. Then Voss kept walking toward the door.

Oliver understood: fourteen minutes was a documentable threshold. Riku's Stage 3 was also a documentable threshold. Right now, Riku's was the one that mattered more.

---

By ten in the morning he'd been three hours in the waiting room and the Registry still wouldn't settle.

It wasn't active reading — he'd had it in passive mode since they crossed the forest road. It was that every time the fatigue eased off a little, the field charged back up on its own with the density of the room: nine people with an active or recovering Domain, their signatures present even though nobody was doing anything with them. Odem under stress and exhaustion had a different frequency than Odem at rest. Oliver had learned to identify it without meaning to. It was like trying to ignore the hum of something that's been running too long.

He went outside.

The village square had three benches, a fountain, and a bar with its shutters closed until eleven. The morning had that alpine kind of cold that never quite commits — direct sun on the upper slopes, still shadow in the street. Oliver sat on the bench farthest from the medical building, facing the fountain, and let the Registry settle without forcing it.

He closed his eyes for a moment.

When he opened them, there was a man sitting two meters away.

---

The signature arrived before any other impression.

It wasn't active reading — it was that something at the edge of the passive layer didn't fit, and the Registry flagged it before Oliver decided to pay attention. The signature was wrong. Not the way a gap was wrong, which Oliver knew well from Grayhaven. Not the way an active Domain was, which always had a recognizable frequency even in someone new. This was something else: overlapping layers the Registry tried to resolve simultaneously and couldn't, as if the same file existed in twenty slightly different versions and the system tried to load all of them at once. The result wasn't cacophony. It was old. That was the only word that kept coming back whenever Oliver tried to classify it. The signature was older than the body carrying it.

Oliver looked at him.

The man looked to be around thirty-five. Functional clothes, no pretension. Brown hair, a cut with no particular memory to it. He was watching the fountain with the posture of someone who'd been on that bench longer than Oliver had been in the square.

Then he looked at Oliver.

"Trouble with the water?" he said in French, gesturing minimally at the fountain. The accent was central European. Not local.

Oliver took a second.

"I don't know."

The man nodded as if that were answer enough.

---

He didn't say anything else for a moment. Oliver let the Registry keep trying to resolve the signature. It still wouldn't load — still stuck in that loop of overlapping layers that never cancelled out. As if the format were familiar but the codec wasn't.

The man ordered something from the bartender, who'd opened one of the bar's windows — coffee, apparently. He turned back to Oliver.

"I pass through here now and then. There's something about this part of the Valais."

"What do you mean?"

"People come through and end up staying longer than planned." A minimal pause, no emphasis. "Martin."

He gave a last name. Oliver registered it and couldn't recover it a second later — not like something forgotten, but like something that hadn't fully landed. He gave his own.

"And what brings you here?" Martin glanced at the van parked in front of the clinic, without pointing at it directly. "Looks like it's been a long night."

"Training," Oliver said. The same cover Hana used.

"Sure."

The tone wasn't ironic. It was the tone of someone who accepts the answer without believing it and decides that doesn't change the conversation.

"Do you have one?" Oliver said. It was direct, and he knew it. But the signature still wasn't resolving, and the direct question was the only path left.

Martin tilted his head slightly.

"One of what?"

"A Domain."

The word shouldn't be used outside the Center. Martin didn't blink, didn't tense, didn't show the small dislocation people had when they heard terminology they didn't recognize.

"Yes," he said.

---

Neither of them spoke for a moment.

Oliver read the signature again. The layers kept overlapping. The outer layer was consistent with the man's apparent age — thirty-something, no visible active tension. But underneath there was another. And under that, another still. The number of layers he could distinguish before the Registry lost resolution was higher than in anyone he'd ever read. As if someone had printed the same document thirty times on top of the last, and the ink from the first copy was still visible through everything else.

"The fatigue after sustained reading," Martin said, with no change of tone, no visible transition to justify it. "It isn't like physical fatigue. It doesn't hurt that way. It's closer to silence. As if a signal had been present for so long you'd stopped registering it as a signal, until it stops."

Oliver didn't answer.

"And when the silence arrives," Martin went on, "there's a moment where the line between what you see and what you are is less clear than it was before you started."

Nobody had described that to him. Not Maren, not Pier, not Maren's protocol, not six months of sessions. It was too specific to be speculation.

"How do you know that?" Oliver said.

"I've had time to observe it." He said it like a minor fact. "In myself and in others."

"How much time?"

Martin looked at his own hands. A brief gesture — not Riku's kind, searching for the origin of something, but the kind of someone checking that something he already knows is there, is there.

"Enough that what I just described feels completely abstract to me now. Like trying to remember what it felt like not to know how to read."

Oliver let that settle.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because nobody else is going to." No drama. No added weight. "People with that kind of information either don't have it in articulable terms, or they have it and prefer not to give it to you because it changes how you use what you've got. And there's an advantage in having someone who's already lived it name things for you before you get there on your own."

Oliver thought about Voss looking at him for two seconds in the waiting room. About Maren documenting the fourteen-minute threshold as one more data point in a report headed to Central Evaluation.

"I'm not saying the system is lying to you," Martin added. "I'm saying some things don't get learned in a protocol. They get learned by living with them long enough."

---

The light had dropped a degree since Oliver had stepped outside. From inside came the muffled sound of someone on the phone — probably Hana.

"There's someone in your group," Martin said then, "whose evaluation regime isn't evaluation. It's management of something the system doesn't know how to read from outside."

He didn't say the name.

Oliver went still.

"I've seen earlier versions of that protocol. Different institution, same logic: when you can't evaluate something, you contain it." A pause, exactly three seconds. "And the person inside that has been in that kind of system longer than anyone should have to be."

"Do you know her?" The question came out sharper than he meant it to.

"Not personally." The precision was deliberate. "I know her situation. I know how the system works with profiles like hers."

Oliver said nothing.

He'd spent weeks not naming what Martin had just named. Sisi's protocol was more restrictive than anyone else's at the Center — no unsupervised outings, no sessions without staff present, no unmonitored communication. The reasons he'd been given were coherent. They were still coherent right now. And they still felt insufficient in the same way they'd felt insufficient the first day and every day since, only Oliver hadn't found the exact phrase for it until a stranger with an impossible signature said it out loud in the square of a village whose name he didn't know.

"I'm not asking you to do anything," Martin said. "I'm just naming what you already know."

---

He stood without signaling that the conversation was over. He left money on the bar's counter — more than the coffee cost, without ostentation. He headed up the street that climbed north.

Oliver followed the signature in the Registry until the line of sight cut off at the angle of a building. Then he kept trying to read it at a distance, knowing the radius wouldn't reach. The signature faded — not with the predictable curve of something moving away in a straight line. It shut down faster than it should have. Gradual at first, then almost vertical. Like a process actively retreating toward a minimum-emission state.

Oliver didn't move for several minutes.

He had a name — Martin — that was probably wrong. A last name that hadn't landed. A signature he couldn't classify with any vocabulary available to him after six months of sessions. And a conversation that had contained more operationally relevant information than anything he'd heard at the Center since he arrived.

He should tell Park. Or Pier. The signature, the description, the false name — all of it was information someone with more context could place. And if he told Voss, the system would do what the system did with information, and the conversation in the square would become a thread in a file on some server Oliver would never hear about again.

What kept him on the bench wasn't the signature.

It was that Martin had named exactly what Oliver had spent weeks managing as if it had no name. And naming something out loud, even in a stranger's mouth, made it harder to keep treating as an abstraction.

He went back inside.

---

Park was back in the waiting room. He looked up when Oliver opened the door — not a question, but not neutral either.

"Air," Oliver said.

Park nodded.

Pier was still in his corner. Dara was still asleep. Asha had switched windows. Hana's phone had stopped ringing.

At half past twelve, Maren opened the inner door.

She looked at the group. Then she looked specifically at Oliver, with the attention of someone confirming a data point rather than seeking contact.

"He's awake," she said. "The signature's stabilizing."

And she went back in.

Oliver looked at the closed door. He thought about Riku's left arm. About what had come out of him in the forest. About what it meant to have carried something for two years without knowing exactly what it was, and to find the conditions that made it necessary all at once, from the inside, surrounded by whatever the creature had been.

He thought about a thirty-five-year-old man with a signature decades deep who described that as if he remembered it firsthand.

He didn't say anything.
