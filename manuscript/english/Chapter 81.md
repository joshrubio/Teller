# Chapter 81 — What's Asked of You

---

Maren was waiting for them in a windowless room, smaller than the orientation room, with seven chairs arranged in a semicircle instead of rows — the first design decision since they'd arrived that seemed thought out for them in particular, not for the building in general.

"I know the welcome's been... economical," she said, once the seven of them were seated. "I'm going to try to make up for it a little."

Beside her, standing with her arms crossed and a tablet she hadn't looked at once since Oliver walked in, was Hana, the same dry voice behind the vans and schedules that had made it possible for them to get here without anyone having to think about the logistics. Nothing about her had changed since the Valais Center. She had the same cutting calm as always, more used to things going wrong than to things going right, and the same habit of fixing it without making a show of it.

"Both of us have been reassigned," Maren said. "We no longer cover the Valais Center. We cover this. Specifically, we cover you."

"Just us?" Petra said.

"Just you," Hana said, no preamble. "There are over a hundred candidates in this complex. You're our responsibility specifically. That means if something goes wrong, we're the first ones they ask."

Oliver noticed Riku, beside him, straighten a little in his chair at that — he didn't know yet whether it was relief or the added weight of two specific people carrying them.

"I want to be clear about one thing before we go on," Maren said. "This center isn't what you're probably imagining after this morning. It's not a classification-agent factory, it's not an official program with a name on any organizational chart you're going to find by looking. St. Gotthard trains people for the cases the normal system can't afford to handle through standard channels — too dangerous, too urgent to wait for the paperwork. Unofficial, in practice, even if nobody says that word out loud."

"And that's why we're here?" Dara said. "Because we're that kind of case?"

"You're here because you're already that kind of case, whether you like it or not," Maren said. "All the time you spent at the Valais Center, they never gave you a formal framework. The Incursion didn't either. What happened there proved the system still doesn't have anywhere to put you, and in the meantime you keep operating, keep being useful, and keep being a risk nobody has formally decided how to manage. This," she said, gesturing vaguely at the bare walls, "is the way someone decided it was better to manage it than to leave it unmanaged entirely."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Pier was the first to break the silence.

"Who decided that?" he said. "Voss?"

"Voss proposed it," Hana said. "She didn't decide it alone. There's a council in Geneva that still hasn't approved anything definitively. Voss is there right now, fighting that part. We handle this one."

Oliver felt something lodge itself in his head — a council, Geneva, something undecided — but he didn't say it out loud. He filed the information away the same way he filed a passive signature he still didn't fully know how to interpret.

"And Yusuf?" Riku said. "We didn't see him get in the cars."

Maren and Hana exchanged a brief look, the kind that confirms a question was already expected.

"Yusuf is following a trail on his own," Maren said. "I can't give you more detail than that, not because we don't trust you, but because the fewer people who know exactly where he is, the less risk he runs. He's going to be off the program for a while."

"How long is a while?" Nadia said.

"I don't know," Maren said, with the plain honesty Oliver already recognized as her way of dealing with them. "And I'd rather say I don't know than invent a number that doesn't mean anything."

"And if we decide we don't want to be managed this way?" Petra said, her voice controlled but with an edge underneath that Oliver recognized from the tense meetings at the Valais Center. "We saw the door this morning. They explained it in full detail."

"Then you cross it," Hana said, without hesitating. "Nobody's going to talk you out of it, and it's not my job to try. My job is that if you stay, you stay knowing exactly what you're getting into. That's what we're doing right now."

"And if we leave?" Petra said. "What happens to the rest? To the ones who stay?"

"Nothing," Maren said. "Every decision is individual. Nobody here carries the weight of someone else's. That's different from the Valais Center too, if it helps to know that."

Pier had been quiet since his question about Voss, arms crossed, the expression of someone calculating something he hadn't decided yet to share.

"How long do we have to decide?" he said, finally.

"As long as you need," Maren said. "But the longer you wait, the more training you lose, and the training doesn't come back afterward. That's not a threat. It's arithmetic."

Nobody answered that right away. Hana let the silence settle for a moment before continuing, as if she knew exactly how much silence it took for the information to sink in without anyone feeling pressured to fill it with one more question.

The meeting ended without anyone announcing that it was ending — Hana looked at the tablet for the first time, said something about the next morning's schedule, and the group understood, with the same silent synchrony they were already starting to share without meaning to, that it was time to go.

---

That night, Oliver stayed awake long after Riku stopped pretending he was still reading something on his phone with no signal.

Room 12 was quiet, but not the empty quiet of the Valais Center — this one had texture, a constant background hum Oliver had stopped consciously noticing around the half-hour mark and that now, in the stillness of the night, made itself present again. He closed his eyes and let the Registry activate without running anything, just listening — Riku's passive signature, asleep, steady, no jolts; further out, something dense and constant that could only be the building itself, generations of accumulated Odem in stone that had spent decades absorbing whatever happened inside it. It wasn't an active reading. It was simply being awake in a place with more history than anyone had told him about yet.

The phone buzzed against the nightstand, once, a message that took a second to fully load because of the weak signal filtering in from some repeater the mountain couldn't quite block.

*Still keeping up with the Chukgi?*

Oliver read the message twice without fully understanding it. He didn't recognize the word — Korean, clearly, something that sounded like the kind of term Park would use without thinking it needed translating.

*What's Chukgi?* he wrote.

The answer took several minutes, long enough that Oliver started to wonder if Park was in motion, somewhere writing wasn't safe.

*The gym sequence. The one you copied off me that afternoon. Thought you were still practicing it.*

Oliver stared at the ceiling for a moment. Of course he remembered it. Not the empty movements he'd copied before, with no mechanism behind them — this was different, and he knew it because he'd lived it in his own body: fifteen minutes of real accumulation, the vibration in his hand that wasn't a tremor, the fear before it had a name, and Park's voice from the far end of the gym, *go back to the start, one step, slow*, the only instruction he'd needed for the weight to find a channel again.

*Haven't tried it again,* he wrote. *Not without you there.*

The answer took longer this time.

*Smart, most days. But you're going to need to be able to do it alone. Start slow. If it stratifies, stop before minute ten, not fifteen like last time. And remember the exit before you need it, not once you already do.*

There was no explanation for why he was asking that particular night, or where he was, or what he was doing. Oliver got up from the bunk without waking Riku, found the free space between the door and the closet — barely a meter and a half, enough — and started.

Weight shift. Torso rotation. Lateral change of stance.

At first he felt nothing, and for a moment he wondered whether the copy had degraded over the weeks without practice, the way Voss said it did with copies nobody maintained. But at four or five minutes it arrived, exactly like the first time — not gradual, a point where the pattern crossed over from memory into something that began to load into him. The weight of his own body, half a second behind every movement. The lag recognizable, almost familiar this time, though the fear was still there, smaller, waiting to see if it had reason to grow.

He counted the minutes without checking the clock, counting steps in its place, each repetition a number filed away without deciding to. At eight minutes, when the load began stratifying in earnest, he forced himself to stop — not at ten, at eight, with plenty of margin, exactly as Park had asked.

He braced against the wall with a flat palm, waiting for the vibration.

It didn't fully arrive. A minor tremor, controllable, something he recognized without needing anyone to tell him what to do with it. He took a step forward, slowly, shifting his weight, and felt the lag dissolve like water finding a channel it already knew.

He stood a moment longer, breath still high, something like pride mixed in with the relief of not having had to use it.

*Eight minutes. Stopped before it got ugly,* he wrote.

*Good,* Park answered, and added nothing else, which, coming from him, was the kind of approval Oliver had learned to value more than any long compliment.

He lay down with the phone still in his hand, not turning it fully off, as if some part of him wanted to leave the door open in case Park wrote again.

---

The morning session started before the real sky — the one that existed outside the mountain, the one none of them had seen since arriving — had a chance to fully clear.

They were taken out through a side access Oliver hadn't seen the day before, to a cleared stretch of ground between two ridges, sheltered enough from the wind to make the cold bearable but not comfortable. Other groups were already there, spread out in blocks with the same tribal geometry Oliver had noticed in the mess hall.

Dax's group occupied one end, already warming up with a discipline that didn't need anyone to order it. Among them, Oliver recognized a man with a Spanish accent he'd glimpsed in the mess hall the night before — quieter than the rest, but watching Dax with a fixed, unbroken attention, as if he were memorizing something beyond the exercises. Riku, following the same gaze, pointed it out without needing words: arms moving with an almost electric precision, an almost imperceptible sparking at the fingertips that vanished the moment Oliver tried to fix his eyes on it.

Johanna's group, closer to the center, had fewer people and less noise — her out front, setting the pace of stretches the rest followed without anyone asking why she led. A dark-skinned woman, calm even in the cold, quietly corrected another candidate's posture without the instructor having to step in — the same kind of silent authority as Johanna herself, though warmer, less cutting.

"Valais group, here," Hana said, pointing to a spot clearly set apart from the others, and Oliver felt the name settle over them like a label he wasn't going to be able to remove anymore.

An instructor different from the one at the pit — younger, with the kind of energy that suggested he hadn't been in the job long — started giving basic instructions, and it was in the pause between two sets, while the group caught its breath, that Oliver caught fragments of a conversation not meant for him to hear.

"...the baseline's too low," Hana was saying, a few meters off, talking to another instructor without bothering to lower her voice fully. "Compared to Dax's group they're, what, half?"

"Less," the instructor said. "Dax's group has been here weeks. These arrived yesterday. It's not fair to compare them yet."

"I'm not asking for it to be fair," Hana said. "I'm asking for a real number for the report."

Riku, beside him, had heard the same thing. Oliver noticed it in the way he kept stretching a second longer than necessary.

"Half of what?" Riku murmured.

"I don't know," Oliver said. "But I don't think it's a number we're going to like."

Across the clearing, Dax's group finished their warm-up set with a synchrony that didn't look rehearsed, only learned through shared repetition, and for a second, before the instructor called them back to formation, Dax looked toward the Valais group with the same cold assessment from the mess hall — this time lingering an instant longer on Oliver than on anyone else.

Oliver held the look. This time, he wasn't the first to break it.

The young instructor gave a sharp clap, the signal to resume the set, and the moment dissolved without anyone else in the group seeming to notice. Dara, behind Oliver, was still trying to catch his breath from the previous round; Asha had her palms up on her knees, crouched, the same gesture as always, now with the vapor of her breath marking the rhythm in the cold air.

"Half," Riku repeated, more to himself than to Oliver, as they got back into position. "I mean, okay. It's information. We can work with information."

"We can," Oliver said, not sure he fully believed it himself.

They started the next set. The mountain cold forgave nothing, and somewhere between the third and fourth repetition, Oliver stopped thinking about Dax, about the number Hana hadn't wanted to give them, about the folder Voss was carrying to Geneva without them knowing yet the exact weight of what depended on it. Only the body was left, the effort, and the cold certainty that whatever this place expected of them, they weren't anywhere close to giving it yet.
