# Chapter 60 — Contours

In the morning, before getting up, Oliver ran the inventory.

It wasn't a new ritual — he'd been doing it for weeks without naming it. But that morning he did it with intention, slowly, the way Maren had taught him to run through a reading: without forcing it, without anticipating, letting the room's Odem arrive on its own.

The contours of the bed. The ceiling. The window, with January light filtering through the closed blinds, white and directionless, the kind of light that in the Valais winter arrived already spent by the peaks before it touched the Center's walls. The armchair in the corner with yesterday's clothes on it — precise contours, loaded. The notebook on the table, closed, with the underlined note he hadn't reread but hadn't forgotten either.

*Seeing the signature ≠ reading the structure.*

That much he knew. What he didn't know yet was where the signature ended and the structure began — whether there was a line, or whether it was closer to adjusting a camera lens: same object, same eye, but the level of detail depended on how much deliberate attention sat behind it.

He sat up. Stretched his left hand, spread his fingers over the desk.

Riku's Static lived in him like a frequency he could turn up or down but never fully switch off. Compression — his fingers tensed slightly, the interference field in a twenty-centimeter radius grew denser, the imperceptible hum of the lamp across the desk cut out — and release. The hum came back. Simple. He'd had it practicable for weeks.

What he couldn't do was read his own Odem from the inside.

He knew the Registry existed — knew it worked, that it copied, that the cost was real and the result imperfect. He knew it because he'd seen it from outside, in what it produced, in what Maren documented. But reading the Registry's own structure was like trying to see the eye he saw with. The tool didn't read itself. It was used.

He wrote that down too. Under the underlined line from the night before.

---

The Valais Center occupied a stone building at the bottom of a side valley in the canton, fourteen kilometers from the main road and fourteen hundred meters up. The stone matched the valley — quarried from the same ground the building sat on, worked into the walls so thoroughly that from the road below it was hard to tell where the terrain ended and the construction began. What the building had been before AMAT claimed it showed in the details nobody had needed to change: the north corridor's arches, too ornamental for functional use, the ground floor's high ceilings, the wide windows facing the valley with the specific proportions of someone who'd built the place to make the mountain part of the interior. Someone, at some point before the Center existed, had wanted this place to be beautiful.

The main building had three stories, with the valley stone worked into the walls so thoroughly that from the road below it was hard to tell where the terrain ended and the construction began. Ground floor: common areas, dining hall, the theory room in the north wing, the administrative corridor. First floor: subject rooms, east and west residential wings. Second floor: staff offices, the archive, the evaluation lab where Maren spent most mornings. The inner courtyard sat between the east and north wings — stone, roofless, visible from the first-floor corridor — and there was another outdoor space at the back of the building, the east courtyard, bigger, with dark wood benches and the uneven stone floor Oliver knew well by now.

The first-floor north corridor had four windows facing the valley. Not the inner courtyard — the real outdoors: the slope running down toward the access road, the fir trees lining its edges, the mountain sky that in January was almost always pale gray or white, rarely blue. That was where you'd see any vehicle arrive.

Oliver was at that window at a quarter past eleven Thursday morning when the black SUV appeared on the straight stretch of the access road, about two hundred meters from the building.

---

It wasn't the first AMAT vehicle Oliver had watched arrive at the Center. Others had come — supplies, technicians from Geneva, once a field agent who'd stayed one night and vanished before breakfast. None of them had brought Voss back from outside.

Voss got out of the passenger seat first. That alone was unusual — Voss didn't leave the Center without a documented reason, and Oliver hadn't seen him in any vehicle for weeks. Then the driver, a Geneva agent Oliver recognized from the administrative corridor but couldn't name.

And then, from the back door, her.

Oliver watched her from the window, far enough away that the details arrived in layers. First the contours — the Odem of someone present, whole, no gaps. Ordinary. Then the figure: not tall, a dark coat too large for her frame, a small bag held with both hands in front of her body as if the weight mattered less than having something to hold onto. And the hair — long, very long, down to her waist, wavy with the texture of hair that hasn't been tamed but simply let grow, a dark brown that in the January light leaned almost to red, falling over one side and covering part of her face.

She didn't look at the building when she got out. She looked at the ground, then the fir trees, then the sky for a second — one exact second, as if checking it was still there — and then followed Voss toward the entrance without gesturing, without speaking, with the economy of movement of someone who's learned that moving less means exposing less.

Oliver read all of that in the time it took the group to cross the twenty meters between the vehicle and the door.

And then he tried to read more.

Her Odem was clear — the signature of existence, defined contours, the normal density of someone carrying Odem with no visible wear. But underneath that, where in any other subject at the Center there was something more — a frequency, a background tension, the passive footprint of a Domain even at rest — there was nothing.

Not absence. Not a gap. Just... no signal. Like a system switched on that emitted nothing.

Oliver stayed at the window until they disappeared through the entrance, and it took him a moment to realize he'd been reading from the instant he saw her step out of the vehicle, without ever deciding to.

---

That was what Maren had told him in one of their first sessions, back when the Registry still felt like something that happened to him rather than something he did: *passive reading has no switch*. He'd taken that as a technical limit — the system always on because he hadn't learned to turn it off. But sitting in the north corridor with the window empty now and the fir trees still on the slope, Oliver thought maybe that wasn't quite it.

It was more like a kid who hasn't learned that staring at people is rude. It's not that he can't look away — it's that he hasn't internalized yet that he should. The impulse to read arrived before the thought that reading was a choice. And that, sixteen weeks after the first involuntary copy, wasn't a technical limit. It was a habit.

He wrote it down. *Passive reading = habit, not just a limit. When did I stop choosing it?*

He closed the notebook, tucked it into his jacket pocket, and went down to the dining hall.

---

Voss introduced her at a quarter past twelve, in the dining hall, with most of the Center present.

It wasn't a speech. Voss didn't give speeches — he made statements with the minimum number of words needed to produce the required effect.

"Isabel." He said the name with the intonation of someone who's confirmed it's the name she uses. "She's joining the evaluation program. Starting today she shares the Center's common spaces."

That was all.

She stood beside him, coat still on, hair falling over the right side of her face, eyes fixed somewhere between the nearest table and the floor. She wasn't looking at anyone directly. It wasn't shyness — Oliver could tell the difference by now, seventeen weeks learning to read people as well as Odem — it was closer to calibration: she was taking in the room, measuring it, calculating how much of herself was safe to give before she knew what everyone in it was.

Riku, on Oliver's left, said nothing. Dara, across the table, watched her with the direct attention Oliver had always read as evaluation but now thought was closer to classification: problem or not a problem, known variable or unknown?

Pier nodded from his chair with the economy of someone who's watched too many new people arrive and learned that the newcomer's first impression matters more than the one already there.

Oliver didn't say anything either. He looked at her — he couldn't help it, or didn't decide not to, which was different — and searched for the signature he found in everyone else. Riku: the background EM oscillation, always present, like hearing a radio playing downstairs. Dara: the double density, the Odem with that latent-duplication edge to it. Pier: the specific weight of someone who controls local gravity, which showed up in how gravity seemed to decide where he was instead of the other way around.

Her: crisp contours. Whole Odem. And then nothing more.

As if whatever she did left no trace in the space she occupied.

As if she operated somewhere Oliver couldn't look.

He stopped trying. Not because he'd decided it was rude — that thought would come later, when he had time for the kind of reflection that didn't happen in a dining hall with Voss standing next to someone who clearly preferred not to be watched. He stopped because chasing something that wasn't there was a waste of time, and because she raised her eyes for an instant and looked toward his side of the table before dropping back to the spot between the table and the floor.

She wasn't looking at him specifically. But it was close.

---

Before going down to the dining hall, Oliver had found Pier in the first-floor corridor, near the window where he'd watched the vehicle arrive.

"Isabel," Oliver said.

"Sisi," Pier said, without turning his head. "That's what they call her."

"Where's she from?"

Pier picked up the coffee he'd left on the windowsill.

"United Kingdom. Months in containment." A brief pause. "The British system doesn't draw much of a line between preventive custody and indefinite custody when it doesn't know what it's got on its hands."

Oliver looked at the window. The valley, the fir trees, the access road already empty.

"And Voss went to get her?"

"Voss went to get her," Pier confirmed, in the neutral tone of someone describing a fact without offering an opinion on it. "Not everyone who ends up here comes because they want to. Some come because the alternatives were worse." He picked up the coffee and straightened. "The Center has the advantage of being honest about what it is."

He continued down the corridor without adding anything else.

Oliver stayed at the window a moment. The afternoon was closing in, the January light retreating before four the way it always did, and the valley below the access road was already a dark smear between the snow and the sky.

---

Hana's session was at two. Classification by activation cost — what she'd promised on Tuesday. Nadia took three pages of notes. Lev took none and answered two questions with the precision of someone who's studied the subject on his own for years. Oliver occupied the space between the two of them: enough to follow the thread, enough to know what to ask.

Yusuf's session was at four, in the inner courtyard.

Yusuf waited for them standing at the center of the courtyard, hands behind his back, wearing the expression Oliver had already learned to read as *today's program doesn't look like last week's*. Riku, Dara, Lev, and Pier were already there. Oliver arrived last, thirty seconds before the hour, and Yusuf said nothing but wrote something on the clipboard he was carrying.

"Circuit," Yusuf said. "Three rounds. Rotate on my signal."

There was no warm-up. No explanation of the objective.

The first station was load-carrying — water jugs at the north end of the courtyard, fifteen kilos each, four trips in two minutes. Simple, physical, no Domain. Pier did it with his usual economy of movement, the weight of the jugs distributing itself in a way Oliver suspected wasn't entirely natural. Lev finished before anyone and waited without breaking a sweat. Dara was last, and when Yusuf called out "next," she still had the second jug in her hands.

The second station was where the Domain came in.

Oliver had read Lev during the first round without meaning to. The Heat signature was unlike anything else at the Center — not an oscillation, not a compressed density, but something closer to a very slow current, so integrated into Lev's Odem pattern that if Oliver hadn't known to look for it, he would have missed it entirely. The air immediately around Lev ran a degree or two colder than the rest of the courtyard — passive extraction, the Domain adjusting the environment without Lev appearing to decide it. Fourteen kilos of jug in his hands, and the signature didn't even flicker.

It was the Domain of someone who'd used it long enough that it had stopped being an act and become simply a condition.

"Hayashi," Yusuf said. "Field active the whole circuit. If it drops, restart."

Riku ran the twenty meters of the courtyard with Static active, Maren's measuring equipment on the second-floor windowsill logging the interference in real time. He dropped it in the second round, at the fifteen-meter mark, when the physical strain compressed his control. He restarted it. He made it to the end.

For Oliver, Yusuf gave no Domain instructions.

"Reading in motion," he said simply, and pointed at Pier.

Oliver took a second to understand what was being asked of him. Then he ran alongside Pier — not against him, with him — and tried to read the Weight signature while the body was under physical load. It was different from reading at rest, different from reading from a window or a theory-room chair. Movement introduced noise. Pier's signature was still there, but the attention threshold needed to separate structure from background was higher, like trying to follow one specific conversation in a room where everyone had turned up the volume.

In the third round, over the last ten meters, he had it clear for four seconds.

Four seconds of clean reading under physical load. It wasn't much.

Yusuf wrote it down.

When they finished, the courtyard had that particular stillness of January cold after exertion — breath steaming, stone damp, the sky already dark gray at a quarter past five. Riku sat down right on the ground. Dara leaned against the north wall, arms crossed, watching Yusuf with the expression of someone still deciding whether what just happened was an insult or a challenge. Lev left without a word, as always.

Oliver was still stretching his shoulders when he saw them.

Sisi was crossing the glassed-in walkway connecting the residential wing to the administrative wing — the one that ran over the inner courtyard, visible from below through the partially transparent roof. She walked with her hands in her coat pockets, hair falling over the right side of her face, and beside her Maren carried a thin folder and talked without gesturing.

They didn't come down to the courtyard. They continued toward the administrative wing.

Oliver followed them with his eyes until they disappeared down the corridor.

Her tests were starting.

He stayed in the courtyard a moment longer. The cold was dry, mountain cold, the kind that didn't hurt so much as simply occupy space. Riku had already gone in. So had Dara. The courtyard was just stone and snow and the steam of his own breath.

He thought about the note in his notebook — *passive reading = habit* — and then about Park's signature in the snowy courtyard the day before, building with no exit, and then about that afternoon: four seconds of clean reading under physical load. Four seconds. Seventeen weeks at the Center, and the attention threshold still climbed whenever the body was under load.

What didn't climb was Lev's signature. Not during the circuit, not under load, not when he finished and walked off. The passive Heat extraction had stayed exactly the same through the whole session — no fluctuation, no visible effort, none of the shift in level Oliver noticed in everyone else when a Domain moved from latent to active. As if Lev didn't have two states. Just one, continuous, so integrated the body no longer distinguished it from the rest.

He'd been like that for years, according to the file Pier had summarized for him without calling it a file. Years managing alone, before the Center, before AMAT. Enough time for the Domain to stop being something he did and become something he was.

Oliver didn't know if that was what waited for him. Didn't know if it was what he wanted.

But he did want to ask how Lev had gotten there.

He crossed toward the inner corridor just as Lev came out of the north-wing washroom — jacket, hands in his pockets, the usual expression, which wasn't closed off so much as simply giving nothing extra away. Lev looked at him for a second. Oliver opened his mouth.

"Tomorrow," Lev said, before Oliver could say anything.

And kept walking down the corridor.
