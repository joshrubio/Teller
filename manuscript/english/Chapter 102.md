# Chapter 102 — What Rots

---

Oliver woke before anything sounded. The bunk below creaked once as Riku turned over without fully waking, his breathing slow and even, the breathing of someone who'd actually slept — something Oliver hadn't managed since the wall. He lay there a while, looking at the ceiling, thinking about Petra two floors down, shoulder still immobilized, probably as awake as he was at that hour, staring at her own different ceiling.

The notice was stuck to the door before breakfast, the same as the leveling day weeks earlier: a name, a time, a room. Only his this time.

"Evaluation room, not the usual one," Maren said, catching him in the hallway before he could get there alone. "I'll walk with you."

"What did I do?"

"Nothing," Maren said, and something in how fast she said it didn't quite land right. "It's routine. Same thing we did with Dara this morning. Everyone in the group gets it, one by one, before the final evaluation."

"Final evaluation?"

"Seven days," Maren said, like the number cost her something to say out loud. "The month's almost up. Whatever AMAT decides to do with this group afterward, it decides based on what it sees then — not on what each of you thinks you've got in reserve. Today is so you don't reach that date without knowing, for real, what each of you actually has."

It didn't quite sound like routine. Maren had the face of someone who already knew how the conversation was going to go and didn't like any of it. She put a hand on his shoulder for a second, a gesture she didn't usually make before a routine evaluation, and pulled it back before Oliver could ask why.

The room was smaller than the classification room, no windows, a metal table and three chairs that didn't match each other — the kind of space St. Gotthard reserved for things it didn't want visible from the hallway. Anka was already inside when they arrived, sitting across an empty table except for a tablet and a glass of water she hadn't touched.

"Sit down," she said. "This is going to take a while."

Oliver sat. Maren stood a moment longer than necessary, leaning against the wall by the door, and when she finally took the third chair, she did it with the same care someone takes sitting down next to a hospital bed — not the posture of someone running an evaluation, but of someone who'd rather be anywhere else.

---

"The rappel report came in yesterday," Maren said, no preamble, once the three of them were seated. "Reber flagged it for review. Not for what Dara did. For what you didn't do."

Oliver said nothing.

"You've got more copied Domains than anyone in the program," Maren went on. "And during the minutes Petra was falling, you didn't use a single one. No one's blaming you for that," she added, before Oliver could say anything. "But if AMAT ever decides this group is ready for something real, they need to know what you actually have — not what looks good on paper."

*No one's blaming you for that.* Oliver heard the whole sentence and felt it ring false anyway, not because Maren was lying but because he blamed himself, with a clarity that hadn't let up since the wall — Petra's body falling and him standing there, hands closed around his own rope, an entire catalog of other people's power stored inside him and not one of them ready to use. If Dara's second Double had failed, if the rope had given a little more, it would have been him explaining to Pier why he'd just stood there watching.

"So let's go through them," Anka said. "All of them. One by one."

---

They started with Tension.

Oliver closed his eyes, reached for the internal motion he hadn't repeated on purpose in months, and pushed it against the arm of the chair. Something tightened, briefly, no real force behind it — like trying to clench a fist that had been asleep too long, a muscle he could barely remember having had. It came apart before he could hold it two seconds, and what was left wasn't surprise. It was closer to confirming a suspicion he'd been dragging around since the wall without wanting to look at it straight on: that everything he carried inside was dying slowly while he looked the other way.

The memory of how he'd gotten it came unbidden — the glass of the observation panel, months back, cracking without his fully deciding it. The first of all of them, before any other, even though it took weeks more to become something clean and deliberate, something Asha had handed him outright, with no need to earn it or steal it. One of the few times anyone had given him something with no conditions attached, and he'd let it starve in some corner without even noticing when it stopped breathing.

"Barely anything," Maren said, noting it on the tablet with no tone of surprise. "When was the last time you used it?"

"I don't know," Oliver said, and it was true.

Static was worse. The field he generated barely nudged the needle of the meter Anka had set on the table — a minimal tremor, nothing like the clean interference Riku produced without effort, already weeks back, in the pit, against Iker. This one had been the first deliberate, clean copy of all of them, the first time he'd done something like it on purpose instead of by accident — Riku giving it to him with no hierarchy involved, session after session in the east courtyard, both of them learning at the same time under the same old Alpine cold. Seeing it reduced to this hurt in a different way than the rest.

"When's the last time you actually practiced it?" Anka asked. "Not used it. Practiced it."

Oliver had no answer for that either.

Impulse worked, but crooked: the glass of water he tried to shift in a straight line veered half a hand's width off course, the precision Nadia had perfected reduced to something approximate, like recalling half of a language, the right words there but no longer fully his. Nothing like the clean feint that had opened Dax's side, weeks back, the one time it had really mattered. Anchor held a second longer than expected, almost stable, but the cost landed in his temple like a dull tug he hadn't felt since that same fight, when he'd pinned Dax's planted foot to the ground with more aim than luck. Nothing since. Sabine had given it to him without knowing it, in the north corridor, months back — without knowing, either, that weeks after its one good use, it would still be there, half-dead, waiting for him to remember it existed.

"Anchor and Impulse are still there," Anka said. "Degraded, but recoverable. The other two are close to zero."

"That's all?" Anka asked, once it was clear Oliver wasn't going to add anything else. "Four?"

Oliver hesitated half a second too long, and knew Anka had caught it — that small turn of her head toward him she made whenever something lit up nearby that nobody had said out loud.

"Four," he said.

Nobody mentioned Swap. It wasn't on any file, and Oliver didn't bring it up. He kept the word lodged in his throat for the rest of the session, thinking about Petra saying *watch* in an empty room, and about how that silence now belonged to him too, not just to her. Anka didn't push. But she didn't look fully convinced either, and that, more than anything else, was what unsettled him most all afternoon.

"Momentum," Maren said, at the end, almost like relief.

That one worked clean. The wrist turn, the absorption, the redirect — all there, all sharp, none of the tremor from the others. Maren noted it without needing to comment; the difference spoke for itself, and it didn't taste like pride to Oliver. It tasted like proof: the only reason anything of his was still alive was that he'd taken care of this one without noticing. The rest had simply died because nobody — not even him — had given them the same attention.

"Why this one?" Anka asked, though it sounded more like she already knew the answer than like she needed it.

"Because I keep using it," Oliver said. "Almost every night. Since Park taught me to build it up."

"Exactly," Anka said.

---

"How long can you hold the Registry active, without cutting it," Anka asked. "Not passive. Active, deliberate, actually reading."

"You don't need to time it," Oliver said. "I know roughly how long I can hold it."

"Then it won't cost you anything to prove it," Maren said, tablet already ready.

He tried. At eight minutes he already felt the familiar settling behind his sternum, the point where he expected the usual tremor to start. It didn't come. At twelve, still nothing. At fourteen — the valley's actual ceiling, the number he'd carried as a limit for months — he kept reading clean, no noise, and something in his stomach tightened at noticing it, not with relief.

Nobody spoke for a long while. The silence stretched in a way that cost Oliver more than the reading itself — there was nothing to do with his mind but let it go, and where it went wasn't anywhere good. Petra falling. The shoulder giving way with that dull sound. His hands closed, useless, while five people who'd given him something real kept waiting, without knowing it, for him to do something worthy of it. He wasn't thinking about the Registry at all anymore — only about the guilt, keeping its own count, more precise than any stopwatch.

At twenty minutes, the first real tremor. At twenty-three, Maren's outline started to blur the same way it had before, the lag between seeing and processing creeping back in slowly, like something that had been there the whole time, waiting its turn.

"Stop," Maren said, and Oliver stopped, grateful again that someone else had decided for him.

"Twenty-five minutes," Maren said, and this time she looked up. "Almost double the fourteen from months ago."

"That's a real improvement," Oliver said, not quite believing it himself.

"It is," Anka said. "And it shouldn't be, if you think about it. You've had the Registry switched on almost the whole day, every day, since you got here. With that kind of constant exposure, the ceiling should be closer to a full hour, not twenty-five minutes. You've improved through sheer brute force of use, not because you trained anything on purpose. And even so, you're not even halfway to what that same brute force should have given you."

"What about Momentum?" Maren asked.

They let him build it up without releasing it, without using it, just letting it climb. Forty minutes later it still hadn't complained, well past the thirty he'd already proven at Val Tremola — and he still hadn't hit the real limit, only the limit of the session's patience.

Nobody said the comparison out loud. It didn't need saying. What he'd actually been training, a few minutes every night, with real intention, had grown faster in weeks than what he'd been using nonstop, all day, for months. What held up everything else — the Registry itself, the base the four copies that had just failed him one by one depended on, plus the fifth nobody in that room knew existed — had improved, yes, but only the way something improves when nobody tends it on purpose: slowly, unevenly, and well below what it should be.

"An unbalanced profile," Maren said, finally, in the voice of someone who'd rather not have to say it. "It's not a criticism, Oliver. It's a data point. You have access to more than anyone's ever had, and you're actively using a single piece of all of it."

Oliver didn't answer right away. He sat looking at his own hands on the table, the same ones that had stayed closed and useless on the wall, and thought that "unbalanced" was too clean a word for what he felt — something closer to being hollow inside, in the exact shape of five people who'd trusted him.

---

It wasn't the imbalance that stuck with him. It was that every one of those copies had someone behind it who'd given it to him — Asha on purpose, Riku with no hierarchy attached, Sabine by accident and then without resentment, Petra in silence — and he'd taken them in the way someone shoves a gift into a closet and forgets it exists. In the one moment it actually mattered, with Petra falling and his hands closed around his own rope, none of it had been worth anything. Not because he lacked it. Because he'd never bothered to keep it ready.

It wasn't imbalance. It was waste, with a name and a face on it: five people who'd given him something real, and an Oliver standing still exactly when it mattered most.

"I'm going to stop chasing everything that crosses my path," he said out loud, without having decided to say it until he already was. "Choose what I keep. And actually let the rest go, instead of just letting it rot without deciding to."

Maren looked at him a moment, something like relief crossing her face — the first real one since she'd walked into that room.

"That's the first useful thing to come out of this room," she said. "Most people don't even get that far on their own. Someone has to tell them."

"And who told me?" Oliver asked, no accusation in it, just genuine curiosity.

"No one," Maren said. "That's why I'm writing this one down differently."

Anka said nothing, but she closed the tablet with a gesture that struck Oliver, for the first time that afternoon, as almost satisfied.

---

He found Anka afterward, alone, in the same courtyard as always, the light already sinking behind the stone walls. She didn't seem surprised to see him — she never did.

"I'm not looking for a new technique," he said, sitting beside her on the usual bench. "I'm looking for how to decide what I keep and what I let go. I have no idea where to start, and I don't want it to be random. I've already wasted enough choosing without thinking."

Anka didn't answer right away. When she did, she didn't talk about Domains.

"Has anyone ever taught you to read a movement instead of a signature?" she said. "Not what the Odem does. What the body does around it. The order weight gets loaded. The angle of the turn."

The memory arrived whole, unasked for: the east courtyard, months back, Yusuf telling him that everyone else learned to move with one Domain, and that he was going to have to learn to move with several. The afternoon after, with Park, the hit that hadn't been force against force — it had been geometry.

"Yes," he said, slowly.

"A style doesn't get built by copying whole Domains," Anka went on, not waiting for more of an answer. "It gets built by copying what people do with what they have. The order weight gets loaded. The angle of the turn. That doesn't degrade the way a signature does. It doesn't depend on keeping something borrowed active inside you — it depends on you, on your own body repeating it until it stops being borrowed."

"That's exactly what Yusuf told me," Oliver said, slowly. "Almost the same words. Months ago, before all this."

"Then you already had the answer," Anka said. "You just stopped listening to it."

Oliver stayed quiet, processing not the idea — clear, useful, exactly what he'd come looking for — but the other part, the one that didn't quite fit: it hadn't been "almost the same words." They'd been the same, word for word — the order weight gets loaded, the angle of the turn, phrase by phrase. That conversation had never left that room.

"How do you know exactly what to say so that—" he started, and cut himself off mid-question, without even finishing formulating it inside his own head.

"I guessed," Anka said, as if she'd heard the rest of the question anyway.

It didn't sound like a lie. It didn't sound fully like the truth either. Oliver didn't push. He filed that one away too, next to Swap, next to the name he'd been carrying for weeks without saying — one more thing he no longer knew if he was protecting someone by keeping quiet about, or just putting off the inevitable.

---

He couldn't sleep. He lay on his back counting the ceiling cracks he already knew by heart, Riku's breathing steady below him, a metronome that did him no good. At some point he brought a hand to the center of his chest, pressing, as if the weight he felt there were something he could push inward until it disappeared. It didn't move. It only changed shape under his palm, dull, exact.

He closed his eyes and it got worse: Asha turning her head toward him in the east courtyard, giving without conditions. Petra in the empty room, her low voice, *watch*, and something in her posture that had already decided not to explain it to him. And then, with no transition, without him calling for it, Petra's temple against the rock, the exact instant she stopped searching and just started falling.

He sat up abruptly. Riku didn't wake. He got dressed in the dark, feeling for his clothes by touch, and went down the three flights to the secondary room barefoot.

The door gave without a sound. He turned on the light — the same bare bulb as always, hanging from the center — and stood a moment in the doorway, looking at the empty space where he'd watched so many others train something he'd never bothered to train fully himself. The clock on the wall read two-fourteen.

He shut the door behind him. He started with Tension.
