# Chapter 8 — What No One Saw Him Do


He returned the notebook the next morning, before anyone else reached Dunmore, his hands still clumsy from a night without sleep.

That part was easy. What came after — two full weeks of repeating the same motion every time he closed alone: taking it out, walking up to the attic with it pressed to his chest under his jacket, putting it back before the shop opened — was what actually cost him. Farid didn't check it daily. He only needed it when an account came up that couldn't be collected through the usual channels, and those accounts, as far as Oliver had been able to piece together, arrived on no schedule any calendar could predict. That gave him room. It didn't give him peace.

Every night he had it in his hands, he counted. Dates, not figures — there were never any figures to count, only crossed-out circles and the same cramped handwriting repeating itself across decades without a single tremor. Seventy-four names, total, up to the last one he'd managed to date. Twenty-nine years since the first, counting from the earliest date he could pin down with confidence. The first six years held barely eleven — the same slow pace you'd expect from a business with one or two shops and a single man deciding every case. After that, the curve bent upward: more names per year as the shops multiplied, until the last fifteen years carried a pace almost three times higher than the first six. It didn't take a graph to see it. The ink density per page said it plainly enough, packing tighter the closer it got to the present.

The other thing that started happening, without his looking for it, was the gap.

The first time, the night of the safe, it had lasted an instant he later couldn't quite trust himself to have really seen. The second night he took the notebook, it happened again — shorter, almost nothing, a flicker at the edge of the counter where for a fraction of a second the outline of things lost its edge, as if someone had shifted the whole image a millimeter without touching anything. The third time was worse: not in the shop, but already up in the attic, the notebook open on his knees, when he felt something on the other side of the room watching him with the same attention he was giving the page, before he'd seen anything at all. He held still for almost a full minute, breath low, until the sensation withdrew as slowly as it had come, leaving no trace he could point to afterward and say *here, this happened.*

By the sixth night, he no longer flinched. That, more than anything else, was what truly started to unsettle him — not the gap itself, but how fast his own body had learned to expect it, to make room for it, the way a person stops reacting to a train that always passes at the same hour. He told no one. He had no way to explain it without sounding like someone who needed more sleep, and sleep was exactly what he hadn't fully managed in two weeks.

Gary had called two nights before, from somewhere on I-95, with the same tired, contented voice as always, and Oliver had talked to him for ten minutes without mentioning any of what he was carrying — not the notebook, not the gap, not the seventy-four names — with the same ease he'd spent half a lifetime perfecting, sorting what his father needed to know from what would only have worried him for nothing. He hung up thinking that ease had, at some point not too long ago, stopped being a talent and started being something else. He didn't have time to decide what.

That afternoon — a Thursday, the same kind of Thursday as any other — he'd had no chance to return it. Farid had arrived earlier than usual, with no explanation, and stayed at the Dunmore counter all morning going over something he shared with no one. Oliver waited for a window that never came. By noon he resigned himself to keeping it on him, under his shirt, against his ribs, and spent the rest of the day with the exact feeling of someone carrying a borrowed thing hours overdue for return.

---

Silas arrived a little before seven, a gray folder under his arm and something on his face Oliver couldn't name until he'd been watching it a while: not his usual calm. The leftover of a calm already spent somewhere else, before he'd arrived. His hands were red, as if he'd been outside without gloves longer than the evening warranted, and he never once put them in his pockets while he waited.

He didn't sit. He set the folder on the glass counter and said nothing until Farid came out of the back room.

"Tobias," Farid said, no question in his voice.

"You don't need to open it." Silas didn't quite let go of the folder. "He hasn't shown up at Foster in four days. I went looking for him myself. His landlady doesn't know anything, neither does his sister, and Delvecchio's men stopped looking for him two days ago — which is worse than if they were still looking."

Delvecchio. Oliver knew the name the way you know bad weather that already passed through somewhere else: the pawnshop with its own neon sign had closed years ago, and with it, so the story went in the trade, the last time anyone had seen Delvecchio in person. What hadn't closed, apparently, was the money still lending itself out through other names, with no shop, no sign, nothing a curious neighbor could point a finger at.

Park, who'd spent the whole evening in the back storeroom going through boxes, didn't come out to look. Oliver knew because the sound of the boxes stopped the moment Silas started talking, and didn't start again until much later — the same trained discretion Park had already taught him, wordlessly, to recognize.

Farid didn't answer right away. He looked at the folder like he already knew every word inside it.

"This is the twelfth," Silas said, without raising his voice. "Counting this one."

Behind the counter, Oliver felt the number land somewhere specific in his body, not just his ear. Twelve. Out of seventy-four. From Silas's shop alone, which had been open just over twenty-five years. He did the math without deciding to, the same automatic calculation he'd spent half a lifetime running on any figure put in front of him, and he didn't like the result: if Silas's shop alone carried that proportion, the other six had to be contributing a good deal more than Oliver had assumed until that night.

Silas didn't ask for anything more. He didn't ask the question Oliver expected — what happened to people, what it meant to be settled. He stood there, watching Farid with the same stillness he'd used for twenty-five years to hand over gray folders without opening his mouth, and this time that stillness carried a different weight. It wasn't asking for an answer. It was demanding that Farid act in front of him, for the first time, instead of alone.

Farid looked at him a moment longer than necessary, as if he were calculating something he had no intention of saying out loud. Then he nodded, once, and went toward the back room.

---

The sound of the dial came the same as always: seven, twenty-three, forty-one, seven again. The same soft click.

Then nothing.

Oliver counted the seconds without meaning to — five, eight, eleven — and knew, before Farid reappeared, exactly what he'd find on his face, because he himself had spent the whole day feeling the exact weight of that same absence against his ribs.

Farid came out with empty hands, his eyes fixed on a point that was neither Silas nor Oliver. For the first time since Oliver had known him, he said nothing while he decided what to say.

"Yamal?" Silas said.

"Go home," Farid said, not looking at him. "I'll handle it."

It wasn't a promise. Silas seemed to take it exactly that way, because he didn't argue, didn't press, didn't repeat the question he'd never actually asked out loud. He stayed a moment longer, studying something in Farid's face that Oliver found hard to read all the way through — not quite suspicion, something closer to a confirmation that needed no words: whatever happened to the people he brought in person, even Farid didn't always have the control he pretended to. Somehow, that turned out worse than any answer Farid could have given him.

He picked up the folder. He left without another word, and Oliver watched him go down Halloway through the shop window, the same stride as always, until the dark of the street swallowed him the same way it had swallowed, at some point in the last twenty-nine years, seventy-four other people.

---

He found his chance a little past eight, with Park still in the back storeroom, busy with something he'd been at since before Silas arrived, and Farid alone at the counter, squaring a column he'd already squared twice.

"I'm going to check last week's receipts," Oliver said, unprompted. "Before Silas takes his tomorrow."

It wasn't a full lie. It wasn't the truth either. He crossed to the counter with his usual calm, though his heart was running faster than any column of receipts deserved, knelt beside the safe with his back to the room — the same posture he'd spent half his life watching Farid make — and returned the notebook to the exact spot he'd taken it from the night before, with the same care you'd use returning something you hope no one noticed was gone. It weighed less than he remembered. He told himself that was only relief.

When he stood up, Farid was watching him.

He said nothing. He didn't ask what receipts, or why at that hour, or why the safe instead of the receipt drawer everyone used for that. He didn't ask the one question that actually deserved asking: how an employee no one had ever given the combination to had just opened and closed it with the same ease Farid used himself, without hesitating on a single digit, back to the room the same way Farid stood. He kept looking at Oliver with the same serene attention he'd spent a whole life reading faces to know when someone was lying, and Oliver, who'd spent half his own life learning to read people, understood — with a physical certainty that needed no words to confirm it — that Farid already knew. Not just that night. Probably from before.

"Everything's in order," Oliver said, because nothing better came to him. And he hurried out of Farid's sight.

Farid didn't answer. He went back to his column as if Oliver had already left the room.

---

He stayed finishing the storeroom inventory longer than it needed, waiting — without quite admitting it — for something to happen: an accusation, a question, anything that would break the silence instead of leaving it hanging there, unresolved. Nothing happened. He heard Farid move toward the back room a while later, no hurry or hesitation in his step, and after that nothing but the ordinary sound of a man going through papers alone: a drawer, a chair, the faint scratch of a pen on paper.

The sound stopped sooner than Oliver expected. There was no flickering light, no sudden cold, no silence shaped like a question. Just the scratch of the pen, and then nothing — so ordinary that if Oliver had been paying attention to anything else, he wouldn't even have noticed. Whatever Farid had just closed out under the name Tobias Wren, he closed it exactly the way he closed every other impossible account: no ceremony, nothing in the shop shifting weight.

Oliver told himself that should have settled him. It didn't. He spent the next few minutes finishing up the tarp over the storeroom boxes without really needing to, just to keep his hands busy with something other than waiting, and when he finally heard the back-room door open again, Farid came out with his jacket on and his keys in hand, as if nothing at all unusual had happened that evening.

"You close up," he said, without stopping. "I've done enough for today."

It didn't sound like tiredness. It sounded like something already finished.

---

At nine ten, Park came out of the storeroom with his jacket already on, whistling something that wasn't quite a tune, ready for the same walk home as always. He found Oliver with his own jacket already on too, standing by the door, watching the back room with an attention Park seemed to notice before Oliver said anything.

"You've been standing there a while," Park said. "Yamal already gone?"

"Just now."

Park didn't ask anything else. He stood by the door, hands in his pockets, waiting with the same trained patience as always, and for a moment Oliver almost told him, finally, what he'd been holding onto for two weeks — the notebook, the gap, the seventy-four names. He never quite decided to.

"Everything okay?" Park asked.

Oliver opened his mouth to say yes.

He never got to say it. The drinks fridge by the door cut off mid-hum, and the sound didn't fade the way it always faded. It stopped like a word interrupted before it finished being said. The air changed weight — the same pull Oliver already knew, in his chest, like an elevator dropping fast and finding no floor — except this time it didn't come from any one point in the shop. It came from everywhere at once, and from the exact center of his own chest, as if the absence he'd spent two weeks feeling grow at the edges of his vision, every flicker, every night he'd stopped flinching, had been building the whole time.

"Oliver," Park said, and his voice had nothing trained or calm left in it.

The lights of Dunmore went out all at once.

In the dark, Oliver felt that something, somewhere that wasn't any piece of paper, had just written his name.
