# Chapter 92 — The One in the Car

---

The apartment smelled like nothing, and that was exactly what Lev wanted.

No strong food, no perfume, nothing a stranger could reconstruct in a hallway three hours after they'd left. Sabine had understood that fast, faster than him in some aspects of this new life — she knew how to fold clothes so they'd all fit in a bag in under a minute, she knew which drawer in someone else's kitchen held the knives without having to open three others first, she knew how to sleep with one shoe on without it showing on her face when she woke.

They'd been in that apartment eight days. The longest since they'd left — the two before hadn't lasted a full week each.

"Your turn," she said, without looking away from the window, where she'd been sitting for the last half hour in the chair they'd moved two hand-spans to the left on the second day, the exact angle to see the street without the street being able to see her.

Lev sat down. She didn't need to tell him twice — the watch shift passed between them without negotiation, like everything else in that house: who threw the second bolt on the door, who checked the go-bag before sleeping, who spoke first when something was wrong. It wasn't a relationship built on grand gestures. It was a relationship built on who did what, when, without the other having to ask.

"Nothing," he said, after a while, his eyes on the sidewalk across the street.

"I know it's nothing," Sabine said. "I'm asking you, not the street."

It was a distinction Sabine made often, one that had taken Lev weeks to fully understand. For her it wasn't enough that the street was empty — the person looking at it had to confirm they'd actually looked, not just that their eyes had passed over it. Years of restraint, of fixing objects and people with absolute precision, had left her with that habit: nothing counted as observed until someone declared it observed out loud.

"The baker changed the sign in the window," Lev said, giving in. "Yesterday's bread for a new one. The couple on the third floor took out the trash eleven minutes later than last night. And the man on the corner bench has been there since I sat down, not moving, not looking at his phone, not doing anything a man sitting on a bench usually does."

Sabine turned, only a little, just enough to verify without it looking like verifying.

"How long has he been there?"

"I don't know. He was already there when I sat down."

"Then it doesn't count," she said, though the tension in her back didn't fully ease. "He could've been there all morning for any reason. Write it down anyway."

They kept a small notebook, no name on the cover, where they wrote down patterns — not suspicions, patterns. A car seen twice was nothing. A car seen three times, from different angles, was. It was the only method they'd found to keep from going mad with fear or careless with exhaustion: everything got written, nothing got decided on pure instinct, not even the instinct of two people who'd spent half their lives training their bodies to read danger before it arrived.

Lev didn't answer right away. He ran a hand over several days' worth of beard, a gesture that meant nothing except that he was thinking about something he didn't want to say yet.

"It has to be done again tonight," he said, finally. "The trace from the last apartment has gone untouched too long already. If AMAT has someone who can read residual Odem density, which I'm afraid they do, it'll have regenerated enough to read by now."

Sabine said nothing, but something in her back straightened. She never said directly that what he did worried her. She said it with her body, in the way she stopped moving for a second too long.

Lev went to the kitchen and came back with the one cup they'd left unwashed, a worthless object, unnamed, bought at some flea market three cities back. He set it on the table between them and closed his eyes.

It wasn't like heating a room. He'd known how to do that since he was a boy without thinking about it, a simple pull of ambient heat concentrated in a point or spread over a radius, the kind of thing AMAT had measured, cataloged, approved without surprise. This was different. This started the same — a pull, the same sensation of emptying something of its temperature — but he didn't stop where the heat ran out. He kept pulling, further in, to a point that had no name in any manual he'd ever seen, a point where what came out was no longer heat but something underneath heat, the very substance that kept the cup being a cup and not just a lump of fired clay waiting to stop existing.

The cup coated itself in frost within seconds. Not the normal cold of something losing temperature — something deeper, a matte white that didn't shine like ice, as if the cup had begun to forget it had ever been warm in some kiln, at some point in its history.

Lev opened his eyes. It hurt behind his eye sockets, a dull ache that hadn't fully gone down in weeks.

"Done," he said.

"How long does it hold this time?" Sabine asked, looking at the cup without touching it.

"I don't know," he said, honestly. "I've never measured it precisely. Long enough, I hope."

What he did with the cup wasn't the goal. It was the rehearsal — practice before doing it for real, on the very air of the previous apartment, on the density of Odem he and Sabine had left there simply by having breathed, slept, existed in that space for almost two weeks. He drained the cup to remember the exact feel, the exact depth he had to reach without going too far — going too far meant something he couldn't quite name, a kind of void that felt wrong even to him, someone already used to doing things no instructor would ever have taught him.

The cup would never accumulate Odem again. He knew that for certain, though he couldn't explain why. An object drained that way stayed drained forever — cold in a way no normal thermometer would know how to read properly, even though at a glance it looked like nothing but an ordinary cold cup.

"I'm going to do it for real tonight," he said. "The old apartment. Before someone with the right Domain passes through and finds more than they should."

Sabine finally turned away from the window.

"It's not just the old apartment that worries me," she said.

Lev waited. She never spoke before she had the whole sentence assembled, and that was one of the things he respected most about living with her — she never said anything half-formed just to fill a silence.

"The gray car," she said. "I've seen it three times in four days. Twice at the corner by the bakery, once here, halfway down the street. Never parked long enough to look suspicious. Never short enough not to be."

"Could be anyone," Lev said, without much conviction of his own behind the sentence.

"It could," she said. "But you and I have spent enough time running from people who know how to look like nothing to recognize it when it passes our door three times in four days."

They didn't argue beyond that. It wasn't how they fought — they'd never been the kind of couple who raised their voices. When something really mattered, they spoke slower, not louder, as if raising the volume would waste something they couldn't get back.

"Tomorrow's the meeting with Aurelio," Sabine said, after a while. "If there really is someone watching this area, we should cancel it."

Aurelio didn't know who they really were. He knew they needed cash, transit documents, a contact who didn't ask questions — and that for a reasonable price, and by never asking too much, he could get them both without anyone else finding out. He didn't know that, without knowing it, he was working for two fugitives from an organization most people didn't even suspect existed. He believed he was helping a couple running from something domestic, something private, maybe a violent husband or a badly paid debt. He'd never asked for details, and Lev and Sabine had never offered them.

"We can't just cancel it," Lev said. "We need what he's bringing. And if we cancel suddenly, after three established meetings, that's also a pattern someone can read."

"I know," Sabine said. "That's why I'm saying we go, but we leave the city the same night. Not one more night here."

Lev nodded. It was the kind of decision they made fast between them, without needing to convince each other — when one said "let's go," the other was already folding clothes before the sentence finished coming out.

That night, while Sabine slept a light sleep, one shoe on as always, Lev walked the four blocks to the old apartment, let himself in with the key they hadn't returned yet, and sat in the middle of the empty room where they'd slept for almost two weeks. He closed his eyes. He searched for the same sensation as with the cup, but at a scale that didn't fit inside a small object — the whole air of the room, the invisible density of having existed there, of having breathed there, of having left, without knowing it, an outline of themselves floating in the space like a scent only someone with the right instrument could smell.

He pulled. He felt the same pain behind his eye sockets, multiplied, and something in his chest that wasn't physical pain exactly — more like the feeling of emptying a bucket too fast, feeling the weight vanish suddenly from your arms. The room went cold, not a normal cold — the same matte white as the cup, invisible in the dark, but he knew it was there, coating the walls like a layer of something that would never accumulate life again.

He left before his hands shook too badly to close the door normally.

The next day, the meeting with Aurelio was in the back room of an auto parts shop, at the hour when the owner had already left and only Aurelio remained, a man in his fifties with hands always stained with grease and a generic distrust of everyone that didn't include, for some reason, Sabine.

"Documents, like you asked," he said, sliding an envelope across the counter. "And this too"—a second envelope, thinner, cash. "Less than usual. Things are more expensive this month. Don't ask why, I don't fully know either."

"We never ask," Sabine said, with the trained calm of someone who'd spent half her life learning not to give anything away with her face.

Lev counted the envelope without it looking like counting, a quick, almost automatic gesture. It was short of what he expected, but not enough to make an issue of it.

"Everything all right with you?" Sabine asked, a small gesture of real courtesy, not fake — they'd had three meetings with this man and at some point, without quite meaning to, she'd started genuinely caring whether things were going well for him.

"Fine as always," Aurelio said, shrugging. "Though last week a guy came around asking, said he was looking for a couple that matched..." He stopped, frowning, as if he'd just connected something he hadn't connected before. "No, never mind. It's probably nothing."

Sabine's face didn't change. Neither did Lev's. But something between the two of them, invisible to Aurelio, tightened at the same moment, like a rope they were both holding without knowing the other was holding it too.

"What kind of guy?" Lev asked, in the most neutral tone he could manage.

"I don't know. Suited, but not expensive-suit. Asked strange questions. I told him I didn't know anything, that all I sell here is screws and belts. He left."

They left the shop separately, five minutes apart, as always. Sabine was the first to notice it, half a street later: the same gray car, less careful about hiding this time, parked right in front of the back exit she'd just taken.

She didn't run. Running was the first thing that gave someone away. She walked at her usual pace, turned at the corner that didn't lead home, and waited two blocks before letting her heart race for real.

They met, as planned for any emergency, at the small train station on the outskirts, the one almost nobody used.

"They've found us," Sabine said, still out of breath. "Or they're close. The car was right at the exit I took. That's not coincidence, twice in one day isn't coincidence."

Lev didn't waste time arguing whether she was right. They'd spent months building an exact system for this moment, and the system said: move, don't think twice.

"We have the bag at the apartment," he said. "Just enough. We can buy the rest in the next city."

"Not the apartment," Sabine said. "If they followed us to Aurelio, they might have followed us to the apartment too. We go straight to the main station. We buy tickets with today's cash, not card, nothing that leaves a trace."

They got on the first train leaving, without looking too closely at the destination — any direction that got them out of the city would do tonight. Lev, sitting by the window, his head resting against the truly cold glass, felt the pain behind his eye sockets still not fully gone, the weight of having drained two objects in under twenty-four hours.

"Do you think it's going to last?" Sabine asked, quietly, her head against his shoulder, the first moment of real stillness since the shop.

"The apartment, I don't know," Lev said. "I left it as clean as I could. But it was fast, not how I'd like. There might be something left."

"I'm not asking about the apartment," she said.

Lev understood, a second late, what she meant.

"I don't know," he said, honest again, because he'd never lied to her about anything that mattered since the day they left together. "But I'd rather not know and still be here with you than know for certain anywhere else without you."

Sabine said nothing more. She closed her eyes, her hand finding his without needing to search for it, like something that already knew where it was.

The train moved on, leaving behind a city, a half-drained apartment, a man named Aurelio who would probably never know who he'd really been helping, and a gray car that — they couldn't know this, not yet — had already been parked for several hours in front of an empty building, three people inside arguing over whether the trail they'd just lost was the closest they'd been in weeks.
