# Chapter 106 — Free Day

---

There was no paper stuck to the door.

Oliver realized it before he'd fully opened his eyes — the absence of a sound he hadn't even known he was expecting, the creak of someone else already awake, moving with the urgency of a person who has an order to follow. None of that. Only Riku's breathing, below, slow and regular in a way Oliver hadn't heard from him in days.

He stayed lying down, looking at the ceiling, not knowing what to do with the absence of instructions. A whole month without a single day like this. His body didn't quite trust it.

"Still awake?" Riku said, from below, his voice still thick with sleep.

"Yeah."

"Are you going to train?"

Oliver took a while to answer.

"I don't know."

Riku didn't push. He turned over again, and at some point in the next few minutes he fell back asleep — the sound changing, deeper, easier. Oliver stayed where he was, listening to him, until the light under the door from the hallway grew strong enough that staying in bed started to feel like a decision instead of rest.

---

The dining hall, that morning, had a different sound — lower, less urgent, people eating slowly instead of calculating how many minutes they had left before the next summons. Dara was already sitting when Oliver arrived, with Asha and Pier, the three of them sharing a bowl of fruit that no one had ever stopped to look at with so much attention before.

"We were thinking of going to the equipment room, to check the gear before tomorrow," Pier said, not making it sound like a closed invitation. "Nothing urgent. Just so we're not showing up empty-handed."

Oliver nodded without fully committing. He served himself something he had no intention of finishing and sat for a moment, listening more than talking. Nadia arrived after, tablet still in hand, though there was nothing to check on it that day — the gesture of holding it seemed to serve some purpose anyway, an anchor for her hands.

"My dad called last night," she said, without anyone asking. "Didn't say anything important. Just called."

No one asked her to explain further. Asha touched her shoulder for a second, brief, and went back to the fruit.

Riku showed up last, his eye almost fully healed, just a yellowish shadow under the lid that no one mentioned anymore. He sat across from Oliver without saying much, his gaze lost somewhere on the table that wasn't really there. Oliver recognized the expression — the same one Riku had been wearing since the fight club, the one that appeared when he thought about Iker without saying so out loud. Not Noa — never Noa. With Noa he'd lost clean, something you could almost file away as a lesson learned. With Iker there was something older, harder to name, the kind of unfinished account that one technical bout was never going to settle.

"Are you going to go after him?" Oliver asked, quietly, just for Riku.

Riku didn't ask who he meant.

"Not yet," he said. "I don't know if I'm going to get another chance after tomorrow."

Neither of them said anything else about it. The empty chair at the end of the table, the one that for days had been Petra's, was still Petra's in the way no one sat there, even though there was room to spare.

---

Oliver broke off from the group mid-morning, without fully announcing it.

"Coming to the equipment room?" Dara asked, already standing, Pier's bag slung over one shoulder.

"I'll catch up," Oliver said.

He didn't catch up. He walked in the opposite direction the moment Dara's back was out of sight, without having fully decided where he was going until his own feet took him there — the wing where the rest of St. Gotthard knew, without anyone saying it out loud, that it wasn't wise to walk in uninvited.

---

He found Yegor alone, sitting on a bench by the door of the converted storeroom, his right arm still wrapped in a rigid bandage that no longer seemed to be holding much — more habit than necessity, the kind of wrap someone keeps on a few extra days because taking it off feels like admitting something. Milan was a little further off, leaning against the wall, and two others Oliver didn't know by name were tossing a pair of worn tennis balls back and forth, with no real purpose beyond killing time — one standing, the other sitting on top of a closed wooden crate, legs dangling.

Yegor saw him coming before Oliver said anything. He didn't jump up. He took his time, flexing the wrist of his bandaged arm with deliberate slowness, like someone who wants the other person to see he doesn't need to hurry for anyone.

"Look who decided to show up," he said, without quite raising his voice, the contempt colder than loud. "Alone. No people behind you, watching. This, interesting."

"I came to finish the thing from Val Tremola," Oliver said.

Yegor let out something like a laugh, short, no real humor in it.

"Finish." He repeated the word like it had too much flavor. "You finished nothing. Someone screamed, I turned, you got lucky, the arm. That's not finishing a fight. That's an accident, with your name written on it."

Milan pushed off the wall, his eyes going from Yegor to Oliver and back, saying nothing. No one called an instructor. No one even seemed to consider it.

"The arm, doesn't hurt anymore," Yegor added, with the same contempt, as if the mere idea that Oliver might think otherwise was already an insult. "This you don't need to ask me. Sorry, also don't need from you."

"I didn't come to forgive you for anything," Oliver said.

"Good," Yegor said. "Because I wasn't going to accept it anyway."

---

They moved to the open space between the stacked crates, three meters of bare concrete Oliver already knew from other times, though never from this side. Milan stepped back to one edge, not intervening, the same spectator's stillness Oliver already recognized from the whole fight club.

Yegor opened with the healthy arm, a quick testing jab. Oliver dodged it without effort, already reading the unevenly distributed weight — the bandaged arm hanging a little more protected than Yegor wanted to admit.

"Still hurts," Oliver said.

"And you, still, care too much what happens to people," Yegor said, and closed the distance for real.

The second strike was real, loaded with the healthy arm. Oliver turned his torso just enough for the fist to graze him instead of connecting, absorbing the graze in the same motion — trained reflex, added to the reserve without thinking.

Yegor gave him no time for anything else. He changed angle, faked toward the shoulder, and finished with a low knee strike Oliver hadn't expected from someone with only one good arm.

It connected. It hurt for real, a dull blow to the thigh that stole half a step of balance.

"Bad arm doesn't make anyone stupid," Yegor said.

Oliver didn't answer. He returned it — a point, small, direct under the ribs, all the absorbed weight released at once in a short pivot on his own foot.

It connected. Yegor doubled over, the air cut out of him — but he didn't fall, and the bandaged arm was already moving before Oliver could close the distance. The whole bandage went taut at once, rigid in a way the fabric alone didn't explain, and the loose end cracked toward his face like a whip with an edge.

Oliver barely turned in time. The line opened on his cheek, thin and hot — a clean cut, not a blow.

The Registry activated on its own, late. It wasn't the arm. It was the bandage itself, hardened until it behaved like a blade, and beneath that, something bigger: Yegor's entire signature reordering itself around any object it touched.

*No wonder rank seven*, Oliver thought, with no real time to think it through.

"Your face, surprised," Yegor said, the bandage already slack again. "It's called Arsenal. What I touch, I control — direction, speed, weight. More Odem I put in, worse for whoever's on the receiving end."

He crouched by the crate of tennis balls and picked up three at once, without taking his eyes off Oliver. No one needed to hand them to him. The moment he had them in his palm, the Registry reacted again — three objects charging at once, each with a weight no tennis ball should have.

He threw them together, not in a line.

The first came straight. Oliver absorbed it with his forearm — a full blow, dense, the reserve swallowing it without fully avoiding it. A bruise was already rising under his sleeve before the second was in the air.

The second didn't come straight. It curved midway, correcting toward the side Oliver had just uncovered — Yegor was still holding it somehow, even thrown, guiding it as if his hand were still there. Oliver turned late. It grazed his side, another dull blow, another bruise.

The third never touched him at all. It hung suspended, slowly correcting course, tracing a wide arc behind him — Yegor wasn't done with it. He was holding it in reserve.

That was when Yegor closed the distance himself, the bandaged arm already extended toward Oliver's wrist, and the bandage coiled before he could pull away, going taut like a cable.

Oliver understood, all at once: the three balls were still his, still charged, and in the same instant the bandage caught him, all three began converging — the first and second returning from where they'd fallen, bouncing with a momentum no tennis ball had naturally, the third closing the arc from behind. A single point of impact, calculated, and him at the center, one arm already pinned.

"Stay still," Yegor said, pulling. "Just a second, stay still."

Oliver didn't think. He swapped.

He didn't know, in the moment, exactly what he'd done — only that his own wrist, caught a second before, ended up free, and that the taut bandage was now holding, uselessly, the corner of the wooden crate where the candidate had been sitting a second before. The kid jumped aside with a short yell, hitting the ground just as the three balls arrived together at the exact point where Oliver had just been standing — the whole crate exploding into splinters under the combined impact, with a dry sound that felt more like a small explosion than a strike. He'd never done it that way before, for real, under pressure, without thinking it through — he'd only ever practiced it alone, at night, without telling anyone. It worked anyway.

Yegor stayed still one second too long, pulling on a bandage that no longer held anything, looking at the remains of the crate without fully understanding what had just happened — and that second was enough. Oliver closed the distance with the last of his stored Momentum and threw the same strike as always — a point, small, under the ribs, in the exact same spot he'd already hit once that afternoon.

Yegor dropped to his knees, out of air.

Oliver already had the next blow charged, fist closed, the rest of his reserve ready to release at a point he'd already chosen before consciously deciding to choose it. Yegor lifted his head in time to see it — to see exactly what was coming, with no strength left to stop it.

Neither of them said anything. Oliver didn't let it fly.

He didn't need to. Both of them already knew: the fight had ended before that fist, not after.

No one counted. No one needed to count.

---

Oliver stood for a moment in the center of the space, breathing hard, his cheek burning, without the satisfaction he'd come looking for. Yegor, sitting on the same bench again, held his ribs with his good hand — and still lifted his head to look at him with the same intact arrogance, as if losing hadn't taken anything real from him.

"Don't get used to this," Yegor said, spitting a little blood to one side without looking away. "Doesn't change what you are. Only what you know how to do, with the hands. And now you know — I have more than one."

Oliver didn't answer. He didn't need to.

Milan said nothing when Oliver passed by him on the way back to the corridor. No one in the group called him back, no one asked him anything. He pressed his sleeve to the cut on his cheek, holding it until it stopped bleeding entirely, and didn't look at the bruises on his arm and side until much later, when no one else could see them.

---

He found the rest in the equipment room, just as they'd said — Pier checking harnesses one by one with the same attention as always, Dara and Asha checking rations, Nadia sitting in a corner, tablet already forgotten on her knees. Riku showed up shortly after Oliver, without saying where he'd been, and no one asked.

"Where'd you go off to?" Dara said, no real suspicion, just passing curiosity.

"Around," Oliver said, staying by the door, where the light fell worse, until the cut on his cheek finally stopped bleeding.

No one pushed. The day still had that strange texture of ownerless time, and everyone seemed to have decided, without agreeing out loud, not to spend it on questions no one really wanted answered.

---

They ate dinner together that night, the five of them, at a table that felt smaller than it was since the sixth chair had gone missing. Someone botched a joke about the climbing gear. Asha laughed for real, the first time in days Oliver had seen her do it without holding anything back. Riku, his hand closed around a glass he hadn't touched in a while, looked at the whole table like someone trying to memorize something before it stopped being available.

It was Nadia who noticed, halfway through dinner, when the dining hall light hit Oliver full in the face for the first time in hours.

"What happened to your cheek?" she said, not loudly, just for him, though Pier looked up anyway.

"Nothing," Oliver said. "Scraped it on something in the equipment room."

Nadia looked at him a moment too long, not quite buying it, but didn't push — the same silent agreement as the whole table, no one spending the day on questions no one really wanted answered. Pier, though, let his eyes rest a second longer than necessary on the cut, and something in his face said he could tell a scrape from a clean cut, even if he didn't say so either.

No one mentioned where they'd be going tomorrow. No one had a real name for it yet.

Oliver ate slowly, present in the body, elsewhere in everything else — thinking about the blow under the ribs, about the taut bandage holding the air where his wrist had just been, about how much it had cost him to feel nothing after all that, and about how tomorrow, when there was an order stuck to the door again, that would be the first real data point he'd have about himself for whatever came next.

"We should sleep early," Pier said, finally, when his plate was already empty. "Tomorrow we don't know what we're going to run into."

No one argued. They got up one by one, the table emptying slowly, and for a moment, before the last one left, something hung in the air none of them knew how to name — the shared certainty, unspoken, that this had been the last dinner like this they were going to have for a long time.
