# Chapter 3 — Escalation


Over the next six years, Farid crossed out eleven names.

He never decided it as a plan. Each one arrived on its own, one at a time, always by the same road: a debt that no longer had any way of being collected through the usual methods, a debtor who'd gone invisible before the pressure could reach him. Eleven times, the notebook did what the notebook did. Eleven times, Farid reconciled the numbers that same evening and found he was short, exactly, ten percent — never more, never less — and eleven times he told himself he'd understand it later, once he had time to look closely.

He never had to send anything anywhere. He never wrote down an address, never left an envelope for anyone to find. The first few times, he waited — almost without noticing he was doing it — for something or someone to come collect that missing tenth: a letter, a messenger, the man in the dark coat standing by the door again. No one ever came. The money simply stopped being where it should have been, with the same clean, borderless absence with which a debtor stopped being in his rented room. The notebook handled both things at once, in the same instant, with the same stroke of the pen, without Farid ever understanding how, or needing to ask anymore.

He didn't have time, because he didn't make any. That, too, was a decision, even if he never called it one.

Of the eleven, some barely left a mark on his memory — a name, a figure, nothing more, exactly as a bad debt closed on time should be. Others stuck to him whether he wanted them to or not. A fisherman who owed him four hundred dollars on a boat he'd never finished paying off, and who one day simply stopped coming down to the dock — the boat stayed tied up, rocking empty week after week until someone else claimed it. A woman who ran a laundry two streets past his first shop, whose husband had signed for her without her fully knowing it, and who cried at Farid's counter the day she came to beg for more time. That time, Farid actually gave it to her free, without touching the notebook — because something in the way she cried didn't fit the coldness a name-crossing required. Her husband, though, did end up as one of the eleven, months later, when the debt still standing under his name alone became just as impossible to collect as any of the other ten.

Farid didn't let himself think too hard about whether that made him a better person or just a better shot. He liked to believe it was the first. He suspected it was the second — that he'd learned, without meaning to, to choose more carefully who caught the worst of his new trade, the same way he'd learned as a boy to tell, with one glance, who was in a hurry and who wasn't.

With the cushion of debts that always came back at ninety percent, Farid started lending differently. Not faster, not sloppier — he still priced every watch, every face, with the same precision as always — but further out. Before, an unsecured loan to someone he barely knew was a risk he'd rather not take. Now the worst case was no longer losing everything. It was losing a tenth, and a tenth was a price he could absorb without blinking.

Two shops became five in a little over four years. He opened the fifth in a new neighborhood, farther north, with the same ceremony he'd used for the first: none. He hired a young man named Silas to run it, taught him to appraise in three weeks — faster than it had taken Farid himself, though he never told him that — and gave him one instruction he'd never given Dennis, years before: any account that turned impossible, any one that truly had no way of being collected, came straight to him in person, no exceptions, with the boy never told why. Silas assumed it was a matter of delicate business, of contacts he didn't have yet. He never asked the question that would have shown him it wasn't.

Five shops became seven, over the years, scattered through Breakwater and the neighborhoods that resembled it — each one as unremarkable as the first, no sign bigger than its neighbor's, Farid still driving the same car long after he could afford another. By then, discretion wasn't just strategy anymore. It was the only thing separating him from Delvecchio, and he held to that distinction with the same discipline his father had held to never raising his voice at the Dunmore stall.

---

Nico Anselmi owed three hundred twenty dollars, and he stopped showing up one Tuesday in October, the same quiet way the ten before him had already stopped showing up. Farid crossed the name out eleven days later, without ceremony, with the same hand he used for any other account.

His wife came into the Breakwater shop one Thursday afternoon, a girl of about seven gripping the sleeve of her coat.

"I need to know if he came by here," said Rosa Anselmi, with no preamble, in the voice of someone who's already asked the same question in half a dozen places and stopped expecting a useful answer. "Nico. Tall, limps a little on the right leg. He worked as a loader on the south dock."

Farid told her no, that it had been a while since he'd seen him around the shop — which, in some sense he preferred not to examine, was true. Rosa nodded, unsurprised, like someone who'd already counted on that answer before hearing it, and stayed a moment longer than she needed to, looking at the glass counter without really seeing it.

"Do you know if he owed anyone money?" Farid asked, in the same neutral tone he used for any appraisal, though he already knew the answer with a certainty that turned something over inside him.

"Three hundred and something, I think. He never let me see the exact book. Said it was his business." Rosa laughed, without any humor in it. "His business. As if the two of us didn't eat off the same table."

"The strange thing," she said, after a silence, almost to herself, "is that I don't know when it was. The last time I saw him, I mean. I've been turning it over the whole way here and I can't—" She stopped, fingers going to her temple, like someone reaching for something she knows should be there and isn't. "Three weeks ago it felt like three days. Now it feels like three months. It shouldn't be like that. A person knows these things. A person knows when she last saw her husband. Doesn't she?"

"Sure she does," Farid said, and hated the sound of his own voice saying it.

The girl, who hadn't said a word, tugged her mother's sleeve and asked if they could go now. Rosa apologized, mumbled something about her nerves, and left, the girl watching Farid over her shoulder until the door closed behind them both.

Farid stayed behind the counter a good while after the bell stopped ringing.

It wasn't the first time someone had asked about one of the eleven. He'd learned, with practice, to give the same neutral answer, not to hold the asker's eyes too long. But it was the first time he'd watched, up close, what happened to the memory of someone who genuinely wanted to remember, who was trying, and still couldn't hold a date between her fingers. That wasn't people forgetting out of convenience, or shame, or wanting to move on. That was something else, and Farid, who'd spent his whole life reading faces to know when someone was lying, recognized without any doubt that Rosa Anselmi wasn't.

---

The next day, he closed the shop two hours early and went to the south dock himself, something he hadn't done in years — sending someone to ask was another man's job, not his. He found the foreman who'd supervised Nico Anselmi, a man with cracked hands who barely looked up from the net he was mending.

"He left," the foreman said, before Farid could even finish asking. "Came in one day, didn't the next. He's not the first."

"When?"

The man's needle went still halfway through a stitch, and his brow furrowed in a way Farid found painfully familiar.

"Good question," he said, finally, no irony in it. "A month. Two, maybe. You'd have to ask someone with a better head for dates than mine."

No one on the dock had a better head than his. Farid went back to the shop without a single solid date from anyone, and with the cold certainty that it wasn't that Breakwater's people had bad memories. It was that the closer someone had stood to Nico Anselmi, the less exactly they could say when he'd stopped being close — as if the absence, instead of leaving a clean edge where something ended, had eaten the edge too.

---

That night he didn't sleep. He took out the notebook and went through the eleven names, one by one, trying to remember something — a face, a voice, the exact sound of a door closing — beyond the figure and the date. He couldn't. Not because he'd forgotten them the normal way a person forgets a stranger, but because, when he tried, he felt the same faint resistance he'd seen on Rosa Anselmi's face: something sliding away from the question before the question finished forming.

He thought about not using it again. He thought about it seriously, for nearly two weeks, while he let one impossible account rot untouched — a man named Fenwick, two hundred dollars, gone a month with no sign of him anywhere known — waiting to see if the ordinary world, the one made of quiet phone calls and jobless winters, still had anything left to offer him. He sent someone to ask around the usual places, offered an extra coin for information, waited. No one had anything. Not the uncomfortable silence of a debtor hiding on purpose, which Farid knew well and knew how to break — but the exact nothing of someone no one quite remembered ever knowing well, even before he owed anyone money, as if Fenwick had always been, to everyone but Farid, a person only half sketched in.

At the end of the two weeks, a boy from the docks brought him a name for a dollar: a boardinghouse near the train tracks, where someone who looked like Fenwick had been sleeping until a few days before. Farid didn't send anyone that time. He went himself, at night, something he hadn't done since before he had employees — he needed to check, without putting it in those words, whether the old world was still good for anything.

He didn't find Fenwick. He found two men on the boardinghouse stairs with no interest in talking about anyone who owed money, least of all to a stranger whose questions smelled like a collector's. It wasn't a fight he could describe afterward with any pride. It was fast, one-sided, and it ended with him on the ground in the back alley, a rib that would take weeks to heal, and not one cent closer to the two hundred dollars.

He told people at the shop he'd slipped on ice. No one had any reason not to believe him.

He lost the two hundred dollars anyway. Not one cent back, not even the satisfaction of having tried it his own way — just the same dry loss of any bad debt, plus a rib, plus the specific humiliation of finding himself slower and older than he'd believed. That night he found himself resentful in a way he hadn't expected: not about the money. About having paid the full price of refusing something — in money and in body — with no one ever finding out he'd even tried. Not Nico Anselmi. Not Rosa. Not the girl who'd tugged her mother's sleeve. Not even himself, until that night.

It was resentment, more than arithmetic, that decided it for him. He weighed the two outcomes, but not with his usual coldness: a broken rib and two hundred dollars lost, against a stroke of the pen that had never cost him more than a tenth of the paper. The comparison didn't strike him, that night, as entirely fair — a beating and a life carry different weights, and Farid, somewhere he preferred not to visit, knew it — but resentment wasn't asking for a fair comparison. It was only asking to win.

He never again let an impossible account sit untouched.

---

That doesn't mean he stopped thinking about Rosa Anselmi. He saw her, months later, from a distance, in the Breakwater market, the same girl — a little taller now — holding her hand, and something in his stomach closed in a way he didn't fully recognize as guilt, because he didn't let himself call it that. He told himself, instead, what he'd already been telling himself for years: that he hadn't done anything to Nico Anselmi. That Nico Anselmi had decided, on his own, not to pay a real debt, and that whatever happened to him afterward wasn't a punishment Farid had chosen to carry out with his own hands, the way he used to — two phone calls and a winter of lost work. It was cleaner than that. Less his.

He held to that distinction with the same strength he'd held, his whole life, to the one that separated him from Delvecchio. Delvecchio broke arms with his own hands, or sent men who did, and had ended up with the neon sign still lit and no one left inside to turn it off. Farid broke nothing. Farid crossed out a name with the same pen he used to write down the price of a watch, and slept — most nights, after the first few weeks — without his hand shaking. He told himself that was the whole difference, the only one that mattered, and for a long time he nearly managed to believe it completely.

---

By the time he opened the seventh shop, Farid no longer reconciled the numbers with any surprise when a crossed-out account recovered nine dollars out of ten instead of the full ten. He wrote it down exactly the way anyone writes down a partial-loss account — *collected 540 of 600, account closed* — no separate column, no mark to set it apart from the dozens of real settlements any lender closes for less than owed when the debtor swears it's all he has.

Silas balanced those books with him, year after year, and never once asked a question about the numbers themselves — not because he wasn't observant, but because there was nothing there worth questioning. A lending business closes accounts for less than owed all the time. Farid knew that better than anyone: he'd spent eighteen years teaching people that ninety percent collected on time was worth more than a hundred percent that never came. His own system, without his ever planning it that way, hid behind his own business wisdom. That, more than anything else, confirmed how well camouflaged it was.

What he did notice, over the years, lived in no book at all. Every account brought to Farid in person — every single one, without exception, that truly had no way of being collected — closed, and the person behind it stopped existing in Breakwater entirely, not just as a debtor. Silas never crossed paths with them again, not on the street, not at the market, not at church on Sundays. He mentioned it to Farid exactly once, naming a man he'd brought to him nearly a year before.

"What happened to him, in the end?"

"It got settled," Farid said, with no further detail, in the same dry tone he used to announce a watch wasn't worth what its owner thought.

Silas never asked about another one again. But he started, without telling anyone, keeping his own separate count of how many there already were — a habit Farid wouldn't discover until much later, by which point there was nothing left he could do to undo it.

He never had to explain to anyone what that difference really was — he'd already had, for eighteen years, a perfectly real explanation to give, himself included. The two men who knew the whole truth — him, and the one who'd made it possible — hadn't seen each other in years, and Farid, in his most honest moments, which were few, suspected he'd never see him again.

He wasn't wrong about that. What he didn't know — what he had no way of knowing — was that somewhere, far from Breakwater, a man with a hard-to-remember face never thought of him either. That eleven names, and the ones still to come, weren't a count that man bothered keeping. That the lie, set loose years ago now, was growing exactly like a fire someone strikes the first spark for and then goes to sleep without looking back.

Eleven crossed-out names weren't, yet, a pattern anyone else had noticed. But they also weren't, anymore, something Farid could keep calling an accident — and somewhere in those six years, without being able to point to the exact day it happened, he stopped trying to decide whether what he did was right or wrong, and started, instead, deciding only whether it worked. That was an easier question. He'd been answering it his whole life.
