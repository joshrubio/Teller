# Chapter 80 — The Rock

---

The name arrived before the explanation.

"Webb," said the instructor at the tablet, not looking up. "East wing, room 12. Sharing with Hayashi."

Oliver stood. Riku was already on his feet beside him, shoulders still hunched from the cold of the tunnel, and for the first time since the gallery something like relief crossed his face — at least he wasn't going to sleep alone the first night in a place where they didn't even know how many levels there were beneath their feet.

The instructor kept reading. Dara and Petra, room 14. Pier, alone, room 9 — nobody asked why he got a single and the others didn't, and the instructor offered no explanation, as if the question wouldn't even occur to someone who'd been in the system long enough. Asha and Nadia, who'd arrived in the second vehicle minutes after the rest, walked into the waiting room just in time to hear their own names — room 16, together.

"The rest of orientation starts at six," the instructor said, putting the tablet away. "You have until then. Don't get lost. The corridors on this level aren't marked for people who don't know where they're going yet."

Nobody asked what that meant either.

The east wing turned out to be farther than the number suggested — fifteen minutes of corridor, two level changes by stairs, no window the whole way. Oliver counted the turns without meaning to, the same habit he'd developed at the Valais Center to avoid getting lost in a building that changed layout every few months, though here the habit felt useless in a different way: it wasn't that the building changed — it was that it simply hadn't been designed for anyone to fully memorize.

Room 12 had two bunks, a built-in closet with the paint chipping at the corners, and a window that wasn't a window — a cold-light panel programmed to simulate, without much effort, something like daylight. Riku sat down on the bottom bunk without asking which one Oliver wanted, and Oliver didn't correct him. It seemed, somehow, that Riku needed the bottom one more than he did.

He left his bag undone on the bare mattress — sheets folded at the foot, waiting for someone to bother putting them on — and walked over to the cold-light panel, passing his hand in front of it without touching it, watching how the intensity responded to the shadow with a half-second lag, neither instant nor fully natural. At the Valais Center the windows had been real windows, with real views of the Alps that changed color through the day. This was a promise of the same thing, made by someone who knew the promise was enough for most people most of the time.

In the hallway, before they could fully close the door, they heard a voice they didn't recognize arguing with someone in the room across the way — a man, an accent Oliver couldn't quite place, complaining that the hot water was taking too long. Nobody answered him. The door shut with the same dry clang of metal against metal that was already starting to sound familiar.

"Pier's alone," Riku said, still lying down. "You think that's rank, or that nobody else can stand sharing with him?"

"I think it's because nobody's asked Pier what he prefers in years," Oliver said, "and at this point nobody's starting."

Riku laughed, a short sound, almost surprised at himself for making it in a place like this.

"I mean, this is worse than the Valais Center in literally every way," Riku said, staring at the ceiling. "And that place had its stuff."

"We don't know yet if it's worse," Oliver said. "We only know it's different."

"I saw someone break another guy's arm half an hour ago and nobody did anything," Riku said. "I think that already answers the question."

Oliver had no argument for that. He set his bag at the foot of the top bunk and sat on the edge, legs hanging, while Riku pulled out the phone he already knew wasn't going to have signal here and checked it anyway, out of habit more than hope.

"Zero bars," he said, putting it away again. "Obviously."

"Were you going to call someone?" Oliver said.

Riku shrugged, the gesture smaller than it meant to be.

"Nobody in particular," he said. "I just wanted the phone to do something normal, I mean. Ring, load a page, whatever. Something that wasn't from here."

Oliver understood that better than he could have explained. He put his hand to the outer pocket of his jacket, checked that Kaspar's note was still there, and said nothing about it.

They went out to explore before six, not because they'd decided together, but because they both reached the room door at nearly the same time with the same restlessness about staying still. The east wing corridor had other doors, numbered like theirs, and through two of them — open, no one inside at that moment — Oliver caught sight of bunks made with military precision, right-angled corners, nothing out of place.

"They're not like us," Riku said, quietly, though there was no one nearby to hear him.

"No," Oliver said. "I don't think that's the point either."

The mess hall turned out to be farther than the corridor suggested, and fuller. The noise arrived before the door did — overlapping conversation, the metallic screech of dragged chairs, something frying in a kitchen not visible from the entrance, a smell of reheated oil and industrial disinfectant that never quite fully mixed. Long metal tables, occupied in blocks that didn't look accidental — one group here, another there, each with its own geometry of chairs and silences, the kind of arrangement nobody consciously decides but everybody respects without exception. Oliver recognized the pattern before he could name it: this wasn't a mess hall, it was a map of who ate with whom, and by extension, of who trusted whom.

The biggest group occupied two tables pushed together near the back wall — six, maybe seven people in the same gray fatigues Oliver had already seen at the pit. They were laughing about something, the laughter real but contained, the kind that isn't looking to draw the whole room's attention even if it doesn't much mind if it does.

"I'm telling you, the second one never saw it coming," one of them was saying, a heavyset man with a fresh bandage on his forearm, gesturing with his fork. "Not even when he was already on the ground."

"Nobody sees it coming," said another, quieter, not looking up from his plate. "That's literally the point."

"I saw it coming the first time," said a woman at the end of the table, in an accent Oliver couldn't place. "That's why I don't eat during his shift anymore."

Laughter again, looser this time. At the center of the table, without anyone having formally assigned it but obvious just from watching where the heads turned when he spoke, sat the man from the pit — hair cut nearly to the scalp, eating with the same economy of movement he'd shown fighting, not one wasted gesture.

"Nobody has to see it coming," he said, without raising his voice much, and still the whole table went quiet to hear him. "That's what most people still don't get. It's not about hiding the hit. It's about not deciding the hit until the last possible moment, so there's nothing to see coming."

The man with the bandage nodded, as if he'd just received the explanation for something he'd spent days trying to work out on his own. The woman with the accent raised her glass, half in jest, half in genuine respect, and the rest of the table did the same without anyone coordinating it — the kind of shared reflex that only exists in groups that have already decided, collectively and without needing to vote on it, who they follow.

It was then, in the pause that followed the improvised toast, that he looked up and fixed his gaze directly on Oliver from across the room.

It wasn't a casual glance. It lasted longer than a casual glance should, and there was no curiosity in it, no open hostility, just a cold, complete assessment, the kind reserved for something whose status — problem, or just a variable — hasn't been decided yet. Oliver held the look just long enough not to seem like he was fleeing it, then broke eye contact first, aware he'd just lost something he hadn't even known was at stake.

"That's the one who won the fight," Riku said, very quietly. "And those are clearly his."

"I know," Oliver said, without fully pulling his attention from the back table, where the conversation had already flowed on again as if the exchange of looks had never happened.

Near the vending machine, sitting alone with a dossier she didn't seem to be reading, was a woman with blond hair pulled back with precision, posture straight even seated. She looked up as they passed, took their measure in under a second — not hostile, just exact — and went back to the dossier.

"New?" she said, before either of them had come any closer.

"We got in today," Oliver said.

"Shows," she said. It wasn't cruel, just a statement of fact. "Johanna."

"Oliver."

"Riku."

Johanna closed the dossier, though she didn't stand.

"A piece of advice, if you want it," she said. "Nobody here is going to tell you the rules twice. The first time someone explains something to you, write it down, even if it's just in your head."

"And the second time?" Riku said.

"The person who explained it isn't going to be around anymore," Johanna said, and offered nothing more, going back to the dossier with the same economy she'd used to speak.

An announcement sounded over some speaker Oliver couldn't locate in time, three short tones followed by a recorded voice repeating the orientation room number. The whole mess hall moved at once, not abruptly, but with the kind of synchrony that only appears when everyone has made the same trip enough times to stop thinking about it. Oliver looked for the man from the pit among the people getting up and found him already on his feet, the conversation with his group cut off mid-sentence, walking toward the exit without looking back.

The orientation room was bigger than any space they'd seen so far — rows of folding chairs facing a dark screen, already half full when Oliver's group walked in, the seven of them scattered among seats that weren't arranged for them to sit together. A man in gray uniform, older than the instructors from the pit — the same one who'd run that afternoon's exercise, though nobody had told them his name yet — stepped up onto the platform with no introduction, no preamble.

"For those arriving today," he said, not raising his voice much, trusting the silence to do the work for him, "three things before anything else."

He pressed something on a tablet. The screen behind him showed a schematic map of the complex, most of the levels marked only with numbers, the same as the map in the waiting room.

"One: evaluation. Everything you do here is measured — combat, knowledge, judgment. There's no single test that decides anything. There's a record built day by day, and that record is what ultimately determines whether you leave this building as something more than what you were when you came in."

"Two," he went on, no pause for questions, "this is not a retention program. Nobody is required to stay. If at any point you decide this isn't for you, there's an exit on the access level, unguarded, unlocked, available at all times. They call it the Free Gate. Cross it if you want. Nobody will chase you, nobody will call you, and nobody in this building will mention your name again after you do."

A different silence moved through the room, not the silence from before the talk, but a denser one, as if several people present were remembering something specific as they heard it. Oliver searched, unable to help himself, for the back table from the mess hall among the rows of folding chairs — found the man from the pit three rows ahead, the same stillness as before, no sign that the mention of the gate told him anything new. Someone who already knew that part of the speech by heart.

"Three," the man said, and for the first time something like real weight entered his voice, "this center doesn't exist to make you feel special. It exists because there are cases the rest of the system can't afford to handle through normal channels — too dangerous, too atypical, or both at once — and someone has to be ready for them before they show up, not after. This isn't official on any organizational chart you'll ever see. That's all you need to know for today."

He switched off the screen. With no more ceremony than he'd used to start, he stepped down from the platform and left through a side door, leaving the whole room with the feeling that the meeting hadn't fully ended, only cut off at the exact point where it suited someone to cut it.
