# Chapter 78 — Two Fronts

---

The room had no official name that anyone used. Voss had been showing up there for two days, and in that time had already picked up the same habit as everyone else: the room, nothing more, said with the tone of something that no longer needs an introduction.

Horseshoe table, sixteen chairs, one for each branch with its own vote, plus the ones added without a vote when the topic required it. Voss had come in with two folders, not one — she'd brought them together on purpose, knowing that separating them would only give the council two chances to say no instead of one.

Park Taeho, director of AMAT-KR, sat in his chair with the same distant courtesy he'd been giving her since they'd known each other — full attention, no sign of which way he was leaning. The director of AMAT-Switzerland, a man with sharp features and rimless glasses whom Voss had known for years without either owing the other anything, had a printed copy of the first folder in front of him, already annotated in the margins — he'd come prepared to ask the hard questions, which, in a way, was better than coming without them.

Cartwright sat at the back, no vote on this item of the agenda, with the kind of stillness that in him didn't mean disinterest.

Outside, Geneva was waking to the kind of uniform gray sky that couldn't quite decide whether it was going to rain. Voss had crossed the city on foot from the hotel, forty minutes she usually used to put her thoughts in order — though at this stage in the process there was nothing left to order. The two folders had been written months ago, reviewed, rewritten until every sentence said exactly what it needed to say and nothing more. What she needed to put in order this morning was patience.

Voss felt the space before she sat down, the habit of years reading proportions without deciding to — the volume of the room, sixteen chairs and the walls that held them, denser today than usual. It wasn't an active reading, just the background perception that never fully switched off: more people in the non-voting seats, more screens connected from branches that didn't normally bother following sessions like this live. Someone had spread word that today she was presenting both documents together. That alone told her something about how many people considered today's outcome to matter.

"Let's start with the group," the session chair said, not looking up from his own papers. "The status of the Valais group."

Voss opened the first folder.

"Seven subjects, all with documented operational history during the April crisis. None holds a formal classification as an agent. None, either, has a framework under which they could hold one," she said. "That's what I'm asking this council to resolve. Not an exception. A framework."

"Why now?" said the director of AMAT-Switzerland, not looking up from his notes. "They've been operating without that framework for months. What changed?"

"Nothing changed," Voss said. "That's exactly the problem. They've been operating without a framework for months because there was never enough urgency to create one. The urgency isn't going to appear on its own. Someone has to decide it already exists."

The director of AMAT-Switzerland looked up for the first time.

"That's an elegant way of saying you want us to act without the pressure we normally need to act."

"It's an exact way of saying it," Voss said. "Yes."

A brief silence. Park Taeho hadn't said anything yet, and Voss knew, from six years of shared sessions, that his silence wasn't indifference — it was the kind of wait that precedes a question already formed, only waiting for the right moment.

"St. Gotthard," Park Taeho said, finally. "Why there, and not a civilian facility?"

"Because the risk isn't civilian," Voss said. "The group trained six months in Valais under controlled conditions and still lost two members who decided the institution didn't deserve their trust. That doesn't get fixed with more comfort. It gets fixed with an environment that doesn't let anyone pretend the risk is smaller than it is."

"That also describes any retention failure as pedagogical strength," the director of AMAT-Switzerland said. "With respect."

"I'm not asking for the failure to be ignored," Voss said. "I'm asking for it to be recognized that the failure already happened under the current system, and the current system has no mechanism to keep it from happening again. That's the second thing I'm bringing today."

A voice from one of the non-voting seats, further back in the horseshoe, cut in before Voss could open the second folder.

"With respect, Evaluator Voss," the man said — Voss couldn't immediately place his name, a minor branch that rarely spoke up in sessions of this weight — "you've spent two days talking about risk and framework. You haven't said a word about what happens if the council approves the classification and one of these seven does something this body can't justify afterward. What guarantee do we have?"

"None," Voss said. "The same guarantee this council had before approving my classification, thirty years ago, or that of any other senior evaluator in this room. The risk doesn't disappear with the framework. The framework only decides who's responsible for managing it when it appears."

Nobody answered that right away. The session chair made a brief note, and Voss recognized the gesture — not agreement, just the record that the question had been asked and answered, available to whoever wanted to review it later.

She opened the second folder.

It was thicker than the first, with the visible wear of nine months of revisions — pages reordered, annotations in at least three different hands, a letterhead dated from the last time Voss had formally presented it, back in January, before anyone in that room had reason to pay it special attention.

"Evaluation methodology for Domains with no measurable outward expression," Voss said. "The proposal has been in front of this council for nine months. The reason I'm bringing it alongside the Valais group isn't coincidence. One of the seven subjects in that group has exactly the type of Domain this methodology is designed to evaluate. There's no honest way to approve the first framework without resolving the second. You'd be formalizing protocols for people you already know you can't fully classify."

"This is about 2025-UK-004," Cartwright said, from the back, the first time he'd spoken. The code needed no translation for anyone in that room with more than a year in the post — everyone knew that behind that designation was Isabel Heredia, though nobody on the council ever called her by name.

"It's about the fact that nine months ago I proposed this working group after losing someone for not having it, and this council still hasn't approved a single line," Voss said, without turning toward him. "2025-UK-004 is the reason I proposed it. It's not the only reason it's needed."

The session chair turned a page without saying anything. The director of AMAT-Switzerland went back to his notes, crossed something out, wrote something else.

"The framework doesn't exist yet," he said, finally. "That's not a judgment on your people, Evaluator Voss. It's a description of where we are. A new protocol for subjects with no prior category needs more than one evaluator's word, however senior."

"I understand the position," Voss said. "I also understand that 'the framework doesn't exist yet' is a sentence that can be repeated indefinitely without anyone being responsible for it continuing not to exist."

Nobody answered that directly. The session chair looked around the table, counting something that wasn't formal votes yet — the temperature of the room before the temperature turned into a decision.

"We're going to review both documents with more time than we have today," he said. "That's not a no. It's not a yes either. It's what it is: more time."

"How much time, in concrete terms?" Voss said. "I ask because I've been getting the same answer for nine months, and 'more time' without a date behind it is, in practice, indistinguishable from a no. I'd rather have an honest no than a promise nobody plans to keep this quarter."

The session chair looked up, the first visible reaction he'd shown the whole session.

"A few weeks," he said, after a moment. "We come back to this in a few weeks, with a decision, not another review. Does that work for you, Evaluator Voss?"

"It works," Voss said. "Have the commitment entered into the record."

Someone at the back of the room typed something, and Voss assumed that was exactly it — being logged where no one could later pretend they hadn't noticed.

Park Taeho nodded, once, committing to nothing more than having listened. The director of AMAT-Switzerland closed his copy of the folder with a gesture that wasn't hostile, but wasn't a promise either.

Voss closed both folders. It wasn't the answer she'd come looking for. Nor was it the one she'd expected to get today — two days of meetings didn't undo nine months of institutional inertia all at once, and she'd known that before walking in. What she was taking from this session wasn't a victory. It was a future date on which the question would be asked again, with less room for the answer to still be "more time."

She stepped out into the hallway with Cartwright a pace behind.

"Partial progress," Cartwright said, in a tone that wasn't looking to be comfort. "It's more than you got last time."

"Last time I didn't have two folders," Voss said. "Let's see if two folders are enough when someone in that room decides they don't want to keep reviewing with more time."

They walked a stretch of the hallway in silence, the echo of the previous session still dissolving behind the double doors. Cartwright broke the silence first.

"The one from the minor branch, the one who asked about the guarantee. Did you know him?"

"No," Voss said. "And that interests me more than the question itself. Nobody from that branch speaks up in classification sessions without someone asking them to."

"You think someone sent him to ask that?"

"I think someone wanted it on the record that the question was asked, without whoever wanted it having to do it under their own name," Voss said. "I don't know who yet. I'm going to find out before the next session."

Cartwright said nothing to that, but Voss recognized the silence as filed away, not dismissed — Cartwright had spent half a lifetime keeping that kind of detail for the moment it turned out useful.

They reached the elevator. Voss pressed the button and waited, gaze fixed on the number descending floor by floor, no apparent hurry to fill the silence.

"A few weeks," Cartwright said, finally. "What are you going to do with them?"

"Find out who sent that man to ask about guarantees," Voss said. "And find someone in that room willing to tell me why they care enough not to do it under their own name."

"And if you don't find out in time?"

"Then I come back in a few weeks with the same two folders and one more question," Voss said. "This isn't the first time this council has made me repeat the work. I don't think it'll be the last."

The elevator doors opened. Voss stepped in; Cartwright stayed on the threshold a moment longer, as if he'd been about to say something else and had decided, at the last second, that it wasn't the time.

"Voss," he said, as the doors were already starting to close. "That name you didn't recognize. I didn't recognize it either. And I've been in that room fifteen years."

The doors closed before Voss could ask him what he meant by that.
