# Chapter 44 — What's Left Standing

"I didn't come to see anything, Zoltán," Katalin had said, and the sentence was still hanging in the air when she closed the distance first, not waiting for Park to say anything.

Zoltán didn't move to meet her. He tilted his body a degree, just enough for Katalin's first strike to graze past his already-burned ribs, and in the same motion caught her wrist — not to hurt her, to stop her in the air, a full second of suspension where cousin and cousin looked at each other from fifteen centimeters away.

"You still move the way I taught you," he said.

"You didn't teach me this," she answered, and twisted her wrist against the pressure of his hand instead of against the natural angle, breaking free with a move Park didn't recognize from any lineage he'd seen so far.

That bought her half a second. No more.

Zoltán was already turning back toward her when Katalin used the momentum of the break free to climb onto the edge of the damaged railing, three quick steps over the twisted iron, and jumped from there with her full body weight dropping into an elbow strike at the back of his neck. Zoltán felt it coming an instant before it landed — turned his shoulder, absorbing part of the hit with the motion instead of blocking it whole — and Katalin hit the cracked asphalt rolling, already hunting the next angle before she'd finished getting up.

"You still don't close your low guard," she said, out of breath. "That hasn't changed either."

"Neither have you," he answered, and something like a real smile, not a cynical one, crossed his face half a second before the Shackle switched it off again.

---

Park came in from the right flank while Zoltán's attention was still on Katalin, going for the same point the AMAT-HU agent had missed earlier — the Shackle, the left wrist. He didn't reach it. Zoltán spun on his own axis without fully letting go of Katalin, and the elbow that came out of that turn carried so much concentrated Pressure that Park felt the air compress before contact, like an invisible wall that hit his chest first.

Park absorbed what he could with Inertia — not all of it. What he didn't absorb he felt in his ribs, a dull pain that cut off his breath for an instant, and the impact sent him three meters back, to the edge of the crack Zoltán himself had opened in the asphalt minutes before.

"A month ago, that hit would have dislocated my own shoulder," Zoltán said, not closing in yet. "Now I don't even need to recover before the next one."

Park got to his feet. The pain in his ribs hadn't eased, but it didn't matter anymore — he'd spent the whole night building reserve without spending it, since the pumping station, and for the first time since Seoul he felt like he had enough saved up for something that wasn't calibrated, wasn't small.

---

Katalin came back at him from his blind side, forcing Zoltán to split his attention again. Park used that half second to close in a straight line — not an attack yet, just distance — and when Zoltán turned to intercept him, Park's arm was already loaded with something he'd been holding since Lisbon.

He feinted at the torso. Zoltán moved to block the torso — the same mistake Park had already watched others make that night, reading the gesture instead of the vector — and the real strike changed angle mid-flight, going straight for the left shoulder, the arm that held the Shackle.

It connected. Square, with Mass concentrated into his fist, the first strike of the night Park threw meaning to do real damage, not to contain.

Zoltán staggered back half a step — the first ground he'd given up in the whole fight — and something in his face switched off for an instant, the same blankness Park had already seen twice from the other end of the bridge. When his eyes focused again, the smile he'd had before was gone.

"That one hurt," he said, and this time it didn't sound like a compliment.

---

What came next left no time to read anything.

Zoltán struck the ground with an open palm — not at Park, not at Katalin, at the bridge deck itself — and a concentric wave of pressure burst out from the point of contact in every direction, cracking the asphalt in a six-meter radius and shaking the riveted steel under their feet with a crack Park felt climb up his legs before he heard it.

Katalin lost her footing. Park didn't — he absorbed the shock through his feet, the same technique he'd used against the tremor at the pumping station, but this time the tremor wasn't localized. It was the whole bridge.

"The north tower!" Katalin shouted, already on her knees, pointing up.

One of the main cables, the one holding the central span, had a visible sag that hadn't been there a minute before — something had come loose at the anchor point, somewhere none of the three of them could see from where they stood. The groan Park had been hearing since he arrived wasn't background noise anymore. It was a countdown.

---

Zoltán didn't wait for them to process that. He closed the distance with Katalin first — faster than Park could get between them — and lifted her by the throat with one hand, her feet already off the ground.

"I don't want to do this to you," he said, and for the first time all night something in his voice sounded like the man he should have been, not like what he had strapped to his wrist. "Let it go. Tell Park to leave. I can still end this without anyone else in the family getting hurt."

Katalin couldn't answer with her throat closed, but she shook her head, once, slowly.

Park didn't have time to think through a strategy — only to move. He closed the distance in two steps and struck the arm holding Katalin, not Zoltán's body — an angle calculated to break the grip, not to cause damage where there was no room for feints.

Zoltán let her go, but not because the strike forced him to. He dropped her and turned on Park with his free arm already loaded, the elbow tracing an arc that was no ordinary strike — Park recognized it half a second too late, the same principle of concentrated Mass he used himself, only amplified by something with no reserve limit to respect.

Park got his forearm up. The impact bent him backward, the bone protesting under the improvised Class-R he'd reinforced the limb with that same afternoon, and this time the pain didn't fade in a second. It stayed, sharp, while Zoltán was already loading the next one.

Faster than the two previous times. Less time to recover from the last hit. Park felt, for the first time that night, that the reserve he'd been building since the pumping station wasn't infinite — that at some point, not far off, he was going to have to spend it all at once or lose what margin he had left to choose when.

---

Katalin, still on the ground, didn't stay there. She dragged herself toward the torn-loose railing panel — the same iron fragment the fourth agent had failed to use minutes earlier — and instead of charging it with a combat Domain she didn't have, she used it as a lever against the sagging cable's mechanism, forcing the anchor back into position with whatever strength was left in her arms.

She didn't fully fix it. She bought time — the groan dropped in pitch, the countdown slowed, it didn't stop.

"We've got minutes, not more!" she shouted, not letting go of the iron.

Fifteen meters away, one of the fallen AMAT-HU agents — the one Zoltán had let go of with something like respect, the one still breathing but unable to move his legs — ended up directly in the path of the next strike as the deck gave a few more centimeters, closer to the tower. Katalin saw it before Park did.

"The agent!" she shouted, already running toward him instead of toward the fight, leaving the iron half-adjusted.

Park couldn't cover both fronts at once. He chose to trust that Katalin would reach the agent before the deck finished opening, and used that full second, the only one Zoltán gave him without meaning to, to close the distance again.

Park barely heard her. Zoltán was already on him again, and this time the attack wasn't aimed at his body — it went for the ground under Park's feet, the same pressure-wave technique that had shaken the whole bridge, but concentrated now, aimed, a crater opening directly beneath where Park stood.

Park jumped before the asphalt finished giving way — not backward, toward Zoltán, using the collapsing ground itself as extra momentum, the inertia of the falling surface adding to his own jump. He hit the air level with Zoltán's head, both fists loaded, Mass and Inertia stacked together for the first time that night.

Zoltán crossed both arms to guard. The impact of Park against that block sounded like something breaking — it wasn't clear whose, not yet, until they both hit the cracked ground, and Park saw blood in Zoltán's mouth before he felt the pain in his own knuckles.

Off to the side, Katalin had already pulled the unconscious agent out of the crater's radius, dragging him by the armpits into the relative cover of an abandoned car. She had time to check nothing more than his pulse before turning back toward the fight.

---

Neither of them stayed down long enough for the other to finish it. Zoltán moved first, grabbing Park by the collar of his coat with one hand and throwing him — not hitting him, throwing him — toward the sagging stretch of cable Katalin had been holding together minutes before.

Park turned in the air, reaching for the cable with both hands instead of trying to brace against the impact with the steel. He half managed it — the taut metal sliced his right palm, but it also gave him something to absorb: all the tension built up in that stretch since the anchor started to give. He let go at the exact angle where the tension threw him back toward Zoltán instead of into the void beneath the bridge, the cable's own inertia adding to his, and he reached the second strike faster than his own body should have been able to accelerate on its own.

Zoltán raised his forearm to block, but Park wasn't aiming for the forearm. His heel connected with the knee that had already been limping since Lisbon, bending it the wrong way, and this time it was Zoltán who went down — a short cry, real, the kind of pain no Shackle could fully switch off.

The cable, its tension already spent by Park, snapped completely with a metallic crack that echoed through the whole river valley. An entire stretch of the north railing gave way toward the water.

---

They got to their feet almost at the same time. Neither of them clean.

"Rank three, right?" Zoltán asked, spitting blood to one side. "Maybe two, after tonight. I don't even know what number I'm at anymore. The Shackle doesn't come with a label."

"You don't need to know," Park said. "You just need to stop."

"Everyone's been telling me that since 2011," Zoltán said, and for a second he sounded almost amused, something tired and old behind the broken smile. "Nobody ever told me how."

"I can't," Zoltán said, and for an instant, half a second, something in his face wasn't defiance or calculation. It was fear. "I don't know how to stop this anymore. Every time I let go of it, it wants to close again. Every time it's harder to open my eyes and be the one looking through them."

The groan from the north tower rose in volume again. Somewhere over their heads, a rivet gave way completely and dropped into the water with a small sound, almost ridiculous, compared to what it announced.

---

That was when Mariana arrived.

Not running, not shouting a name: she crossed what was left of the bridge's north lane with the same calm stride Park had seen her use in Mouraria, as if the chaos around her were familiar ground instead of a crisis. She wore the same dark coat from Lisbon, an old burn still visible on the back of her left hand, and carried nothing else — no weapon, no backup, as if she'd decided the only thing she needed for what was coming was to be there. She stopped ten meters from the three of them, and the air around her started emptying in a way Park recognized immediately — the same ambient void of A Saudade, but directed, concentrated, not the slow diffusion he'd seen in Lisbon.

"Zoltán," she said, and her voice carried more weight than anything Park or Katalin had said in the whole fight. "Rafa is dead."

Zoltán turned toward her, and for the first time all night lost the thread of what he was doing completely. The arm he'd been holding up against Park dropped, not from fatigue — from something that broke inside him before the Shackle could stop it.

"I know," he said, his voice breaking in a way it hadn't the whole rest of the night. "I knew before anyone told me."

"Then you know what it feels like to lose someone of your own blood because you let something bigger decide for you," Mariana said, already closing in, the void around her swallowing the bridge's ambient noise until only their voices were left. "I still haven't decided whether I came to save you or to finish what I started that night in the machine room."

Park didn't move. Neither did Katalin. Neither of them had seen Mariana really fight, but something in the way the air around her went still — not absent, held, like a breath deliberately kept — told Park that what he was watching wasn't a rescue entrance. It was a reckoning that had been postponed since Lisbon.

"I don't need you to decide anything," Zoltán said, but his voice no longer carried the weight it had before. "I need you to step back, Mariana. I mean it."

"I already stepped back once," Mariana said. "I let you walk out of that room hurt instead of dead because I still loved you more than I trusted my own judgment. Rafa paid for that decision. I'm not making it the same way twice."

Zoltán moved before she finished the sentence — not at Park, not at Katalin. Straight at Mariana, his open palm going for the same half-second contact that had emptied out an entire agent in the bridge's first exchange.

Mariana didn't dodge it. She let Zoltán's hand touch her forearm, and at the instant of contact the void she'd been holding stopped being ambient and turned direct — she didn't absorb the strike, she absorbed the intention behind it, the concentration of Odem coming apart before it finished forming, the same way she'd drained Zoltán in the machine room at Santa Justa, only this time with no grief between them holding her back halfway.

"Not again," she said, and for the first time all night it was Zoltán who stepped back from something that wasn't physical pain.

---

That didn't stop him. He turned to the fallen railing, closed his hand around an entire section of torn iron panel — nearly two meters of twisted metal, heavier than a human body should be able to lift with one arm — and hurled it at Mariana in a straight line, loaded with the same pressure wave that had been opening craters in the asphalt all night.

Mariana stretched both hands toward the iron in the air, and the void closed around the metal like a second gravity, draining the charge before it arrived — but not all of it. What was left kept its course, slower, less lethal, and still fast enough to split the air.

Katalin saw the corrected trajectory an instant before anyone else — the iron, barely deflected, was going to miss Mariana and catch Park square in the side.

She stepped into its path.

The impact lifted her off the ground and threw her four meters into what was left of the south railing, the sound of her shoulder giving out audible even over the groan of the bridge — not a clean hit, the bone popping out of its socket at an angle no training made any less painful. Katalin cried out, once, short, and stayed slumped against the iron unable to use her right arm, the sleeve already darkening where something inside had cut more than it should have.

"Katalin!" Park reached her in two strides, but she was already shaking her head before he touched her.

"I'm fine," she lied, her voice tight, the shoulder visibly out of place under the darkened fabric. "Go. There's nothing more I can do here anyway."

It wasn't entirely true and they both knew it. But the arm wasn't responding, and arguing the point would have cost Park the exact second Zoltán needed to start moving again.

---

Zoltán, on the other side, was getting ready to close the distance with Mariana again — faster now, the Shackle pulling at him with something that no longer looked entirely like his own will — and this time he jumped. Not a normal jump: fifteen meters from one end of the damaged span to the other, the kind of jump no unreinforced human body should survive landing, and when his feet hit the asphalt the shockwave from the impact knocked down Park, Mariana, and the nearest abandoned cars in a ten-meter radius, windows blowing out in a cascade the whole length of the central span.

Park got up with his mouth bleeding, a loose tooth he didn't bother checking. Mariana was already on her feet, the void around her tighter than before, like a rope about to snap.

"Every jump like that brings him closer to the end," she said, not taking her eyes off Zoltán. "Not the end of the fight. The end of him."

"Then let's finish this before it decides on its own how to do that," Park said.

He ran for the nearest crater, the same one Zoltán had opened minutes before, and instead of going around it he jumped in, using the depth as launch momentum — a burst of reserve built up since the pumping station, spent with precision, not desperation. He shot up and forward, higher than any jump of his had ever reached, eight meters of air crossed in under a second, Mass and Inertia concentrated into both fists at once.

Zoltán raised his arms to block, but this time Park didn't arrive alone. Mariana closed the void around the point of contact a fraction of a second before impact, draining whatever Zoltán was trying to gather to reinforce his guard. Park's strike connected clean, with nothing on the other side to cushion it.

The sound was the biggest of the whole night — not a crack, a boom felt in the riverbed itself. Zoltán went flying twelve meters, tore through what was left of an abandoned car, and came to a stop against the north tower's pillar, the vehicle's metal bent around his body like foil.

He didn't get up right away.

When he did, he pulled himself out of the bent metal slowly, one arm hanging at an angle that should have been impossible to ignore, and still took two steps before stopping completely — not because his body allowed it, but because something older than his own will decided, in that moment, that it had taken enough.

---

The Shackle, on Zoltán's wrist, flared — not light, something denser, a darkening of the metal Park felt before he saw it, as if the entity inside had noticed Mariana's void closing in and decided, on its own, that it wasn't going to let itself be shut off that easily.

Zoltán screamed — not with rage, with something Park had no exact word for — and dropped to his knees on the cracked asphalt, both hands on his head, while something under his skin, in his left forearm, started moving in a way no human muscle should move.

Katalin took a step toward him, her hand already reaching out, the mark on her own wrist visible for an instant under her sleeve, and Park caught her by the arm before she got any closer.

"You don't know what it'll do if you touch him now," he said.

"I don't care," she answered, not taking her eyes off her cousin. "It's the only thing I have left of trying to do this right, after four years of getting everything else wrong."

Mariana didn't move from where she stood, but the void she held around herself expanded another meter, swallowing even the echo of the river under the bridge. Zoltán lifted his face toward her, and for half a second his eyes weren't those of a man arguing with the woman he'd loved — they were something older, looking out through him like through a window that no longer fully belonged to him.

"That's not him looking at you right now," Mariana said, low, almost to herself. She stepped back half a step, not from fear — recalculating, the same calm she'd shown reading Bruno the first time Park saw her use A Saudade with purpose, adjusting whatever she was about to do to something that was no longer quite a man on the other end.

It didn't give her time to adjust anything.

What came out of Zoltán wasn't a directed discharge — it was an expulsion, something that no longer asked his will for permission, and Mariana's void closed around it without being able to fully hold it. Park saw her fold forward, one hand on her chest, blood bursting from her nose all at once, as if she'd tried to swallow an entire river and her own body had said enough halfway through.

"Mariana!" Katalin went to her with the one arm she still had working, and this time Park didn't stop her — there was nothing left to stop.

Mariana didn't quite go down, but she couldn't raise the void again either. She stayed on her knees, one hand on the cracked asphalt, breath short.

"I can't keep holding this," she said, not looking up. "Whatever's going to happen now, it's going to happen faster than I can keep up with again."

The whole bridge groaned once more, long, deep, a sound Park felt in the soles of his feet before he fully heard it. Somewhere above the north tower, something that had spent a hundred fifty years holding exactly the weight expected of it finally stopped being able to — and in the same instant, Zoltán got to his feet in a motion that had nothing human left in it, his arm already hanging straightened on its own, and took off running toward the Buda end of the bridge at a speed neither Katalin, with her broken shoulder, nor Mariana, hollowed out inside, had any chance of matching.

Park did.

He didn't think about it. He ran after him, leaving the two women behind, the sound of the tower giving way at his back mixing with his own footsteps — the only person on that bridge with enough reserve, and legs fast enough, not to lose sight of him completely.
