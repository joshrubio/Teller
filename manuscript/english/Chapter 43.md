# Chapter 43 — Node

Katalin's car had been climbing northeast for twelve minutes, toward a road that didn't even have a name on the map she was trying to follow from memory, when the phone buzzed again. This time it wasn't Ilona with a routine update. It was a call, direct, and Katalin put it on speaker without asking permission.

"You need to turn around," Ilona said, no preamble. "The reading we were tracking toward the ruins moved. Fast. He's back in the city."

"Where?" Park asked.

"Liberty Bridge. Szabadság híd. Rush hour, mid-afternoon, normal traffic and people crossing on foot. We already deployed a containment team before you two picked up."

Katalin was already swinging the car around on the shoulder, without fully slowing down first.

"How many?" Park asked.

"Six. The best we had available inside an hour."

"And the ruins?"

"Empty," Ilona said. "Whatever he went there to get, he's already got it on."

The line went dead. Katalin accelerated back toward the city without saying anything for almost a full minute. Her hands on the wheel, white at the knuckles, more than the sharp turn alone explained.

"I've spent four years imagining this moment one way," she said finally, not taking her eyes off the road. "Me finding him first. Me talking to him before anyone else could. I never imagined it would be this — a bridge full of people and a whole AMAT-HU team already on the ground before I even got there to see it."

"If he came back to the city wearing it," she said, "it's because he needs something the ruins don't have. Scale. People. The river out in the open, not hidden."

"Why the bridge?"

"Because it's over the water," Katalin said, "and because there's no way to clear it in the time anyone has left."

Park's phone rang before he could ask anything else. Cartwright, not Ilona.

"Level 2 meant I'd be kept informed," Cartwright said, no greeting. "It didn't mean I'd stay on the phone if this turned into what Ilona just described to me. I'm on my way. I'm not going to make it in time for the bridge, Agent — but I'm going to make it in time for whatever comes after, and I want that on the record, for you and for me."

"Why are you telling me this now?" Park asked.

"Because if this goes the way Ilona's afraid it will, someone with my signature needs to be close by when people start asking what AMAT was doing letting a Korean civilian face this alone in the middle of a bridge full of people," Cartwright said. "Do what you have to do. You're not alone on paper anymore, even if you are on the street."

She hung up before Park could answer. Katalin said nothing, but something in the way her grip tightened on the wheel told Park she'd understood the whole sentence just as well as he had: nobody was arriving in time. Everyone was arriving in time for what came after.

---

They saw it from three hundred meters — traffic stopped in both directions, people getting out of their cars instead of laying on the horn, a knot of pedestrians running toward the Pest side instead of Buda. The bridge itself, green and old, with the riveted iron towers Park had seen on postcards without ever really looking at them, still standing. Still.

The air, even at that distance, carried the same weight Park had felt near the pumping station the night before — but multiplied, dense in a way he hadn't felt before, not in Lisbon, not in Seoul, as if the river itself beneath the bridge were breathing faster than it should.

An older man was dragging a woman by the arm toward the south exit of the bridge, shouting something in Hungarian Park didn't need translated to understand. A car sat abandoned with the door open and the engine still running, stalled halfway across a lane, nobody coming back for it. Nobody was filming on their phone — that, more than anything else, told Park the people still fleeing understood, without needing it explained, that this wasn't something they wanted close enough for long enough to get a photo.

At a hundred fifty meters, the first of the five AMAT-HU agents was already on the ground — not fully still, one arm moving without coordination, as if the signal meant to arrive from the brain had gotten lost halfway there.

At a hundred meters, Park saw Zoltán for the first time since Lisbon.

He didn't look like the same man who'd limped out of the machine room at Santa Justa. He moved differently — faster than a body with those burns should move, and around his left wrist, something dark, old metal, clamped against the skin as if it had been there his whole life instead of hours. He still had the limp, the bandaged arm under his sleeve, the skin drawn tight from the fire in Lisbon — but he moved through those wounds now, not despite them, as if pain had stopped being information his body bothered to register.

A second agent went flying toward the bridge railing — he didn't go into the water; the riveted iron itself stopped him, with a sound Park felt in his teeth before he fully heard it.

"That's the Shackle," Katalin said, already getting out of the car. "I've never seen it worn. Not even in photographs."

---

Park didn't have time to fully process what he was seeing before it happened again. The four agents still standing moved at once — coordinated, trained, one closing in from the north with something Park didn't recognize (an area-containment Domain, a mesh of Odem visible like heat over asphalt), the other two flanking.

Zoltán didn't wait for them to finish getting into position.

The one from the north extended his hands first, the mesh starting to weave between his fingers: the feint of a complete net, meant to make Zoltán hesitate between dodging it and breaking through it head-on. Zoltán did neither. He read the pattern an instant before it closed — the threads of heat still unconnected at the left edge, the exact point where the mesh was only intention and not yet a net — and went straight there, not to the center where the agent expected the impact.

He touched the man's shoulder before the mesh finished deploying — half a second of contact, nothing more — and the agent folded inward, not backward, as if something had emptied his chest of air and will at the same time. The containment mesh dissolved half-formed, the threads of heat going out one by one on the asphalt like embers.

The other two were already on him when that happened. One of them landed what looked like a clean hit — the fist connecting square with Zoltán's jaw, the kind of impact that should have dropped him.

Zoltán didn't even take the full step back.

"A few hours ago that would have hurt," he said, and the agent was still processing the sentence when Zoltán's hand closed around the wrist of the fist he'd just thrown.

It wasn't a move Park had seen coming from anyone yet, not even from himself. Zoltán didn't return the punch. He let the Shackle pull through him — a full second where something in his face switched off, his eyes losing focus as if he were listening to something that wasn't coming from the street — and out of the point of contact came a discharge that wasn't a detonation and wasn't Pressure, a compact wave of force that burst outward in every direction from his hand, with no need to concentrate it into one point first. The agent went flying six meters. The asphalt under Zoltán's feet cracked in a perfect circle.

Katalin went still for a moment, looking at the crack.

"That's not his Domain," she said, almost without voice. "That's the entity, unfiltered."

---

The fourth agent, still on his feet, saw the iron railing panel torn loose by the earlier hit and charged it with his own Domain before Zoltán finished turning toward him — a meter-long fragment, its edge already sharp from the break, loaded with something Park never quite identified before it went flying straight at the exact point where the Shackle gripped Zoltán's wrist. Good read. The object, not the man, was the real target.

Zoltán didn't dodge it. He raised his right forearm and something hardened over the skin an instant before impact — not a full Mantle, barely a layer, just enough for the fragment to bounce off without cutting. The agent was already running for the cover of a stalled car when Zoltán closed the distance in three steps, faster than the gap between them should have allowed.

"Good try," Zoltán said, and the rest of the sentence never made it out, because the agent was no longer in any condition to hear it.

---

The last agent still standing didn't fall back. He went straight at Zoltán with something charged in both hands — the kind of decision that only makes sense once you've already accepted you're going to get hurt and all that matters is making the hit count.

Zoltán let him come.

He read the angle of the attack an instant before it landed — the agent feinted at the torso, the kind of feint that at that range should have worked against anyone. Zoltán didn't move to block the torso. He stayed exactly where he was, and when the real strike came in low, toward the ribs, he took it head-on.

He didn't dodge it. He didn't block it. He let it in, and the impact seemed to cost him less than breathing.

The agent still had his fist against Zoltán's ribs when Zoltán's hand had already closed around his throat.

"Well fought," Zoltán said, no irony this time, almost something like respect. "Better than the other four."

He let go before Park could decide whether that had been a death threat or not. The agent dropped, conscious, breathing, but unable to get up — something in his legs no longer fully responding, as if the contact had disconnected more than his breath.

Six. None dead. None unhurt.

The bridge itself was no longer the one Park had seen from three hundred meters. The asphalt cracked at the center, an entire panel of the iron railing torn loose and bent toward the river, two abandoned cars with their windows blown out by the shockwave of the first discharge. Something in the deck structure, near the north tower, gave off a low, constant groan that hadn't been there half an hour earlier — the kind of sound an engineer would spend weeks explaining without ever using the right word for it.

---

Katalin was already running toward the bridge when Park caught her by the arm.

"Wait," he said. "Look at him. It's not just speed. He's one step from a level neither of us has seen up close."

"He's my cousin," Katalin said, not taking her eyes off him. "And that collar is killing him while he talks. Do you see it? Every time he uses it, something in his face leaves for a full second before it comes back."

Park had seen it. He'd registered it without quite naming it — the same brief blankness Zoltán showed every time, as if for an instant it wasn't him winning the fight.

Park gestured at the whole bridge, the people still fleeing toward both banks, the five AMAT-HU agents on the ground. "If we go in now, alone, with this, there's no backup behind us. Ilona already used the best she had."

"If we don't go in," Katalin said, "he stays there, in the middle of a bridge full of people, with something that's already proven it can take down six trained agents in under a minute."

Park didn't have a clean answer for that. Neither of them did.

"If he goes down wearing that," Park said, slower, "what happens to you?"

Katalin took a moment to answer.

"I'm next," she said. "You already know that. What I don't know is whether the succession works the same way if the previous Guardian doesn't die as a Guardian, but as whatever this is he's turning into right now, in front of us."

Park thought of Ji-ho, of the one time he'd watched someone dispatch a threat of impossible scale without it seeming to cost him anything — and how that ease had never been free, only invisible to whoever was watching from outside. What he had in front of him now wasn't that. Zoltán wasn't winning for free. Every time the Shackle pulled on him, something sank a little further in than it had been a minute before.

That didn't make him less dangerous. If anything, it made him more urgent.

A hundred meters away, over the cracked asphalt and riveted iron towers of the bridge, Zoltán turned toward them for the first time since they'd gotten out of the car — as if he'd been sensing them there since before they'd spoken, and had only been waiting for them to finish.

"Katalin," he said, his voice carrying far enough over the traffic-empty bridge for both of them to hear clearly. "About time someone from the family came to see this in person, instead of just reading it in a file."

"I didn't come to see anything, Zoltán," she answered, not moving a step closer. "I came to decide what to do with you."

"You already decided that four years ago," he said, "the first time you started tracking me without telling anyone back home. You just didn't know yet you'd have to do it here, in front of people who don't understand any of what they're seeing."

He said nothing about Park. He didn't need to. The Korean agent was already there, and by the way Zoltán's shoulders dropped half a centimeter — not fatigue, something closer to anticipation — Park understood he'd been waiting for this part of the night since long before he'd known Park existed.

Behind Zoltán, the river kept running under the bridge, indifferent to the six bodies laid out on the asphalt and the groan that still hadn't quieted in the north tower. Park thought, unable to help it, that this indifference was exactly what Katalin had described to him the night before at the family property — the Ruler didn't hate anyone. It simply no longer fit where it had been kept for two centuries, and that night, on that bridge, someone had finally thrown the door wide open.
