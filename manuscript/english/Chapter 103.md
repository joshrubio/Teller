# Chapter 103 — Don Martín

---

The mist never fully lifted, not even by mid-afternoon. It stayed tangled in the coffee trees on the lower slope, and from the back room window Kaspar could watch the hillside vanish halfway up, its summit swallowed by something that was neither cloud nor smoke. The clinic had no name on any door, only a hand-painted sign — "Consultations Tuesdays and Thursdays" — hung with wire over the frame of a house that had belonged to someone else before Kaspar paid, in cash and with no questions asked, for it to stop belonging to them. Waiting for the line outside to finish moving, he counted the same faces as always: Señora Herminia with the swollen foot no hospital three hours down the road would look at inside a week, two kids with the seasonal cough.

"Twelve today," said Marlene, the town's nurse, the only one with real training, the one Kaspar paid too without anyone asking where the money came from. "One new. Says he's from further up, from the hillside."

"Bad?"

"Not physically."

Kaspar understood what that meant without needing more. He went out to the back patio, where Sisi sat on a bare log, watching the line work from a respectful distance, the straw hat someone had given her the week before still without a right angle on her head.

---

Crossing the square, at that hour, took Kaspar three times as long as it should have. Doña Carmen stopped him to say the new roof had held through the last storm — he'd paid for it himself, without anyone asking twice, after the old one gave way with her youngest still inside; a woman pressed a bag of green mangoes into his hands, unasked, that Kaspar knew he couldn't refuse without offending her more than the fruit was worth.

"Don Martín," most of them called him, a name he'd been using there longer than he'd used any other in forty years. "That new generator is thanks to you, you know? Power used to go out every time it rained hard on the mountain."

Kaspar smiled, answered with just enough modesty not to look like he was fishing for the compliment, and kept walking. None of what they said was a lie. The generator existed. Doña Carmen's roof had held through the storm. The kids who used to get sick every rainy season now showed up at the clinic with the ordinary cough, not the other one — the one that used to take one or two of them every year without anyone outside the town ever finding out.

The whole square loved him. That, more than any deal with whoever happened to control the road that week, was what kept the place running exactly the way Kaspar needed it to run.

---

"What's that?" Sisi asked, pointing at the hillside, where something flickered through the barely visible mist, scattered points of light too still to be real fire.

"Candles," Kaspar said. "Someone goes up every night to leave them. Has for longer than anyone in town remembers."

"For who?"

"That's a story for another day," Kaspar said, and it wasn't entirely a dodge — it just wasn't the day.

"Why here?" Sisi asked, not looking up from the ground, when Kaspar sat down beside her. "Out of every place you could hide, why a town the mist swallows whole every afternoon?"

"Because nobody else comes here to help," Kaspar said. "That makes it easy to be useful."

"That's not a reason. That's an advantage."

Kaspar didn't deny it. He never did with her; he'd learned fast that lying to Sisi wasted time instead of avoiding a question — she found the crack anyway, just later, and more resentful for the delay.

"Both things can be true at once," he said. "I'm useful. And it suits me to be."

"And the one who came down from the hillside?" Sisi tipped her chin toward the house, toward the man Marlene had described as "not physically" bad off. "Does that suit you too?"

"Depends what happened to him."

"You don't know."

"I'll know in ten minutes," Kaspar said. "And then I'll decide if it suits me."

Sisi went quiet a moment, chewing on that. It wasn't the first time Kaspar had noticed what she did with answers she didn't fully like: she didn't reject them on the spot. She kept them, and brought them back out later, reworded, sharper.

---

The man was named Wilmer, and he didn't want anyone else to hear him. Kaspar saw him alone, in the back room, door shut, Marlene tending the line as if nothing were happening.

He wasn't sick. He was terrified, with the kind of fear that no longer shows on a face because it's been there too long. He herded goats up on the high slope, closer to the mountain than anyone else in town, and he'd gone three weeks without sleeping well — not because of anything he'd seen, but because of what he felt crawl up his skin every time the wind shifted and carried something down from above. A weight, he said, for lack of a better word. Two of his goats had stopped eating. One had vanished entirely, no trace, as if the mountain had swallowed it without chewing.

"Others go up too," Wilmer said, not meeting his eyes. "To leave the candles. Not me anymore. Not since I started feeling it."

Kaspar neither confirmed nor denied knowing what he meant. What he did was ask for dates, an exact point on the mental map Wilmer carried of the mountain, how long the weight had been growing. And at the end, before letting him go, he put a hand on his shoulder and told him the only thing he could actually promise:

"Don't go up again. Neither you nor whatever goats you've got left. I'll go look."

Wilmer left with something like relief, though not entirely clean.

---

"Are you going up yourself?" Sisi asked, who'd been listening from the half-open door without Kaspar bothering to close it all the way.

"Tonight."

"Do you know what it is?"

Kaspar didn't answer right away. When he did, he didn't soften any of it.

"I have an idea," he said. "Not a complete one. Something's been up there longer than anyone living remembers, and something woke it up more recently than it should have."

"And if it's actually dangerous?"

"Anything worth watching is."

"That means you're going up alone, without telling me what you expect to find."

"That's exactly what it means."

"And you're okay with that?"

"No," Kaspar said, without hesitating. "I think it's necessary. Those aren't the same question, even if at twenty-something everyone thinks they are."

Sisi didn't respond to that. She kept looking at him with the same expression she wore for everything she still didn't know how to classify — not approval, not condemnation, just the work of trying to figure out how both fit inside the same person.

---

The scream came from the far end of the square in the middle of the afternoon, before Kaspar could even tell what it was. He ran, not because he knew what had happened but because any scream that size, in a town that small, was his business before anyone asked him to make it so.

A girl of about eight stood at the center of a circle of adults who didn't dare get closer, the ground around her cracked in a pattern that matched nothing physically reasonable — thin, radial fractures, as if the earth itself had wanted to pull away from where she stood — and a ceramic vase floating, unsupported, at the height of her head, turning slowly on an axis that wasn't its own.

"She fell," someone said, unable to explain it any better. "She fell and she shouldn't have gotten back up like that."

The girl wasn't crying. She stared at the vase with the same look of absolute terror she gave the adults who wouldn't come closer, as if she understood, without having the words for it, that she'd just done something that set her apart from everyone else forever.

Kaspar knelt in front of her, slowly, hands visible, his voice lower than he used for anyone else in town.

"What's your name?"

"Yesenia."

"Yesenia, look at me, not the vase. The vase is going to be fine a little longer."

It worked well enough. The spin slowed, not because Kaspar did anything — he couldn't, this wasn't his axis — but because the girl's fear, redirected for a moment toward his face instead of the object, loosened whatever was holding it in the air. The vase fell and shattered against the cracked ground, and this time nobody flinched, because breaking was normal, what a vase was supposed to do.

He spoke with the parents that same night, in the kitchen of the house, Yesenia already asleep in the next room — or more likely pretending to sleep, door left ajar. He explained what needed explaining and nothing more: that there were people like this in the world, that it wasn't a curse or a sickness, that he knew ways to help her learn to live with it without it scaring her every time. He didn't mention AMAT. He didn't mention Geneva, or protocols, or what happened on other continents when a case like this reached the right files. What he offered was simpler and harder to turn down: he would take care of it. Nobody else needed to know. The girl would stay theirs.

The parents, terrified and grateful in equal measure, said yes before Kaspar finished the sentence.

Walking back to the clinic alone, the square already empty, the mist finally lifting a little with the night breeze, Kaspar thought of another kitchen, another half-open door, forty years back, a whole continent away — and of the young man who hadn't known what to do with what he was seeing then, and who'd taken her anyway, convinced he was doing the right thing. Yesenia wasn't going to AMAT. Yesenia was going to stay exactly where she was, with the people who loved her, learning to live with what she had without anyone looking at her like a file.

That, he told himself, was the difference. He didn't let himself ask, that night, whether it was a real difference or just the one he needed to believe in order to keep sleeping.

---

He went up alone, after midnight, once even the town's dogs had stopped barking.

The mist wasn't the same up there. Down below it stayed loose; on the high slope it closed like a room with no windows, and Kaspar walked twenty minutes guided by the one thing no map could have given him: the weight. He felt it before he saw anything — not on his skin, deeper, an even pressure that came from no exact point, as if the air on the hillside had stopped having a direction. Nothing he could turn to face. Just there, all around, thick.

The candles appeared first as smudges of heat in the mist, dozens of them, some already nearly spent, others freshly lit by hands he never saw. He kept climbing until the mist closed completely behind him, and the town stopped existing.

He came down three hours later than he'd planned, before dawn, mud to the knee and something on his face that Sisi, half-asleep in the clinic doorway, didn't ask about. He didn't talk about what he'd found. Not that night, and not the ones that followed.

The only thing he said, passing her on his way to the back room, was a sentence Sisi didn't fully understand and that Kaspar never repeated:

"Not yet. But soon."

---

Camilo arrived at dusk the next day, when the heat finally dropped enough for people to come back out to their patios. He was twenty-three, with big hands from working the land since he was a boy, and a Domain Kaspar had found eight months earlier that no AMAT office on the continent knew existed: with his eyes closed, he could sense anyone entering a radius around the town who shouldn't be there — not who they were, only that they didn't fit, a gap in the pattern he'd felt his whole life without a word for it until Kaspar gave him one.

"Nothing tonight," Camilo said, sitting on the same log Sisi had used earlier. "Two people crossed the old path an hour ago. Not from around here. Headed north, didn't stay."

"Armed?"

"I can't tell that. I just know they didn't fit."

Kaspar nodded, filing the detail away without further comment. Camilo never asked what Kaspar did with that kind of information, and Kaspar never explained — a silent understanding between the two of them, different from the one he had with Sisi, who asked about everything.

"Yesenia's going to end up like Camilo," Sisi said, later, once Camilo had already left, though Kaspar hadn't mentioned the girl again all afternoon. "In a few years. You're going to teach her, and she's never going to know there's another way this could end."

"There are worse ways."

"I didn't say this was the worst one. I said it's one you're choosing for her, without anyone else in that house knowing the other one even exists."

"Is that how it is with everyone you recruit?" she went on, not waiting for him to answer the last part. "You teach them to use it and that's it? Nobody asks what for?"

"Camilo asks less than you do."

"That's not an answer to what I asked."

Kaspar allowed himself something like a smile, brief, no real joy behind it.

"No," he said. "It's not the same with everyone. I make an exception with you. With most of them, the question that matters isn't 'what for' — it's 'finally, someone's actually looking at me.' That's already more than AMAT ever gave them."

"That sounds like you're taking advantage of the fact that they're alone."

"Yes," Kaspar said, with no hesitation at all. "I am. Since you ask everything, here's the question you should be asking yourself: does that make me any worse than the institution that left them alone in the first place?"

Sisi had no ready answer for that. Kaspar took it, silently, as a small victory not worth celebrating out loud.

---

That night, while the town slept and the nameless clinic went dark, Kaspar stayed up longer than usual. From the back room window he could see the empty square, doña Carmen's roof cut out against the moon whenever the mist allowed it, the mountain reduced to a black silhouette except for the points of light still burning on the high slope, stubborn, with no one to put them out. A whole town sleeping soundly because he'd decided it should — and, now, something further up, sleeping or not sleeping, waiting for something Kaspar still didn't know if he himself was part of.

He turned Camilo's report over without being able to let it go. Two people on the old path, headed north, not staying. Didn't fit. It could be anything — just another smuggler skirting the road. But he'd spent forty years learning to recognize the exact texture of someone approaching with too much care to be coincidence, and that night, for the first time in months, he felt the quiet certainty, no panic in it, that someone — not the dead goats on the mountain, not whatever it was that had recognized him under the mist — was starting to look toward the exact place where, for so long, nobody had been looking.

He thought of Yesenia asleep, of Camilo with his ear turned toward the old path, of Sisi still awake on the other side of the wall. Forty years, and the two real cracks that had opened that week hadn't come from any road or from Geneva — they'd come from the mountain, and from a path someone else had started walking toward him.

Nobody in the square knew his real name. They'd given him one of his own, one he genuinely wanted to use, one he'd paid for with a generator and a roof and a clinic with no sign. That night, for the first time, he wondered how much longer he'd get to go on being Don Martín before someone — or something — arrived to remind him who he really was.
