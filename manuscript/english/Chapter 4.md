# Chapter 4 — What You Get Used To


He stopped counting at some point in the years that followed, and it wasn't a decision — it was simply that one day he realized he no longer knew the exact number, and that he didn't care enough to sit down and rebuild it from scratch, name by name, the way any man who still needed the figure to sleep soundly would have. Eleven had been a number he knew by heart, with a name and a face for each one, even as the faces blurred the same way the face of the man who'd handed him all this had blurred. After eleven, the numbers turned approximate. Twenty-something. Thirty-odd. A figure he lived with the same way he lived with the exact age of his shops, or the years since he'd last seen his sister: true somewhere, but no longer urgent.

Silas, though, kept counting. Farid knew it without Silas ever telling him — he saw it in the way Silas jotted something on a separate sheet, quickly, every time one of those accounts closed, and tucked the sheet somewhere Farid wouldn't stumble across it. He said nothing about it. A man has a right to his own accounts, and Farid, better than anyone, understood the need to keep a record no one else could see.

Once, only once, he caught Silas watching him with an attention that had nothing to do with balancing books — eyes fixed on Farid's hands as he crossed out a name, as if expecting to see something other than the usual gesture. Farid looked up and held the stare just long enough for Silas to drop his eyes back to that month's accounts, without a word. Neither of them mentioned the moment afterward. Farid wondered, briefly, how much longer it would be before the question Silas was carrying found its way out — and decided, with the same coldness he decided everything else, that he'd deal with it when it came, not before.

---

Fenwick's rib — that was still how he thought of it, years later, though it no longer ached — had been the last time he'd truly stopped to think before crossing out a name. After that night, the decision stopped carrying the weight of a decision. It became just another step of the trade, like appraising a watch or calculating how long a customer would hold out before paying. He could cross out a name in the middle of a conversation with someone else, without his voice shaking or his mind drifting from whatever they were discussing. He did it once while on the phone with the landlord of his fourth shop, discussing a leak in the roof. He hung up, went on with his day. That night he couldn't recall the name he'd crossed out, or the figure. He had to check the notebook to find out, and when he did, he felt the same calm nothing he felt confirming that a cheap watch was, in fact, cheap.

That was normalization, he told himself, without using the word: not losing track of what he was doing, but losing the need to feel anything while he did it.

There was a time, at his own kitchen table, a plate of rice still steaming in front of him, when he crossed out a name between two bites because he suddenly remembered he'd owed it to himself for weeks. He didn't get up from the table. He didn't stop eating. The name belonged to a man who owed him almost a thousand dollars and who'd been gone six weeks with no sign of him anywhere known — a figure that, years before, would have cost him two weeks of insomnia and a rib. That night it cost him, at most, a one-second hitch between bites, and he wasn't even fully sure of that; afterward he couldn't recall whether he'd really stopped or only imagined it later, thinking back.

There were others, of course, beyond the ones he no longer bothered counting precisely. An electrician who owed him four hundred fifty dollars and one day stopped slipping invoices through the mail slot. A young couple who'd borrowed money for a wedding that never ended up happening, of which only one name survived in the notebook — his, since she'd never signed anything — crossed out without Farid remembering afterward which of the two it had actually been. Names that once would have cost him a sleepless night now barely earned a line. He didn't remember them individually. If anything, he remembered them the way he remembered the years in general: as a texture, not a list.

Rosa Anselmi moved out of Breakwater a few years later — Farid learned it from a customer who mentioned, in passing, that the flat above the old laundry had new tenants. He stopped thinking about her as often as before. When he did, he no longer felt the knot in his stomach from the early days. He felt, at most, the same distant curiosity he felt for a watch he'd pawned years back and never saw again: where had it ended up? — without the question costing him a single night's sleep.

---

Eight shops became ten. By then Farid had more gray hair than he liked to admit, and a reputation in Breakwater that no longer needed explaining: Mr. Yamal, who lent hard but never cheated, who never raised his voice, who settled whatever needed settling without anyone having to ask how. People spoke of him with the same reluctant respect they gave the winter weather — something better not to have against you, and better not to gamble with. Dennis, his first employee, had his own shop in a different neighborhood by now, and greeted him on the street with the uneasy respect of a man who'd learned the trade from someone who turned out to be more than he taught.

He still lived alone, above the first shop — he'd never tried again with anyone since the flower seller, and never felt the lack urgently enough to try. By then he had the kind of life a stranger might envy without quite knowing why: ten businesses, no boss, no debts of his own. If he bothered to think about it, there was very little missing that he could name precisely — and that, more than anything else, was what unsettled him most on the few nights he did bother to think about it.

It was at the original shop, the one on Dunmore, that a man of about thirty walked in one Tuesday afternoon, his hands stained with something soap couldn't quite lift, a shirt that had seen better days, and a folder with its papers creased soft at the corners from being carried from place to place too many times. He was tall, broader through the shoulders than the shirt let on, with the kind of face that ages all at once somewhere between twenty-five and thirty-five, and not before or after.

"I need a loan to open a business," he said, no preamble, like a man who has rehearsed the line too many times for it to still sound natural. "Appliance repair. Radios, irons, whatever people can't afford to throw out and buy new."

Farid looked him over with the same old reflex — age, clothes, quality of the fabric, how much of a hurry he was in — and found a man in no hurry at all, only accumulated tiredness and a quiet kind of stubbornness Farid recognized, without being able to say from where, as the same stubbornness he'd once used to appraise his first watch.

"Name?"

"Gary Webb."

"And why repair, Mr. Webb? There are easier trades."

Gary shrugged, almost embarrassed by the answer before he gave it.

"Because I'm good at it. My whole life people have brought me broken things — family, neighbors — and I've gotten them running again." He laughed at himself, a little embarrassed by his own enthusiasm. "I've never thrown out anything I thought could be fixed. Now I've got a wife, and a kid on the way, and fixing the neighbor's toaster for a slice of pie doesn't cut it anymore."

He already had his eye on a small storefront in Shelter 9, the ground floor empty since the notions shop before it closed, two blocks from where he lived. He talked about it with more enthusiasm than he'd shown until then — a hand-painted sign, a secondhand counter he'd already lined up, the vague idea of teaching the kid the trade someday, if the kid turned out to have the hands for it.

It wasn't the best reason Farid had heard for borrowing money, nor the worst. It was, above all, honest, and honesty, after all these years in the trade, was still the rarest thing to cross his counter.

"How much do you need?"

"Three thousand five hundred. Tools, rent on a small space, signage. I can pay it back in two years, with whatever interest is reasonable."

"And what do you consider reasonable?" Farid asked, not because he cared about the answer, but because the way a man answered that question told him more than anything else he could ask.

Gary considered it a moment, uncomfortable, as if he knew any number he named would sound either naive or insulting. "Something I can actually pay, so the neighbor's toaster doesn't end up costing me the business before it even starts."

It wasn't the answer most people gave — most named a figure and defended it with more pride than the situation warranted. Gary had answered with a condition, not a number, and that, more than anything said until then, was what made Farid think the man might not be foolish with money at all, only desperate to stop being close to needing it.

He couldn't help being reminded of the kind of customer his father used to wave through at prices lower than what he posted at the Dunmore stall — not out of weakness, but because a man who understands the real value of things is worth more, in the long run, than one who only knows how to haggle. His father would have recognized Gary Webb in two sentences. Farid needed only one.

They settled on a figure right there, without much haggling. Farid had already decided it before Gary even finished explaining what the money was for — a little more than reasonable, a little less than predatory, the exact point years of reading faces had taught him to find without effort. He felt nothing special calculating it. Gary Webb was, to his eyes, no different from the hundreds of men who'd walked through that door with an idea and empty hands — one more loan, one more name for a notebook that already held too many for a single one to stand out.

"Come back Thursday," Farid said. "I'll have the papers."

Gary nodded, thanked him with more warmth than Farid expected, and left with the same restrained hurry he'd come in with — a man who'd spent weeks holding that hurry back on purpose, so as not to scare off the one person who could grant it.

Farid wrote the name in the notebook that same afternoon, in the section for ordinary loans, with no mark to set it apart from any other. Gary Webb. Three thousand five hundred dollars. Date. Nothing in the handwriting, nothing in the gesture, gave away that this line was any different from any other he'd written that week.

On Thursday, Gary came back with the same folder, a lease agreement now folded in with the old papers. They signed standing at the counter — Farid never offered a seat for business he expected to close quickly. Gary read every line before signing, something most people didn't bother with, too eager to walk out with the money before Farid changed his mind, and asked twice about a clause on late payments, which Farid explained without impatience, with the exact same patience he'd once reserved only for customers he genuinely cared about keeping.

"Thank you, Mr. Yamal," Gary said when it was done, tucking away his copy with the care of a man who hasn't had many important papers to keep in his life. "You don't know what this means."

Farid did know, in general terms — knew it by heart, from hearing it with minor variations hundreds of times. He did nothing to correct the idea Gary had of the conversation, the one about two men closing a deal in good faith between equals. He let him leave believing it. It cost less than explaining what it actually was: a calculated bet, dressed up as trust, that only one of the two men understood completely.

He had no way of knowing — no one could have told him, not even the man who'd sold him the tool so he'd never have to know anything more — that this name would outlast every other name in his notebook across more than two decades of the trade. That the kid on the way would, someday, keep the books at that very counter, in handwriting he hadn't yet learned to form. That Gary Webb's debt, unlike so many others, would never end up crossed out — it would end, instead, paid off in a way neither Farid nor Gary himself could have imagined that Tuesday afternoon, hands stained with grease that soap couldn't quite lift.

A month later, when Gary came back to pay the first installment, right on time, hands stained again with something soap couldn't quite lift, Farid had to look at the name in the book a second before he placed the face.

It was, in every sense that mattered to Farid, the most ordinary loan he'd made in years — no drama, no suspicion, nothing to set it apart from the hundreds before it that he no longer bothered remembering one by one. And it was exactly that nothing, that complete absence of anything memorable, which, two decades later, would end up being the only thing Oliver Webb could ever tell anyone about how it all started: that his father took out a loan on an ordinary Tuesday, got it without trouble, and no one in the family thought about the name Yamal again until it was already too late for thinking about it to do any good.
