# Chapter 86 — What a Domain Doesn't Fix

---

This time, the pit wasn't for watching from a gallery.

Reber gathered them at the very edge of the concrete, no glass railing between them and the drop, and read off the pairings from his tablet without ceremony, mixing groups in a way that left no room for negotiation. Domain against Domain. Domain against nothing. Nothing against nothing. The order looked random until Oliver understood, halfway down the list, that it wasn't — Reber was pairing, with deliberate care, exactly the matchups that were going to hurt the most.

"Riku Hayashi," Reber said, "against Iker Zabala."

Riku went pale in a way that had nothing to do with the day before's exhaustion.

"He's number two," Dara said quietly, looking at the same tablet Reber had just put away. "I saw it on the Board yesterday. Right behind Dax."

"Thanks," Riku said, without turning around. "Exactly what I needed to hear right now."

---

Oliver didn't have a turn yet, so he stayed at the edge with the rest, watching.

Riku and Iker took opposite ends of the pit, and for the first few seconds nothing visible happened at all — both had the same electromagnetic Domain, both stood with their hands quiet at their sides. Oliver, who'd been able to see Riku's field in real time for months now, saw what the rest couldn't: Riku's familiar contour extending first, the radio interference he already knew by heart, field nodes opening around him like concentric ripples on still water. Riku was in EM Reading — stage three, the one he could only sustain under supervision — trying to sense Iker's bioelectric pulse before anything else happened.

Iker let him. He waited, activating nothing of his own, letting Riku extend the whole field, and only then — when Riku's reading was at its most open, most vulnerable, most *connected* to whatever he was trying to sense — did something Oliver had no vocabulary of his own for: he didn't generate a new field. He took the one already in the air — Riku's, still extended — and folded it back on itself, the way you'd grab the end of a rope someone else is holding and yank it the other way.

Riku's field came back at Riku.

His breath cut off mid-inhale, his left hand — the origin hand, the asymmetric one — closing on its own in a spasm he hadn't asked for, fingers rigid around nothing. It wasn't a blow. It was his own Static, amplified and returned, finding the one body in the pit already tuned to receive it.

"That's Redirection," Reber said from the edge, not addressing anyone in particular — the kind of technical comment he made only when something deserved to be said out loud. "Stage four. Not yet taught to anyone at this facility."

Riku dropped to his knees, left hand still shaking, and didn't get up before Iker closed the distance and finished the exchange with a control so precise that Oliver, watching the Registry almost without meaning to, found nothing irregular to read, no crack in the execution. Only the same efficient stillness Iker had used for everything else.

Riku got up off the floor of the pit alone, refusing the hand someone offered him, his left hand still not closing quite right, and said nothing on the way back to the edge. Iker caught up with him halfway, unhurried, and said something Oliver, from where he stood, caught in full.

"Your problem isn't the Domain," Iker said, without raising his voice, each word placed with the same precision he'd just used in the field. "It's that you've spent years letting other people decide how far you can go, until you stopped even asking yourself the question. Nobody ever asked it for me. That's why I'm here and you're still stuck on stage three."

Riku didn't answer. He stood looking at him a second longer, something crossing his face that wasn't quite resentment, closer to a real wound — the kind that doesn't show from outside but leaves a mark anyway — and kept walking toward the edge without a word.

Oliver didn't say anything either when Riku reached him. No need to ask what he'd just felt — seeing, for the first time, not the ceiling AMAT had set for him, but the real ceiling of what his own Domain could become, and the exact distance between the two.

---

Petra went next, matched against a nameless candidate from Johanna's group, an older man with a trained calm that gave nothing away. The exchange turned ugly fast — he backed her against the edge of the pit, denying her a clean line of sight on anything close enough for a useful Swap, exactly the kind of situation where activating it without thinking would have been the easy way out.

Petra didn't activate it.

There was a moment, halfway through the exchange, when the choice became visible from outside — a metal candelabrum less than two meters away, mass comparable to the man crowding her, exactly the kind of object Petra would have used without a second thought just weeks earlier. Oliver watched her look at it. Watched her decide against it.

She fought with her hands, with the little hand-to-hand she'd picked up over the weeks, taking more hits than she landed, and lost the exchange clean, no trick, no shortcut. When she got up, lip split, breath ragged, Oliver saw her eyes search, for a second, toward where he stood — not asking for anything, just checking whether anyone else had understood what she'd just chosen not to do.

He understood. He thought, without meaning to, of the unfinished conversation two nights earlier, of what Petra had said about something unsettled between them — and wondered if this, in some crooked way, was her way of starting to pay it forward, without anyone having asked her to.

---

Nadia watched from a bench off to the side, arm still in a sling, excused from combat by medical order that nobody argued with this time. When Dara sat down beside her between matches, Nadia spoke without being asked, her eyes fixed on the pit.

"My father keeps files like this," she said quietly. "Not combat. Archives. But I've seen enough of his folders on the kitchen table to know what a record looks like when someone's building it chapter by chapter, without noticing." She fell quiet a moment. "I don't know why I thought of that just now."

Dara didn't press her, and Nadia didn't offer anything more either. It was a single loose sentence, dropped into the noise of the yard, and the moment passed before anyone else noticed anything had happened at all.

---

Pier lost his match, against a military candidate considerably younger than him, and he did it without any of the signs Oliver would have expected from someone losing — no frustration, no rush to claw back ground, just the same steady patience absorbing blow after blow until the result was decided. When it ended, he didn't go sit down or drink water. He walked over to Dara, who'd just lost her own bout against another of Johanna's candidates, her face still red more with embarrassment than exertion, and said something Oliver didn't catch in full — only the end: *"...you don't train fear to go away. You train it to fit inside a body that keeps moving anyway."*

Dara didn't answer, but something in her shoulders dropped a couple centimeters, as if the sentence had weighed less than she'd expected.

Asha, further off, won her own match with the same calculated economy as always — nothing showy, nothing wasted, every movement exactly the size it needed to be and not a hair larger — and afterward stood by the edge, watching the rest of the group with the same steady attention she'd been giving everything for weeks now, as if she were building, without saying so, her own private file on who each of them was under pressure. Oliver wondered, not for the first time, what Asha would do with everything she'd observed so far, and whether anyone besides her would ever get to see it.

---

"Oliver Webb," Reber said, "against Dax Achterberg."

The surname, said out loud like that, formal, sounded strange even inside Oliver's own head — nobody at St. Gotthard ever used it, and no one needed to when everybody already knew exactly who they meant. Dax's name, by contrast, sounded exactly the way it had sounded since day one: a single word, needing nothing else.

They took opposite ends of the pit. Dax said nothing at first, no gesture of greeting or contempt, only that same near-animal stillness Oliver knew from a distance — now close enough to cross in three steps.

Around them, the rest of the group had gathered at the edge of the pit without anyone giving the order — Riku still wearing the distant look of his own match, Petra with a swollen lip, Nadia leaning forward from her bench despite the sling, as if the extra distance would help her see better. Nobody said anything. They'd spent weeks waiting for this matchup without saying so out loud, and now that it had finally arrived, none of them seemed to know what to do with their hands.

"Begin," Reber said.

Oliver didn't open with anything yet. He circled the edge of the pit, keeping his distance, letting Dax take the first step — not out of fear, but because he needed a data point before he spent anything: how Dax's body reacted before his head finished deciding.

Dax closed the gap with a short feint, barely anything, just a shift of weight. It was enough. Oliver saw the tell — Instinct, the mode that reacted before the mind finished processing a threat, the same near-animal stillness he'd seen from a distance in the pit that first night, legible up close now — and confirmed what he'd already suspected: Dax didn't anticipate specific blows. He reacted to intent, to the charge of Odem that preceded an attack, not to the attack itself.

So Oliver fed him two false charges before releasing anything real.

He faked the start of an Impulse once, then again, letting Dax burn half a step dodging something that never fully existed — not much, but something, a minimal wear accumulating exactly where Dax least expected to find it. On the third, once the pattern was planted, Oliver released the real Impulse — Nadia's copy, a straight line, no curve — aimed not at the center of the chest but off to the side, toward where the earlier feint had left Dax's weight least prepared to correct.

It nearly connected. Dax got clear anyway, later than he had the first time, the margin shrunk down to something no longer comfortable.

"Well thought out," Dax said, without breath to spare for more, and closed the distance completely.

Oliver had no time for a third feint. What he did was trust the second data point he already had: Anchor, not thrown at random but calculated to the millimeter on the planted foot Dax had just set for his next blow — the one instant in the entire fight his weight was going to hold still long enough. He felt it activate under his skin, that heavy, absolute stillness Sabine carried in her body like a second nature, and this time it wasn't luck. It was real aim.

It worked better than the first time he'd ever used it. Dax froze in place, the blow he'd been loading interrupted halfway through, and for that instant something crossed his face that wasn't quite panic — closer to genuine surprise, the first real crack Oliver had seen in him since they'd arrived at St. Gotthard. The whole pit seemed to go as still as he was.

It didn't last. Something changed in the air around Dax — the tell of a mode switch, a brief heat Oliver already recognized from the fight against Johanna — and brute Force, costly, unsustainable past a few seconds, tore the entire anchor loose in one wrench that shook the concrete under both of them. Dax gasped once, the true cost of that mode billing him immediately, but he was free.

Oliver had no third Domain to throw with the same confidence. Strength/Momentum was off the table, too dangerous without Park there to guide it; Tension needed prep time a live fight wasn't going to give him. So he stopped hunting for a third shortcut and held on with the only thing he had left: Mantle, clenched tight around the ribs where he knew the next hit was coming, and something close to Focus, concentrated in the forearm he raised to block instead of dodge.

He held out longer than he should have. Blocked two clean hits, returned one of his own that actually connected — enough to send Dax back half a step, enough for the instructor nearest the edge of the pit to look up from his tablet — and for a few seconds, Oliver almost let himself believe a draw was possible.

It was a miscalculation, not a lack of effort, that lost it for him: he misjudged the distance on the next exchange, a centimeter too far on the block, and Dax took it without hesitating a single instant — Speed, the short burst he hadn't shown once in the whole fight, closing the gap Oliver had just left before his body could finish correcting the mistake.

"I've spent weeks watching you read everyone except me," Dax said, very close now, his voice low, almost kind. "This wasn't what I expected."

"What did you expect?" Oliver managed, without breath to spare for anything more.

"Less," Dax said.

The blow that followed wasn't spectacular. It was precise, short, aimed at an exact point under the jaw that Oliver never fully saw coming, and the whole pit went dark on him all at once, no transition, none of the warning second the movies promised and reality never gave.

---

He came to with the metallic taste of his own blood in his mouth and Maren's face too close to his, checking something with a small light that hurt to look at directly.

"Still here," she said, without drama, the tone of someone who's run this check many times before. "Pupils even. You're going to have a brutal headache for a couple of days, and nothing worse than that."

Dax was already gone from the pit. Someone told him later that Dax had walked off on his own the moment the fight ended, without waiting to see whether Oliver got up on his own.

---

The hangar screens, that afternoon, still showed Dax at number one — right where he'd been since the assembly, the kind of position a single fight, won or lost, was never going to move. The gap between them, on paper, was still wide enough that anyone glancing at the Board alone would have assumed, without needing to ask, that the fight would end fast and leave no story worth telling.

It hadn't ended fast. Nobody who'd been at the pit, who'd watched Dax gasp after an anchor that shouldn't have cost him that much to break, would have called it an easy result — even if the result, in the end, had been exactly what the Board predicted.

His own number, though, hadn't stayed exactly where it was. #112 to #110 — two spots, nothing that was going to change anybody's life, but there, visible: the first time the Board had climbed after a loss instead of dropping. Losing well, Oliver understood, looking at the figure, counted for something different than winning, even if nobody had ever explained it to him in those terms.

Oliver stood looking at the screen a moment longer than necessary, his jaw still swollen under his fingers every time he touched it without meaning to, thinking that a number accumulated over weeks was never going to predict what happened in the exact second two people actually collided — not even when, in the end, it still got right who stayed standing. The Board measured history. It didn't measure the present moment, and it measured the one that hadn't come yet even less.

He thought, too, of Riku, of the look on his face seeing the real ceiling of his own Domain for the first time. Of Petra, looking at a candelabrum and deciding against it. Of Nadia, dropping a sentence about files that had nothing to do with the day's exercise, and how nobody — not even her — seemed to know yet what to do with it once it was out loud. One single day, and every one of them had run into something no Domain, their own or copied, had been able to fix.

That, he thought, was probably the one thing a Domain — or the lack of one working — was never going to be able to fix.
