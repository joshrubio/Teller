# Chapter 115 — Final Round — Part 1

---

They'd been chasing a group of three across half of Milan since mid-afternoon, and Johanna was starting to suspect the chase was going to cost them more than the badges still up for grabs were worth.

Two had already fallen, one after the other, on two different streets barely a hundred meters apart — the first trapped in a genuine dead end, the second too worn out to keep running after nearly an hour of steadily losing his lead. The third was still loose, and by far the fastest of the three, cutting corners none of Johanna's group knew as well as he did.

"I've got him," a voice said over the comm, broken up by the running itself. "Almost. Not losing sight of him."

Johanna didn't answer right away, saving her breath for her own legs. She ran the math as she ran, the same math she'd been running every hour since dawn: thirty-one badges among the five of them, a number that should have been enough days ago and that, with the board's brutal drop in the last few hours, no longer felt as certain. Two of her group were still outside the top twenty-five, by a margin that could still close or widen in either direction.

The comm crackled again, no words this time — only the breathing of someone who's stopped running because there's no need anymore.

"Four," the voice said, finally, no breath to spare for anything more. "Four badges, ours. The third one isn't getting up for a while."

It was then, rounding into a plaza Johanna didn't quite recognize either, that the sky changed to the north of the city — a column of white light shooting up between the buildings, too intense to be anything normal at that hour.

Johanna stopped dead, the rest of her group doing the same a second later.

"That's not a building with its lights on," someone said.

It wasn't. Johanna knew it with the same certainty she read anything else she'd spent weeks learning to read: someone had just lit a beacon, and every last person still standing in the city was going to see it just as well as they could.

---

Asha counted heads twice before accepting the number.

Three. Of the five who'd gone out that afternoon to check a rumor about a large group hiding near Porta Romana, only three had made it to this alley — the rumor had turned out to be true, but the large group had turned out bigger and faster than anyone had calculated, and the retreat had come apart along the way, each of them taking whatever exit was closest the moment it became clear staying to fight wasn't worth the risk.

"Riku and Dara still aren't answering," Nadia said, the comm pressed to her ear, her voice tighter than she wanted to let on.

"They'll be fine," Pier said, without the same certainty he usually put into that kind of sentence. "They know how to take care of themselves. We've been training for this all week without meaning to."

Twenty-four badges, split now three ways instead of five, didn't mean the same thing as twenty-four badges split five ways. Asha knew it without needing to say it out loud, and from the other two's faces, so did they.

It was Nadia who saw the light first, a white glow growing over the rooftops to the north, too uniform and too intense to be a fire or nighttime construction work.

"That's a signal," she said, lowering the comm slowly. "Someone wants it seen."

No one argued with that. The three of them stood a moment watching the light, each running their own version of the same impossible math, before Asha took the first step toward it.

---

It took Oliver the whole walk to the stadium to feel like his legs were fully his own again.

He arrived along the outer perimeter, his body still charging him for the previous night's fall with every stride, and stopped atop a retaining wall from which the whole field lay in view, lit up completely under four rings of floodlights.

Empty. Or nearly.

At the exact center, alone, the way he'd been all week, Dax stood waiting, no hurry at all, looking up at the high stands as if he already knew exactly what was coming through them and only needed to be patient.

Oliver ran his own math as he watched, the only math that actually mattered to him now: how many people were still standing across the whole city, how many of them were going to converge here tonight, and how much of what he himself had gathered all week was about to be decided in the next few hours, not by any calculation made ahead of time. This wasn't an ambush he could avoid, or a target he could choose not to chase. This was the end, arriving from every direction at once, and the only thing left to decide was where to step into it from.

---

They arrived scattered, trickling in, each one convinced they'd been first to see the signal.

A lone kid, limping, came through one of the entry tunnels and stopped dead at the sight of Dax in the center, calculating in real time whether it was worth pushing forward or pulling back while he still could. Two more, a pair Oliver didn't recognize, stuck to the shadow of the low stands, watching without deciding.

Then the groups arrived.

Johanna's came first, five figures moving with the same tired discipline as all week, taking the east side of the field with no words needed between them. Thirty-eight were left that night across the whole city, according to Oliver's own device, and Johanna was still number three on that list — inside the podium, despite everything the week had cost her.

Valais arrived in pieces, not as one block.

Asha came in first, from the opposite side, Pier and Nadia a step behind her — three of them together, but only three. Oliver had had all three located on the device for days, no need to check twice: Asha twenty-seven, Pier twenty-nine, Nadia thirty-six. All three outside the cut, and the gap showed in how they moved, closer together than usual.

Riku arrived on his own minutes later, through a completely different tunnel, and something in Nadia's shoulders eased a degree the moment she saw him cross the field toward them. Thirty-three, Oliver remembered from the board — also not in, despite everything they'd learned.

Dara was last of the five, entering almost where Yegor had, so close that for an instant it looked like they'd arrived together before she broke off toward the rest of her group. Twenty-two. The only one of the five still inside the cut, and by a margin that didn't invite confidence.

Five, finally complete, four of them outside the twenty-five that mattered, though none of them looked capable yet of fully relaxing their shoulders over it.

A third group came in through the north stand, bigger than the other two, led by a stocky man Oliver recognized from the fight club without recalling the full name — Lukas, he remembered a moment later, sixth on the board, the one who'd been at ten before the week started unraveling every number that had mattered up to then.

And last, when it already seemed the field couldn't fill any further, came Yegor — alone at first, until Oliver understood he wasn't entirely alone. Behind him, a handful of shapes moving with the discomfort of people who'd rather be anywhere else. Thirty-one, still, despite everything he'd spent the whole week doing to change that number.

Four groups, one man alone at the center, and a handful of loose, sideless candidates scattered along the edges. Oliver reminded himself where he stood on that same list — second, behind only Dax — and understood that the number, tonight, didn't matter half as much as the fact of being here, still standing, to defend it.

---

The number changed in front of everyone before anyone took the first step.

Thirty-eight dropped to thirty-seven, somewhere in the city none of them had seen, and the change ran through every device at once — a curt reminder that the clock wasn't waiting for the stadium to decide anything. Oliver felt the whole field's mood shift with that single digit, two full minutes of nobody moving spent all at once.

It was Yegor who broke the silence first.

"Dax," he said, his voice carrying effortlessly across the whole field, his thick accent as sharp as ever. "Look at you standing there, like nothing can touch you. Let's see how much of that's true, and how much is just that nobody's dared."

Dax didn't answer. He didn't even turn his head toward where the voice was coming from.

That, more than any direct insult, was what truly set Yegor off. He crossed the field toward the center without waiting for anyone, not his own people or anyone else, rage carrying him faster than his own group could keep up with.

"Answer," he said, close now, almost shouting.

"No," Dax said, without raising his voice, the same economy as always.

The punch Yegor threw mid-shout never landed. Dax moved — not fast in the sense Oliver knew fast to mean, but in a different way, a motion his own Registry barely managed to read before it was already over — and Yegor flew backward, his whole body sailing across the grass, so fast that half the field never even saw the impact that caused it, only the result.

No one needed a clearer signal than that.

---

They all threw themselves in at once.

Dax changed modes the same second the first one reached him — Oliver, Registry open, watched the whole structure reorganize itself: his gaze narrowing to one point at a time, his stance dropping a degree, his breathing lengthening where a moment before it had been short. The Hunt. The first time Oliver had seen him actually use it, not cutting the shift off halfway the way he had in the pit weeks earlier.

The first to reach him — one of Yegor's recruits — landed a real hit, full on Dax's shoulder, and it did him no good at all. Dax absorbed the contact barely moving and answered with a short elbow to the throat that dropped him breathless to the ground, convulsing once before going fully still. The second, more careful, tried circling around behind him; Dax pivoted on his own heel without needing to fully see him coming, a straight punch to the solar plexus that folded him in half and sent him rolling across the grass.

Someone from Lukas's group — a woman with something already loaded in her fists — read the pattern before the others and feinted twice, hunting for the angle everyone else had missed. Dax didn't take the bait. He waited for the third move, the real one, and caught it halfway with an open hand, closing his fingers around her fist for an instant — just long enough for Oliver to watch, with a cold jolt, something shift in the woman's own structure, a glow dimming by a degree, draining into Dax before she even understood why her legs had stopped answering her. She dropped to her knees, conscious but emptied out, and didn't get up again.

Lukas shouted an order none of his people fully obeyed — two of his were already running at Dax on their own, waiting for no signal, the same pack instinct that had spent the whole week hunting the weak now turned on the one target that actually mattered. Johanna, on the other side of the field, shouted something similar, and it didn't work either — two of hers broke formation, pulled toward the center as if Dax himself generated his own gravity.

Dax kept dropping bodies, one after another, no two the same way — an arm bent at an angle it shouldn't bend, a scream that wasn't a word, someone who simply stopped moving mid-stride with no one catching the exact hit that stopped him. The grass, already torn up in several stretches, began marking out the entire map of the fight without anyone needing to ask what had happened where.

No one still standing was debating whether to step in anymore. The only question was how much it was going to cost.

One after another, they kept joining in, and somehow what had first looked like a series of disordered, chaotic attacks turned into something closer to a coordinated pack with a single goal: swallowing Dax whole.

That was when he saw it, in the middle of the chaos — first one, gripping Dax by an arm, then a bigger one, Lukas himself, managing to lock him in a hold from behind. Then another, and another. From where he stood, moved by something closer to empathy than reflex, watching the pack close on its prey, Oliver took a step forward. He understood too late that what he was watching wasn't, by any measure, the end of Dax.

It happened in a blink — a release of force that drove the ground beneath them down with a sound that arrived only instants before the shockwave itself. And the sound of bodies colliding against each other in midair was drowned out by the sight that followed: Dax, airborne, swinging Lukas's body like a rag doll to strike five others out of the air.

Dax's body seemed to fly as he moved from one place to another, faster than Oliver had ever seen anyone move. No longer defending — finally, at last, attacking. And something in the air changed just enough to tell Oliver that the final confrontation had, at last, begun.
