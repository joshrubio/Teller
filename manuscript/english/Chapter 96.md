# Chapter 96 — What Counts Every Month

---

The order arrived the same way it had the first time, taped to the door before breakfast: dead lift, full repetition, the whole center at once. Nadia recognized it right away, and recognized too, with a little surprise, that her stomach didn't clench the way it had the last time.

"Better this time," Riku said, without much conviction, tying his laces in the hallway. "Or I hope so. I don't want to hear that comment about calling me dead again."

Nobody laughed, but nobody argued either.

---

The east wall clearing was as crowded as Nadia remembered it — sandbags, cones, the slope waiting, the same uneasy overlap between groups that normally didn't even greet each other. Reber didn't repeat the whole speech: he pointed at the slope and said, with no embellishment, that the clock ran the same for everyone.

It went better this time, without being spectacular. Nadia, her collarbone fully healed now, carried the full sack without anyone needing to correct her. Dara wasn't last — Asha was, for the first time, and she took it with the same calm as always. Riku improved enough not to repeat last time's joke, though the eye still yellowed and the lip still not fully healed made clear where part of that improvement had come from.

Nobody talked about numbers afterward. The Board barely moved that afternoon — the effort had been real, but the system measured something else in this test.

What did catch Nadia's attention, somewhere between the fifth and sixth repetition, was Noa.

---

Noa Shapira, from Johanna's group — the candidate she'd barely exchanged more than a greeting with since they'd arrived. Black hair pulled back so tight it didn't loosen even halfway up the slope, skin tanned by real sun, not a gym, a compact build with nothing to spare — the body of someone trained not to waste. Today she didn't move like that.

She didn't move differently in technique than any other day, but the usual administrative calm had been replaced by something close to contained rage against the slope itself. Every climb cost her more than her body should have allowed, and she didn't stop where anyone else would have.

On the thirteenth climb, her footing gave out all at once — not a clean stumble, the kind of failure that comes when the body stops negotiating with the head. She went down on her knees halfway up, the sack rolling to one side, her breathing wrong from every angle.

She wasn't the only one who fell — with two hundred people on the east wall, half a dozen fell every afternoon without anyone making a scene of it. What made Noa's different was how long help took to arrive, and how long she stayed down afterward.

Riku got there first, without thinking about it — a water bottle from a nearby row, offered with no ceremony, without even knowing yet whose it was. Nadia arrived a step behind, and between the two of them they helped her sit up instead of leaving her kneeling in the middle of the slope, without saying anything that sounded like pity or a question.

"Thanks," Noa said, once she could finally speak without her voice cutting out mid-word. "You didn't have to."

"I know," Riku said. "That's why we did."

Noa looked at him for a moment, something close to a smile crossing her exhausted face, and didn't say anything else until her breathing settled back to normal.

---

They sat against the wall together, later. Johanna didn't join them — she was still further down the slope, finishing her sets with the same regularity as always. Neither did Dax, working his own row further north.

"Why do you push yourself like that?" Nadia asked, the question coming out more direct than she'd planned. "Nobody was asking you to."

Noa took a moment to answer, still holding the water bottle. When she spoke, it didn't sound like a confession — it sounded like someone reciting an administrative fact she'd had to explain for years to people who'd never lived it themselves.

"Because this is my second attempt at classifying. A month and change here, at St. Gotthard. Before that, almost six years split between two different centers, one in Israel and one already in Europe. My family's been with AMAT for generations. It's not that I have more time than I need. It's that I don't have room to get it wrong even once." She kept going without anyone asking, her eyes fixed on the figure still climbing and descending the slope without pause. "Johanna's on her third attempt. Two months and change here, three years before that at a center in Germany without ever getting past this phase. Her first month at St. Gotthard, she didn't even make the top hundred. The second, she reached the final exam and lost it. If this one goes wrong, there's no fourth."

Nadia looked toward the slope, toward the figure that wouldn't stop, and understood all at once why Johanna hadn't come to sit with them. It wasn't pride. It was that sitting, even for a moment, meant admitting the body could stop before the clock demanded it.

Dax arrived then, unannounced. Riku had his own bottle in hand, turning it over absently, and Dax took it from him without a word, with the same calm as always — and went back to his row without ever opening his mouth.

Riku stood staring at his empty hand as if he still didn't fully understand what had just happened.

"He stole my bottle. Mine."

"You took his flag not that long ago," Noa said, without much ceremony. "This might be the closest thing to a comment he's going to make about it."

Nadia laughed for real, the first genuine laugh of the whole afternoon, and even Noa, still exhausted, let something like a smile slip through.

"That's his way," Noa said, once the laughter died down. "I've never heard him string two sentences together outside a fight. Six months here, from what I've heard, and nobody knows it from him directly — he's under review, special case, something administrative nobody explains fully. He's not competing to classify. He already did, a while back. He's still here for some other reason he doesn't share with anyone."

Riku and Nadia glanced at each other for a second, the kind of silence that gives away that neither of them had ever thought of Dax as someone with his own clock running, different from everyone else's.

Riku's hand rose toward his still-yellowed eye without meaning to, before he lowered it himself. He didn't need to ask anything — Noa saw it anyway, the kind of look that already knew where the gesture came from.

"Yegor's been here over a year," she said, without anyone asking. "He should have been reassigned a long time ago, with the cycles he's burned through without classifying. But he's still here. Another special case, just on the side nobody likes to look at closely — there's no review holding him with honors, nobody's just signed the paper that gets him out yet. There's more people like him than the new group tends to imagine. For some, this stopped being a camp a while ago. It's where they got stuck, with nobody deciding what to do about them."

Neither Riku nor Nadia said anything. Noa seemed to notice the gap the silence left — the lack of real urgency on both their faces, the look of someone who still hadn't done the math — and went on, this time without needing any gesture to invite her to.

"A month per cycle. That's the standard frame across the whole system, not just here. Intensive training, evaluation, result. If you don't classify, you start over, if you've got attempts left. Three attempts total. If all three run out, reassignment — it's not a punishment, you just stop being on the field track. You're still useful to AMAT, some other way."

Nadia thought about Yegor, about Milan, about everyone the fight club held inside it without anyone from Valais ever asking why they were there in the first place, or how many cycles they'd spent repeating the same thing without getting anywhere. She thought too, without being able to help it, about her own father, about the five steps of the procedure she already knew by heart from the Classification Room — and about how nobody in her own group had ever explained how many attempts they got, or what happened if they ran out, as if it were assumed the rules didn't apply to them the same way.

She didn't ask that last part out loud. But she kept the question, in the same place where she kept everything she still didn't know how to process fully.

---

Oliver heard all of it the next morning, secondhand, sitting on the edge of his own bed while Riku finished tying his boots.

"I was going to tell you last night," Riku said, not looking up from the laces. "But by the time I got back you weren't there, and this morning it was already too late for it to sound urgent."

"I was training," Oliver said, though they both knew he didn't need to say it. Every night since Val Tremola he'd gone back to the secondary room to build Momentum, a little longer each time, until he'd lost exact count of how much he already had stored — something past half an hour, and still climbing without him pushing it much.

Riku told him fast, without dramatizing it — Noa falling on the slope, the exact month of each cycle, the three attempts, Dax under review with nobody knowing why, Yegor unclassified and unreassigned, stuck somewhere he could no longer name properly. Oliver listened to all of it without saying a word until the end.

"That's messed up," he said, finally. "The thing with Yegor, especially."

"Yeah," Riku said. "But it also means the clock's running for everyone, not just us. A month, three attempts. And Dax has been here six months with nobody knowing why he's still around."

Oliver thought about that more than he let show. He didn't feel the same weight it seemed to have left on Riku — if anything, something about the concrete number, the real limit laid out on the table, sounded more like instruction than threat. They'd beaten Dax's group just days ago. The Board had rewarded it like nothing before. If the clock ran for everyone, it ran in his favor too, as long as it kept moving.

"Then we climb as much as we can, while we can," he said.

Riku looked at him for a second, something like doubt crossing his face, before nodding without much conviction.

"Yeah. I guess so."

---

The order for individual duels arrived before noon, taped next to the previous day's exercise notice — the same concrete-level pit where Oliver had lost to Dax days earlier, no glass railing between the candidates and the edge, the same instructors pretending to look at their tablets until something actually earned their attention.

Riku went first, against a candidate from Johanna's group who limped slightly on one ankle — nothing as bad as Riku's still-yellowed eye or his still-unhealed lip, but enough that neither of them started with a clean advantage. Riku won fast, needing no more than two well-placed Static exchanges, and stepped out of the pit without the smile Oliver expected to find on him — only something like relief, as if beating someone who was also damaged didn't fully count as a win.

Nadia faced Noa shortly after. It lasted longer than Oliver expected — Nadia measuring distance carefully, not giving ground fast, something she'd spent days watching Asha do under pressure. It wasn't enough. Noa read the whole exchange before Nadia finished setting up the second one, and closed the fight with the same cold economy she brought to everything — a short wrist lock, no need for extra force. Nadia took a moment to tap out, holding on longer than expected before hitting the ground with her palm, and when she stood up she wore the same face Riku had worn in the previous day's exercise: someone who'd lost, but confirmed something real in the process.

"You're still a problem for someone faster than you," Noa told her, without contempt. "That's not nothing."

Asha won hers with the same economy as always, wasting no extra movement. Pier did the same, slower but just as final, and stepped away from the pit without needing anyone to confirm the result. Dara lost hers clearly, against a candidate from Dax's group who gave her no room to think, and returned to the line with her jaw tight, something like frustration in her eyes. Petra, matched against a woman from Johanna's group younger than her but with real years of military training behind her, faced the same choice Oliver had already watched her make once before, in that same pit — a nearby object, comparable mass, the easy way out served on a plate — and once again decided against it. She lost with her fists, clean, no trick, and when she passed Oliver on her way to the edge, she didn't say anything. She didn't need to.

---

Oliver drew a candidate he didn't know by name — military, shorter than him, sitting a handful of positions above him on the Board, the kind of opponent the system put in front of you when it wanted to see whether a recent climb was real or just accumulated luck.

They took their places at opposite ends of the pit, three meters of concrete between them. Oliver waited to read the other man's weight before each step, the habit that had worked for him against half a dozen bodies trained the same way. It didn't help here: the candidate didn't walk in a straight line, moving diagonally, a short turn that left him two steps off from where he should have been, and turning again before Oliver could correct the angle.

This wasn't a fight of blows waiting to be read. It was constant motion, nothing still long enough to fix a tell.

The first contact wasn't a strike — a low sweep, the consequence of where the man's foot had landed after a turn, not a calculated attack. Oliver felt it late, his leg giving a centimeter, and only the habit of keeping his center low — whole days of night training, one after another — kept him from falling.

"Standing still won't get you a read on me," the candidate said, already turning again, his voice arriving from a different angle than expected.

Oliver stopped trying to anticipate the next move and did the only thing he could actually control: every glancing touch — an elbow, a shoulder brushing past on a turn, the edge of a foot — he absorbed instead of dodging, adding it to the reserve building up inside him. These weren't blows meant to hurt. They were noise, the kind of wear an acrobatic body used against someone trained to fight standing still.

The noise built up anyway.

On the sixth turn, the candidate changed the pattern without warning — a lateral jump that put him at Oliver's back, an angle nobody at St. Gotthard had shown him yet. The fist arrived from where Oliver wasn't looking.

It landed clean on his shoulder blade. It hurt for real, different from any blow absorbed so far — this one he hadn't seen coming in time.

Oliver had no room for an elegant answer. He turned too, just to avoid facing away again, and in the same turn released part of what he'd stored — scattered this time, not calculated like with Yegor at Val Tremola, a blind burst more than a precise strike.

It caught the candidate in the side mid-turn, cutting it short. It didn't drop him, but it threw off his balance enough that the next turn came out crooked.

The two of them stood facing each other, no longer turning, both breathing hard and full.

"Didn't have that one calculated," the candidate said, not sounding annoyed. Almost curious.

"Neither did I," Oliver said.

The instructor cut the fight there, not letting either of them risk their whole body over an advantage that didn't exist yet. Neither protested. They separated with the same cold courtesy Oliver had already seen from Dax and Johanna days earlier, and somewhere, without either of them seeing it happen, someone must have written down the word they already knew by heart: draw.

Oliver stayed a moment in the center of the pit, his breathing still full, without the euphoria he'd expected to feel. He thought about Riku, winning without smiling. About Nadia, losing with something closer to pride than defeat. About Petra, deciding again against the easy way.

Nobody mentioned the Board that afternoon. Nobody asked either. Whatever the screen was going to say once it updated, Oliver already knew, looking at his own hand still closed around what was left of his unspent Momentum, that it wasn't the number that had really changed that day.
