# Chapter 112 — The Impossible Math

---

The hot water lasted less than Oliver would have liked, but by the time it ran out he'd already run the math three separate times, each worse than the last.

A hundred fifty-six were left that afternoon. If the rate of decline held — and it had held for three days, almost with the regularity of a clock — by the exam's last day there wouldn't be more than seventy left, maybe fewer. Twenty-five spots among seventy wasn't an impossible math problem by itself. The impossible part was the other half: how many badges the six of them needed, not just to make it in, but to make it in with enough margin that none of them got left out by a single spot.

He ran the numbers anyway, just to see what came out. More than forty badges between the six of them, counting the thirteen they already had — and that was without the Board itself taking any away in the process, without any of them falling into a direct encounter with Reber, Anka, or Father Conti. That last detail weighed on him more than the number itself: the whole calculation assumed no instructor ever touched them, because up to now, as a group, they'd dedicated themselves entirely to avoiding them — hunting candidates, not crossing paths with the people who could actually knock them out in one stroke. The real math, the one that included that risk, was even worse than the one he'd just done.

It sounded ridiculous. It was ridiculous — more badges than the six of them together had gathered in three full days, multiplied several times over, and all of it without a single failure anywhere along the way.

And yet.

He thought about Val Tremola, about Yegor loading the weight into his back before the hit he'd thought would end it all, and about how he himself had dropped his guard on purpose just to let that hit land — thirty minutes of Momentum built up in silence, no one the wiser, converted all at once into a pivot on one foot and a punch concentrated on a point the size of a coin. He thought about the fight club, more recently, about the exact instant that fight had ended before the last punch, not after. He thought about the first night, about the four badges he and Riku had gathered between the two of them, against every prediction, when no one else would have even dared try. He thought, not entirely by choice, about the last time he'd seen Petra — her head bandaged, half her face still marked with scratches, smiling anyway, as if the price were already paid and she owed no one anything for it. He wondered, not for the first time, whether she would have left regardless, even if he'd done something different that afternoon on the rappel, or whether part of that leaving without goodbyes was also something he himself had pushed into happening without wanting to see it at the time.

Maybe it wasn't so impossible. Maybe, for him, it wasn't.

---

He came out of the shower with his hair still dripping and the towel tied badly, and it was Asha's voice, low but clear from the living room, that stopped him before he crossed the hallway.

"Riku, listen to me. I know Oliver's idea sounds good to you because the first night worked. But the first night there were two of you, not six, and you got lucky with who crossed your path."

"I'm not saying he's right about everything," Riku said. "I'm saying splitting up would cover more ground than we covered today, and today we lost a clean target because we were all watching the same street."

"There's another way to cover more ground without splitting up entirely," Asha said. "The first day I saw which way Johanna's group moved when we left the warehouse — north, past where we've been hunting. No one's touched that zone yet. We could try there before we break anything."

"And if that doesn't turn up anything either," Pier said, his voice calm as always, "we still have the other bet. We don't need to win every day. If the drop rate holds, there'll be seventy left by the last day, eighty at most. We could spend the remaining days just gathering data — where everyone sleeps, how they move, who drops their guard — and put it all on the line the final day, when everyone's margin for error is as small as it's going to get all week."

"That's also crazy," Riku said.

"Less crazy than what Oliver wants to do right now," Asha said. "Talk to him. He listens to you in a way he doesn't to us."

That was the moment Nadia and Dara walked in through the front door, loaded down with bags, and Dara's "hey" — aimed at Oliver, still standing in the hallway with the towel tied badly — cut the conversation dead. Asha, Pier, and Riku all turned at once, and in the way they did it, Oliver knew there was no longer any way to pretend he hadn't heard.

---

"It's fine," Oliver said, stepping fully into view. "What you're proposing is reasonable. It's a good plan, for the right situation."

No one said anything yet, waiting for the rest.

"But to me it's the same as standing still." He said it without raising his voice, without the anger from earlier this time, something colder and more tired. "I ran the numbers in the shower. With what we have now, even if we wait for the last day, it's not enough for all six of us. And I'm not the only one who can do that math, so I know you already know it, even if nobody's said it out loud yet."

"That's exactly why we have to play it smart, not fast," Asha said.

"Or we have to get enough of a surplus that no one has to be left out," Oliver said. "That's the only thing I actually care about. Not that my number looks good. That it's enough for all six of us. And right now it isn't, no matter what we do together, moving as slowly as we're moving."

"Oliver—" Asha started.

He'd already turned toward his room, not waiting for her to finish.

---

The backpack was emptier than he would have liked to admit.

A little food, not enough for four days. A cap. A pair of sunglasses he didn't even remember packing. A lighter. The knife he'd bought on the second day, the price tag still stuck to the handle. A few meters of rope, coiled without much care. A third of the first day's cash, the rest already spent without his noticing exactly on what.

He thought about going back to the living room and taking some of what Nadia and Dara had just brought. He talked himself out of it almost as fast as the idea came — he didn't want anyone to see what he was about to do before it was already done.

He pulled a shirt on over the T-shirt already stretched out of shape from use, and over that a thin jacket — not much warmth for what was coming, but better than nothing. He slung the backpack over one shoulder and opened the curtainless window, the same cold as before pouring into the room at once.

He had one foot already on the sill when the door opened behind him.

Riku didn't say anything at first. He stood there, hand still on the handle, and the two of them held each other's gaze exactly long enough that neither could pretend not to know what was happening.

"You told me I'd leave," Oliver said, finally. "You were right."

"I didn't want to be."

"I know." Oliver already had half his body out the window. "I'm sorry. For this, and for this morning."

Riku didn't move to stop him.

"I'll come back," Oliver said, and jumped before anyone — even himself — could say something that might change his mind.

---

Hours later, on a rooftop he'd reached without quite remembering the exact route, Oliver wondered how each of them would have reacted if they could see him now.

Asha, arms crossed, the same flat stare from the living room. Pier, silent, waiting for him to realize on his own something he already knew. Riku, saying nothing, because he'd already said it all at the window. He chased the thought away before it reached Nadia or Dara — there was no point carrying six versions of the same disappointment at once.

Instead, he forced himself to go over what actually mattered.

Three days sweeping the area before tonight had left him a map no one else had complete — not on paper, not on the Board's device, only in his head, built street by street without fully meaning to. He knew which corner he'd spotted the same face watching twice from a doorway. He knew where two groups had clashed the night before, from the marks he'd read on the ground hours later, with no witness to confirm it. He knew exactly which alley he'd gotten the badge he'd left on the table that same morning, and he knew, above all, where the groups that preferred moving at night passed through, instead of holing up like everyone else.

He hadn't done it on purpose. Or not entirely. But looking now at the map he carried inside him, he understood he'd spent three days preparing for exactly this, without ever deciding it at any one moment — every night sweep, every memorized zone, one more step toward a night like this, long before the argument in the living room pushed him to admit it out loud.

---

The shape crossed the alley two streets down, fast, hugging the wall, and Oliver had it through the Registry before his eyes finished confirming it — a stocky man, worn green military jacket, hair shaved almost to the scalp.

He dropped from one rooftop to the next, Void holding him fully dark, and followed from above, roof after roof, the man in the jacket never once looking up. It led him into an area he hadn't covered in his earlier sweeps — narrower streets, older buildings — until the man slipped through a half-open gate into one of those interior courtyards Milan hid behind facades that promised nothing from the street.

Oliver stayed at the edge of the lowest roof, right above the entrance, and waited.

A second person arrived about eight minutes later — a young woman, thin, a dark cap pulled low over her eyebrows, looking back twice before crossing through the same gate.

He counted the exits while he waited — the main one, where both of them had gone in; a side one, toward a service alley; no others, as far as he could see from up there. Enough. He decided to go in.

A third appeared just as Oliver finished dropping down to the exact edge above the door — thin, gray tracksuit, a backpack slung across his chest — and he waited for the man to fully arrive before moving: letting him go in alone would have been cleaner, but arriving with him was faster, and fast was what he needed tonight.

Inside, someone checked through a fisheye peephole. The door opened.

Oliver jumped at the exact instant, Swap active before he touched the ground, and the world traded places without a sound: the man in the gray tracksuit vanished from the threshold, and it was Oliver who crossed through the door in his place, one step behind whoever had opened it.

---

For a few seconds, everything inside the apartment kept looking exactly like what it had been before Oliver arrived.

The man in the military jacket walked ahead of him without turning around, taking for granted that whoever was behind him was the same man he'd just let in a second earlier. They crossed a short, undecorated hallway into a room where three others sat waiting around a low table — badges spread among them, a folded map, the remains of an unfinished dinner.

One of them, an older man with several days' worth of beard, shot to his feet at the sight of him, surprise crossing his face before he found the voice to say anything.

By the time the man in the military jacket turned to see what was happening, it was already too late. Oliver landed a kick loaded with Momentum straight into his side, and the impact threw him into the bearded man — both bodies collided and rolled together against the wall, neither of them ever quite understanding what had just happened.

The woman in the dark cap, the one who'd arrived eight minutes after the first man, managed to raise her hands before Oliver finished turning toward her — and stayed there, fixed, arms halfway through a guard she never got to finish. Anchor. She wasn't moving from that spot until Oliver decided otherwise.

Of the other two at the table, a thin man in a plaid shirt was the first to actually react, throwing a direct punch that Oliver read in time — not the real attack, a feint to see whether Oliver was still reading the gesture instead of the vector. Oliver shifted the angle mid-motion, and the real blow only grazed his shoulder instead of landing full. He answered with a short elbow, Momentum still loaded from the earlier impact, and the man in the plaid shirt dropped sideways, stunned, not unconscious.

At some point in that first exchange, without anyone catching the exact motion, one of the badges on the table stopped being on the table.

The last one, a small woman with short black hair and a thin scar crossing one eyebrow, was the only one who didn't lunge right away. She hung back, her breathing controlled, and when she finally moved it wasn't straight at Oliver — it was in short bursts, impossible to fully track by eye, a speed that didn't match anything Oliver had seen from this apartment so far. A real Domain, then. Not just a frightened candidate.

That was enough to make the two others still on their feet — the man in the plaid shirt, still dazed but back on his legs, and the man in the military jacket, recovered from the hit against the wall — react at once, as if the woman's speed had returned, all at once, the nerve they'd just lost.

Oliver dodged the military jacket's punch by twisting his torso, and the plaid shirt's by ducking under an arm that passed through where his head had been an instant before. It was her who landed a hit — one of her bursts connecting straight to his side, the same spot that already ached from before — and Oliver didn't try to block it. He let it land, and in that same instant released Impulse against her, the force he'd taken converted into something far bigger, sending her straight back in a line.

She flew back through the hallway, clearing the whole length of it before anyone else could react, and slammed into the railing at the far end — the one overlooking the interior courtyard — right in the frame of the door Oliver had left open when he came in.

Oliver triggered Swap the same second as the impact, the image of her still in the air fixed in his memory, and the world traded places again without a sound.

He left the whole room in confusion — two men on their feet no longer sure who they were fighting, a third still fixed in place by Anchor, and the scarred woman, who an instant before had been flying toward the railing and now, with no visible transition to anyone watching, was standing in the middle of the room, as disoriented as everyone else.

---

Oliver reappeared by the railing, the shock of the swap still vibrating through his own body, and didn't waste time fully getting his bearings before heading down the stairs toward the entrance.

The man he'd swapped with on the way in, the third one he'd seen enter from outside, was still there, sprawled near the street door — the fall from where Oliver had been crouched had left something broken in his leg, judging by the angle. Hurt, not seriously, but unable to get up in time for anything. Barely conscious, his half-open eyes following Oliver without the strength to do more than that.

Oliver crouched, slipped the badge from around his neck without needing to struggle, and held it a moment alongside the others before pocketing them all.

The man's phone lay half a meter away, the screen still lit. Oliver picked it up and dialed the emergency number himself, waiting until a voice on the other end asked what was happening, where they were — and only then set it back down on the ground, beside the man's hand, the operator still talking with no one answering.

He left smiling, something he hadn't let himself do in three days.
