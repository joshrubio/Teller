# Chapter 37 — Noise

Pier was already in the dining hall when Oliver arrived.

It wasn't late — it was seven fifteen, forty minutes before the usual breakfast hour — and the dining hall was empty except for the two of them. Pier had made coffee in the Italian moka pot Asha had criticized three weeks earlier, and he'd made it without the precision Asha would have demanded: too much pressure, probably, the kind of coffee that tasted like urgency. Oliver poured himself a cup and sat down across the table.

Pier held his with both hands, his fingers wrapping the porcelain with the same exactness he would have used holding any other object — as if the cup's weight were a variable he'd calculated before lifting it. He didn't do it with effort. He did it the same way Oliver used to square a column of numbers before closing it out: by habit, without conscious thought, because a body that's spent long enough doing something ends up doing it without instructions.

"First thing," Pier said, without looking up from the cup, "it's not a flaw."

Oliver waited.

"The involuntary part." Pier looked up then, and it was the look of someone who'd learned to distribute his attention with the same precision he distributed his weight. "Maren will treat it as a variable to control. She's not wrong. But there's a difference between controlling something because you're stronger than it and controlling it because you understand what it does."

"What was yours?" Oliver asked.

Pier set the cup down on the table. Not gently — precisely. Two centimeters from the edge, exact angle, as if he'd decided beforehand where it was going to be.

"Third month here. Applied physics lab in Lausanne — I didn't know yet that this was where I'd end up." Pier looked at the cup he'd just set down. "Department meeting. Twelve people around a table. Someone said something I disagreed with."

He stopped there. Oliver let him stop.

"For about four seconds, the local gravitational field changed direction."

"How much?"

"Enough that three cups of coffee spilled upward." Pier looked at him with something that wasn't quite humor but occupied the same space. "My colleague Renata's chair rose two centimeters off the floor. She was in it."

Oliver tightened his fingers around his own cup without noticing.

"What did they do?"

"What people do when something has no reasonable explanation. They decided there'd been a structural vibration and called maintenance." Pier picked the cup back up, the same exact gesture. "Renata filed a materials fatigue report three days later. Forty-six pages."

"And you?"

"I signed the report." He said it without inflection, without the weight of guilt or the lightness of someone who no longer feels that guilt — with the neutrality of someone who's had enough time to file something under the right category. "And I called the only person who'd given me their number four years earlier, telling me that if anything strange happened near me, I should call."

Oliver recognized the shape of the story.

"How did you control it?"

"I didn't control it." Pier leaned forward. "I accepted that the signal was always going to be there. The difference between involuntary and deliberate isn't willpower. It's knowing what you're feeling before it acts."

Oliver looked at his own hands on the table. His fingers were tense for no reason. He opened them slowly.

"The problem is I didn't feel it until it was already happening."

"I know." Pier stood up. "That's why the next step isn't the observation session with Maren."

Oliver waited.

"It's the nine o'clock session with Yusuf."

---

Yusuf Adeyemi was shorter than Oliver had calculated from the sound of his footsteps in the corridor — the footsteps of someone who knew where his weight was at every moment, which explained the sound better than his height did. Mid-forties, short hair, a diagonal scar on his jaw that no one had ever mentioned, because at the Center no one mentioned the obvious. He wore training clothes with no logo, and the room where he was waiting for Oliver had the same functional layout as everything else in the building: surfaces that didn't try to look like anything other than what they were.

"Oliver," he said, without the question mark that would have meant he was checking. A statement. "Sit on the floor."

Oliver hadn't expected that. He sat. The floor was polished stone, cold through his clothes.

Yusuf sat facing him with the ease of someone for whom this wasn't a gesture but a working posture — back straight without rigidity, hands on his knees, the kind of posture a body learns when it's spent long enough being a tool before it becomes habit.

"Pier told me about the glass."

"Yes."

"I'm not interested in the glass. I'm interested in what your body did half a second before it happened."

Oliver thought about it.

"Tension in my hands."

"Where exactly?"

"In the space around my fingers. Not in the muscle."

Yusuf nodded with the slowness of someone confirming a hypothesis.

"The Domain doesn't operate in a vacuum. It operates from the body, and the body holds information the head hasn't processed yet." He extended a hand in front of him, fingers spread, palm up. "When I activate mine, there's a specific heat at the base of my left thumb. Always. Before I do anything, before I decide to do anything."

Oliver looked at the hand.

"What does yours do?"

"Not relevant to what we're going to work on." Yusuf lowered his hand. "What matters is that you've spent ten years deliberately ignoring it and it's still there. The body doesn't forget the things that matter."

Oliver didn't ask why he'd ignored it. It wasn't that kind of conversation yet.

"Stand up," Yusuf said.

Oliver stood.

What followed wasn't what Oliver had expected. There were no strikes, no equipment, no kind of physical conditioning the term suggested. Yusuf asked him to walk slowly around the room — five meters in each direction — while naming out loud everything he perceived. Not of the Odem. Of the body.

"Weight on your heels or on your toes."

"Heels."

"Equal on both?"

Oliver adjusted his attention.

"No. The left more."

"Temperature in your hands."

"Cold. Both."

"The same?"

"Yes."

"Shoulders."

"Tense. The right more."

Yusuf wrote nothing down. He listened without missing a detail.

"The problem with passive reading," he said, as Oliver completed the third pass, "is that you don't distinguish between external signal and internal signal. It all comes in mixed together. The body starts responding to other people's signatures as if they were its own, because there's no clear line yet between what you see and what you do with what you see."

"And Asha had been a constant signal for weeks."

"Exactly." Yusuf crossed the room in four steps and positioned himself in front of Oliver, a meter and a half away. "Now hold still and tell me when something changes."

Oliver waited.

Yusuf moved.

It wasn't a demonstration move — there was no artificiality to it, nothing executed to be observed. It was simply a lateral shift of three steps, a turn on the left axis, the arms following the torso with the kind of economy that indicates someone has made that movement thousands of times. No waste. No visible wind-up. Like a well-maintained mechanism executing exactly what it should and nothing more.

Oliver watched it and something in his perception activated without him deciding it.

Not the Odem. The pattern.

The structure of the movement — the order in which the weight transferred, the angle of the turn, the radius the arms kept from the torso's axis — was legible in the same way the structure of an active Domain was legible. Not identical. But the same kind of information: layers, direction, point of origin. A sequence with internal logic, something that could be understood before it was executed.

Oliver said nothing for a second.

"What did you notice?" Yusuf asked.

"The movement has structure."

Yusuf looked at him.

"Go on."

"Like..." Oliver searched for the right analogy. "When I read the Odem of an active Domain, I see layers. Where the force comes from, where it's going, where it anchors. What you just did has the same thing. The weight left your right heel before the torso turned. That came first."

The silence that followed had a different quality from Maren's silences.

"Repeat it," Yusuf said, neither confirming nor challenging it.

"The right heel first. Then the turn from the left axis. The arms follow, they don't lead."

Yusuf did the movement again, more slowly. Oliver saw the same thing — the same layers, the same order, the same internal logic. Like a file running at reduced speed.

"Can you reproduce it?"

Oliver tried the shift. It came out clumsy — his body knew it should do something but didn't have the fine map yet. Too much force in the shoulders, the turn came late, the arms didn't follow but anticipated.

"No," he said.

"Not yet," Yusuf corrected, and it was the first time he'd used that specific tone — the tone of someone who wasn't being kind but exact. "Reading the structure and reproducing it are different steps. You just did the first one. The second one takes longer."

Oliver took a second with that.

"Do you teach this to everyone here?"

"No." Yusuf looked at him with the expression of someone who wasn't going to elaborate, and then elaborated anyway. "Pier doesn't need to learn it — his body already works that way. Asha took three months to understand that her Domain and her posture are connected. Riku can't practice it in enclosed spaces yet."

"And the rest?"

"The rest learn to move with one Domain. You have to learn to move with several, when that point comes." Yusuf gestured minimally at the space between them. "What you just noticed is going to be useful sooner than you think."

Oliver didn't ask when. It was the kind of information that would arrive when it arrived, and Yusuf wasn't the kind of person early questions worked on.

"Tomorrow we continue." Yusuf turned toward the door and then stopped, not turning all the way around. "Today, when you finish, go to the east courtyard. Riku has an exposure session."

"Exposure to what?"

"To someone who doesn't turn off phones when he walks into a room."

---

The east courtyard was the smallest at the center — a stone rectangle with three benches and a steel roof that, Oliver understood on arrival, was strategically far from any critical electrical system. No artificial lighting. No equipment. It was, essentially, a space designed to contain static without the static breaking anything important.

Riku sat on the center bench with his knees slightly apart and his hands on his thighs, in the specific posture of someone who's learned that moving too much makes nearby electronics emit sounds of protest. Seventeen years old, uneven black hair, an expression Oliver recognized because he'd seen it before in people who'd spent a long time being the problem in the room and had ended up internalizing it as a personality trait.

Oliver sat on the bench across from him. And watched.

Not with the passive attention of the previous weeks — with the attention Yusuf had been building over the last hour: deliberate inventory, internal signal distinguished from external signal. What the body noticed. What the eyes saw.

What the eyes saw was different from what Oliver had expected.

Riku's passive signature wasn't dense like Asha's — it wasn't volume or mass occupying the same space. It was movement. The Odem around Riku oscillated in a radius of about a meter, with the specific cadence of something that can't stop because stopping isn't part of its nature. It wasn't visible the way the contours of solid things were visible — it wasn't edge or boundary. It was like looking at the air near an intense heat source, that refraction effect that distorts the background, except the background wasn't distorting: what moved was the Odem itself, oscillating at a frequency Oliver could follow with his eyes if he concentrated but that vanished the moment he tried to fix it in place.

Like trying to read text on a screen with too high a refresh rate.

"You don't have a phone," Riku said.

"Maren kept it, during the transfer. Mine."

"Ah." Riku thought about it. "Pier always carries one that doesn't work anymore. To remind me it can be here without consequences."

"Does it work?"

"The phone, no. The demonstration, yes."

Oliver kept watching. Not Riku's face — the meter-wide radius around him, where the Odem did what it did. He tried to hold his visual attention steady without trying to stop it mentally, without trying to read it yet — just registering it, the same way Yusuf had asked him to name the weight in his heels without shifting the weight yet.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"Yes."

"How does it feel to you, when it's active?"

Riku looked at him, weighing whether the question was technical or whether they were in a different kind of conversation.

"Like noise... not sound. The difference between a clean signal and a signal with interference is that the interference won't let you ignore it. I carry it with me. It's always there."

"Even now?"

"Especially now." Riku looked at his own hands. "Now that there's something nearby that can respond."

Oliver looked back at the oscillation radius. He tried what Yusuf had asked him to do with the movement — look for the structure, not just the effect. Riku's Odem oscillated, yes, but not randomly. There was a pattern in the cadence — like a wave that always breaks at the same distance, even though the sea isn't the same water twice. The frequency was constant. What changed was the direction.

"I'm reading your Odem," Oliver said.

Riku didn't answer right away.

"And?"

"It moves. Not like the others — the others have volume, static presence. Yours oscillates. Like..." Oliver searched for the right image. "Like the field near an active transformer, but with no fixed direction. The frequency is constant. What changes is where it's pointing."

Riku was looking at him now with a different kind of attention.

"Can you see it right now? This moment?"

"Yes. If I don't try to fix it in place. If I fix it, it disappears — it's too fast to hold still."

"How wide a radius."

"About a meter from where you're sitting."

Riku looked at the space around him as if trying to verify something he'd never been able to see directly.

"Maren tells me two meters at full activation." Riku dropped his eyes to his left hand. "Nobody had ever told me what it was in passive."

"A meter," Oliver repeated. "Denser toward your left than your right. I don't know if that means anything."

"My left thumb," Riku said slowly.

Oliver looked at him.

"What?"

"It always starts in the left thumb. When it loses control, when something fails near me." Riku looked at his left hand. "Nobody had ever confirmed the asymmetry was real. I thought it was psychosomatic."

It wasn't. Oliver could see it: the Odem oscillated with more amplitude on Riku's left radius, as if the source of the signal had a lateral origin, not a central one. It was a data point. Probably an important one for Maren, for the sessions, for how Riku would learn to modulate the interference when that point in the progression arrived.

"It's not psychosomatic," Oliver said.

Riku didn't answer. He looked at the space around him for several seconds with the expression of someone who's just received the first outside description of something that's spent years being only internal.

"Why can you see it?" he said finally.

"Because seeing is the base condition." Oliver searched for a way to explain it that didn't require all the terminology from the sessions with Maren. "I don't know exactly how it works. But vision comes first. Without it, the rest doesn't operate."

"The rest of what."

"Of what the Registry does."

Riku nodded slowly, the kind of nod of someone cataloging new information without classifying it yet.

"Maren wants to do joint sessions," he said. "I asked for it to be here."

"Why?"

"Because if something goes wrong, the only thing that breaks in this courtyard is the benches."

Oliver looked at the benches. They were stone.

"Reasonable," he said.

Riku's Odem kept oscillating in its meter-wide radius, constant, asymmetric to the left. Oliver watched it a moment longer without trying to read it or hold onto it — just seeing it, the same way he'd been doing before he even knew he could, back when it had no name.

It was noise. But the noise had frequency.

And frequency, Oliver was starting to understand, was always the beginning of a structure.
