# Chapter 97 — Almost

---

The safe house was empty when Park arrived, and had been empty long enough for the coffee in the kitchen to have gone cold, but not long enough for dust to have started settling on the table where someone had left, face-down, an unwashed plate.

"Hours," Elena said, kneeling by the bedroom door, her palm extended without quite touching the wood. "Not days. Hours."

"How many?" Park said.

"Less than six." Elena closed her eyes for a moment, reading something Park couldn't see. "And this time it's not as clean as the last two. There's still something real here, not just the echo of someone trying to erase it."

Marc was already at the window, cataloging the street with his usual methodical economy. Sofia worked through the rest of the apartment, looking for what Elena couldn't read with the Domain — a receipt, a date, a name.

"They left in a hurry this time," Sofia said, from the bedroom. "The rest of their clothes are still in the closet. They only took the small backpack."

Park stood a moment in the center of the room, letting the space tell him what nobody had said out loud yet. Three apartments in under three weeks, each cleaner than the last — until this one. Someone had made a mistake, or someone had had to leave so fast the mistake stopped being optional.

"I want to talk to Aurelio," Park said.

---

They hadn't had the name until two days ago — a resource contact Marc had traced through a chain of cash payments too regular to be coincidence, three visits to the same spare-parts back room in under a month. Park recognized him the moment he walked in: the same grease-stained hands he'd seen weeks earlier, in another city along the route Lev and Sabine had followed, stepping out of a double-parked car with a blanket in the back seat still holding the shape of a body. Aurelio didn't recognize him. There was no way he could have — that night Park had never gotten close enough before the man vanished into city traffic.

Aurelio didn't resist when Marc and Sofia stopped him leaving the shop. He didn't lie either, not in any way that mattered — he carried the genuine fear of someone starting to understand, too late, what he'd gotten himself into.

"I don't know who you people are," he said, hands open on the table in the room where Park questioned him, without raising his voice. "I never asked. Documents, cash, that's what I do. I don't ask why people need it."

"What did they tell you yesterday?" Park said.

"That they were leaving that same night. That they needed whatever I already had, without waiting for the full order." Aurelio hesitated, something crossing his face that wasn't a lie — it was shame. "I asked the woman if she was all right. I'd never asked anyone that before. She said yes. It didn't sound true."

"Did they mention a destination?"

"Trains. Just that. They didn't say which one."

Park got nothing more out of him, and nothing more was needed. He left the room with a rough direction and the uncomfortable certainty that Aurelio, once this was over, would still never know who he'd helped — or whether what he'd done counted as help or as the first step of something he wouldn't be able to undo.

---

The small station on the outskirts had fewer cameras than the central one, which was exactly why Sofia flagged it first among the three reasonable options. An employee, bribed with half the truth Marc was willing to offer, confirmed two passengers matching the description, a train that had left the night before, and a single stop where the system logged a last-minute ticket change.

"It's the only real lead we're going to get," Elena said, already back in the car. "If we wait to confirm everything, we lose the window."

Park didn't wait to confirm everything.

---

They reached the freight platform at dusk, the least lit stretch of a transit station most travelers didn't even know existed — containers stacked in two rows, a freight train stopped on track three, and between the shadows of the containers, two figures moving with the kind of contained hurry of people who still believed they had time to spare.

Park saw them before they saw him. For half a second it was almost disappointing how simple it turned out to be — after three weeks of cold trails and empty apartments, there they were, two people walking between containers like any other couple in a hurry to get somewhere.

The second ended when Sofia, twelve meters to Park's right, let her boot brush a loose piece of metal.

The sound wasn't loud. It was enough.

Lev turned first. Sabine was already moving before his head finished turning, the two of them with the sync of people who'd spent months practicing exactly this moment without wanting to admit it. They didn't run in a straight line — Sabine cut toward track three, Lev toward the containers, splitting apart to divide whatever pursuit came after them.

"Sofia, take Sabine," Park said. "Marc, with me."

He went after Lev.

The first container gave Lev a second's head start that he spent well — by the time Park rounded it, Lev had already planted his feet between two rows of stacked metal, facing him, no real intention of running further. Something in his stance had changed. This wasn't flight anymore. It was a different decision.

Turning into the container corridor, Park caught, over his shoulder, the other half of what was happening: Sofia and Sabine, thirty meters away on the far side of track three, Sofia trying to cut off her path to the freight train and Sabine changing direction three times in five seconds, neither of them closing the distance completely. He had no time to see more than that. He turned back to Lev.

"We don't want to hurt you," Park said, the distance still at six meters, calculating the angle if Lev decided to move left.

"I know," Lev said. "That's what scares me."

It wasn't a threat. If anything, it sounded closer to exhaustion.

Park took a lateral step, testing whether Lev reacted to the movement or only to the distance — the kind of quick read he ran before committing to anything. Lev didn't move with him. His eyes were fixed on a point behind Park, not on Park himself, and only when Park understood, too late, that Lev wasn't measuring him but calculating the geometry of the corridor itself, did Lev reach both hands toward the nearest container to his right — not toward Park — and pull.

It wasn't the simple pull Park would have recognized as an ordinary use of a Heat Domain — this went deeper, faster, something felt in the air itself before it could be seen: the temperature of the whole corridor between containers dropped all at once, sharp enough that Park's own breath turned visible in an instant. Frost began spreading across the nearest container's metal in real time, a flat white advancing over the surface like a disease, and the cold didn't stop there — it kept pulling, taking something beyond temperature, until the container's own structure seemed to give up a degree that had no name on any scale Park knew.

"Back off," Marc said, already retreating himself, his voice tight.

Park didn't back off in time.

Lev closed his hands, and everything he'd just pulled out of the container — concentrated cold, stolen density, the very substance an object needed to keep being what it was — came back out all at once, inverted, turned into something that had no right to exist under any physics Park had ever studied: a blast of heat so violent the air between Lev and Park visibly distorted, and the container itself, already weakened to the bone by the extraction, split at the seam with the sound of metal giving way under pressure it was never built to hold.

Park threw himself sideways. The heat grazed his left shoulder — it never fully touched him, but the air itself burned, a dry, surface pain that would raise blisters later, once he had time to notice it.

"Sofia!" Marc shouted, from somewhere beyond the row of containers.

Park didn't see what happened. He heard it: a short cry, real, cut off mid-note, and then the sound of something metal falling on a body.

Lev heard it too. Something in his face broke — not the calculated coldness of a seasoned fugitive, but the genuine panic of someone who'd just done something he hadn't planned to do, or had planned to do differently.

He didn't wait to see the result. He ran toward the sound, not away from it — toward Sabine, toward whatever had just happened to someone who wasn't him.

Park let him go. He found Sofia two rows further on, sitting against a container, one hand pressed to her side, real blood between her fingers but conscious, cursing under her breath in a way that, strangely, reassured Park more than any medical report could have.

"A piece of the container," Sofia said, teeth clenched. "It wasn't her. Sabine never touched me. It went flying when the other one blew his open."

By the time Park raised his eyes back toward the tracks, there was no one left between the containers. Only frost melting fast on overheated metal, and a silence that explained nothing of what had just happened.

---

They went back to the car without the capture, Sofia holding her side in the back seat and Elena already working on the wound with the same precision she used to read traces. Nobody spoke for the first few minutes.

"It wasn't premeditated," Park said, finally, more to himself than to the others. "What he did with the container. It wasn't a weapon he was carrying ready."

"How do you know?" Marc said.

"Because after he did it, he didn't come finish us off," Park said. "He went to check on Sabine."

Nobody answered that. Park sat looking out the window at the station falling behind, thinking about the file he'd read twice on the flight over, weeks ago — Heat Domain, high-end Level 2, advanced undocumented use, not a single line on who the person behind those numbers actually was, or on why someone capable of something that destructive had spent the last twenty minutes running instead of attacking, right up to the exact second he stopped having another option.

Elena finished bandaging Sofia's side and sank back against her seat, eyes closed, not from physical exhaustion but the kind of tiredness that came from reading too many things at once for too long.

"The trail's already gone," she said, without anyone asking. "Whatever he did to that container, it left behind something I've never felt before. It's not just emptied out. It's like it can't ever fill back up again, not even given time."

"What does that mean?" Marc said.

"That what he can do isn't reversible," Elena said. "And that if he ever decides to use it on a person instead of a container, we're not going to get a second chance to stop him."

Nobody answered that either. The file said one thing. What Park had just seen said another, and he didn't know yet what to do with the difference — or with the possibility, harder to ignore by the minute, that the official version of Lev and Sabine had been written by someone who'd never seen them run toward each other's danger instead of away from it.
