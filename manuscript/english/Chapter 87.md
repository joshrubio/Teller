# Chapter 87 — Second Round

---

Two days before the session, Voss stopped reviewing files.

He found Park Taeho in the lobby of a hotel that wasn't his own, at an hour that was no accident — Voss knew, from six years of shared sessions, that Park ate dinner alone every Tuesday, without exception, at the same restaurant fifteen minutes on foot from wherever he stayed when Geneva required him. It wasn't coincidence. It was the kind of information a senior evaluator accumulated without setting out to, and that only became useful the day he decided to stop pretending he didn't have it.

"I'm not going to ask how you knew I'd be here," Park Taeho said, no real surprise in his voice, gesturing to the empty chair across from him.

"Good," Voss said, sitting down. "That way I don't have to lie to you about how easy it was."

Park Taeho didn't quite smile, but something close to it crossed his face. He ordered for both of them without asking — something local, unpretentious, the kind of dish a man orders when he's spent years eating alone in the same city and stopped looking at the menu long ago — and waited for the waiter to step away before speaking again.

"We've shared a room for six years," he said. "This is the first time you've sought me out outside it."

"It's the first time I've needed you outside it," Voss said. "I don't confuse the two. I know perfectly well that what I'm asking isn't free — not for you, not for me."

"What do you need, Evaluator Voss?"

"Nothing you can't give without committing to anything," Voss said. "I'm not asking you to back my case. I'm asking that, if someone tests you in session, you answer with what you actually think instead of what's comfortable. That's all I need from you."

Park Taeho fell silent a moment, the kind of silence Voss already knew well — not indecision, but the exact amount of time it took Park Taeho to decide whether the question he was about to ask deserved to be asked out loud.

"Why me, and not Furrer, if you're looking for something that sounds neutral?"

"Because Furrer already decided his position before I ever got to this city," Voss said. "You haven't yet. That makes you more valuable, not less."

"That could also be read as me being used to pressure Furrer without him knowing he's being pressured."

"It could," Voss said, not denying it. "The question I'm asking isn't whether that's true. It's whether you care that it is, so long as what I'm asking of you is, in itself, honest."

Park Taeho studied him a moment longer, and something in his expression — the same small recalibration Voss would see again later, in the room, when Furrer named the pattern in his Registry cases — suggested he'd already made the decision before the conversation reached this point, and what remained was only the formality of saying so.

"I'm going to hear the case seriously," Park Taeho said. "That's true regardless, with or without this dinner. If someone asks me something in session, I'm not going to pretend we didn't talk today. But I'm also not going to lie in your favor if the honest answer doesn't favor you."

"I'm not asking you to lie," Voss said. "I'm asking exactly the opposite."

The food arrived, and they talked about other things for a while — AMAT-KR, a recent case Park Taeho mentioned in passing without going into detail, Geneva's weather compared to Seoul's this time of year — the kind of neutral conversation two people keep up once they've already said what actually mattered, and neither wants the whole dinner to feel like a transaction. Voss didn't bring up the case again. Neither did Park Taeho. It was, strangely enough, the part of the evening Voss enjoyed most — not because he forgot why he was there, but because for a few minutes he could pretend, with Park Taeho's silent complicity, that he wasn't.

They said goodbye at the hotel entrance without a handshake or any formal promise, only a brief nod that, two days later, in that same room, would translate into a question asked at the exact moment it was needed most.

They didn't talk much more after that. Voss left with what he'd come for, and with the familiar, uncomfortable certainty that every favor collected this way was also a debt logged somewhere, one that sooner or later someone was going to want collected back.

---

The session, two days later, wasn't what Voss had come to win. It was, at best, what he'd come to avoid losing.

Furrer arrived with the same meticulous notes as always, and this time he didn't stop at pointing out the angle Voss already knew he'd find — the personal insistence on the case, the speed with which he'd offered to travel there in person. He took it a step further than Voss had anticipated.

"I'm formally proposing we open a conduct review into Evaluator Voss's handling of this case," Furrer said, without raising his voice, every word already drafted in advance. "Not the Valais group. Him. Six months personally documenting it from Valais Center, an insistence that's reached this room twice in one month, and a pattern this council should start looking at more closely: every Registry case documented in AMAT's historical record has, somewhere along its trajectory, Evaluator Voss's personal fingerprint — closer, more present, more involved than his position would require in any normal case. This council has a right to know whether what we're hearing is the judgment of a senior evaluator, or of someone who can no longer separate any case of this kind from himself."

The room went quieter than Voss had ever heard it. A conduct review wasn't a minor procedure — it meant weeks of an open file, colleagues summoned to testify, and in the worst case, the real possibility that Voss would be pulled off the entire case while it was resolved, leaving Oliver and the rest of the group with no one to defend them in that room.

"That's precisely the definition of a conflict of interest," Furrer said. "You just call it experience."

"Call it whatever you prefer," Voss said, his voice steady even though something inside him wasn't. "The result is the same: nobody in this room knows the case better than I do. Opening a conduct review doesn't change that. It only guarantees that, for weeks, nobody with that knowledge will be available to defend it."

"That's precisely the question this council needs to resolve," Furrer said. "If the case depends on your being available, the case already has a bigger problem than anything I could propose today."

It was Park Taeho who spoke up then, not defending Voss directly, but asking the question he'd promised to ask if the conversation reached this point.

"A simple question, Director Furrer," he said, turning toward him instead of Voss. "Does your proposal seek to protect the integrity of this process, or does it seek to settle, once and for all, whether you trust Evaluator Voss? Because those are two different questions, and this room only has real authority over the first."

Furrer didn't answer right away, and that silence, brief but real, was the first thing all session that had moved in Voss's favor.

"If responsibility for a case like this always falls in two places at once — the evaluator who recommends it and the institution that approves it," Park Taeho went on, not waiting for an answer, "then a conduct review adds nothing the normal oversight framework doesn't already cover. It adds time. And time, Director Furrer, is exactly what you yourself have spent weeks saying this case cannot afford to spend without justification."

Nobody in the room, not even Furrer, had any way of knowing the question had already been decided two days earlier.

The session president let a moment pass before speaking, weighing something that wasn't a formal vote yet.

"We're not opening a conduct review today," he said, finally. "The proposal stays on record, not dismissed. If a concrete element appears in the coming months — not just a general concern — this council will take it up again without anyone needing to propose it from scratch."

Furrer nodded once, not hiding that he accepted the partial defeat as exactly that — partial, not final.

"Let the record show the proposal stays alive," he said. "Not withdrawn."

"Noted," the session president said.

There was no vote on the status framework. There was no time, and no appetite in the room, to get there after what had just happened — and Voss, for the first time all session, felt something close to relief that it was so: pushing for anything more today, with the shadow of the conduct review still fresh on the table, would have meant leaning on a door that was already closing on its own.

"We'll return to the Valais group's status and the classification methodology at the next session," the president said. "Neither point moves forward today. That's not a no. It isn't a yes either."

Voss closed both folders without opening either. He'd come to gain ground for the case. He was leaving instead with the case exactly where it had been the last time he'd walked through that door, and with his own name now written into a review proposal that hadn't disappeared — only paused.

---

He walked out into the hallway with Cartwright a step behind, exactly like last time.

"Furrer went further than I expected," Cartwright said. "A conduct review isn't a bluff. That threat is serious, even if it didn't go anywhere today."

"I know," Voss said. "And it didn't go as badly as it could have. That wasn't luck."

Cartwright stopped mid-step.

"What did you do?"

"I talked to Park Taeho two nights ago," Voss said. "I asked him to hear the case seriously if someone tested him in session. Nothing more than that, technically. But I knew exactly what question he'd ask, and I knew that question would give the room something different from what Furrer was offering."

Cartwright said nothing for a moment, and when he spoke, there was something in his tone that wasn't quite reproach, but wasn't calm approval either.

"You didn't tell me."

"I didn't ask you because I didn't need you committing to something I didn't even know would work," Voss said. "If it had failed, I'd rather it failed with just me."

"That's not what I asked you," Cartwright said. "I asked why you didn't tell me."

Voss didn't answer right away, and the silence that followed carried a different weight than any other moment between them in the past weeks.

"Because the fewer people who know exactly how every piece moves in this city," Voss said finally, "the harder it is for whoever sent that man from the minor branch to figure out how to stop me next time."

That seemed to land differently. Cartwright started walking again, slower, and said nothing for several meters — the kind of silence Voss recognized as calculation, not offense: Cartwright reordering, in real time, how much of what he thought he knew about how Voss moved through this city was still true after tonight.

"How many more times have you done this?" he said finally. "Before today, I mean. Things you haven't told me."

"Fewer than you imagine," Voss said. "More than you'd like."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the honest answer," Voss said. "I don't keep an exact count, and if I did, I wouldn't give it to you in a hallway. What I will tell you is that I've never done it to hide something from you specifically. I do it because the fewer people who know the whole map, the fewer people can use it against me without my seeing it coming. You included, if you ever reached that point. I don't think you will. But I'm not arrogant enough to plan my moves assuming it could never happen."

"The president's comment, at the start," he said. "That the guest hadn't asked for access again."

"I thought about it at the time," Voss said. "I still don't know if it means he lost interest or someone told him to stop trying. But after today, I lean more toward the second. If I can move a piece without anyone in that room fully noticing, I'm not the only one who knows how."

Cartwright said nothing to that for a while, and when he finally spoke, there was something softer in his tone — not quite forgiveness, closer to a truce neither of them bothered declaring out loud.

"I don't like it," he said. "But I understand the logic. And for the record, I wouldn't have told you everything either, if the roles were reversed."

"I know," Voss said. "It's the only reason I trust you to keep walking beside me down this hallway instead of another one."

They reached the elevator. Voss pressed the button and waited, eyes fixed on the number counting down, floor by floor.

"Furrer's proposal is still alive," he said finally, more to himself than to Cartwright. "Paused, not dead. Next time he brings it, he won't have Park Taeho asking the right thing at the right moment by coincidence. I'm going to need more pieces like tonight's, and each one is going to cost more to get."

"Welcome to the big leagues," Cartwright said, with just enough irony to point out that Voss had been there thirty years and had only started playing like everyone else tonight.

"I've been playing in the big leagues longer than you, Cartwright," Voss said. "I just hadn't let you see the scoreboard until today."

The elevator doors opened. Voss stepped in. Cartwright stayed in the hallway, not following this time, and for a second it looked like he was going to say something more — but he only nodded once before the doors closed between them, leaving Voss with the certainty that surviving an attack wasn't the same as gaining ground, though in Geneva, more and more often, the two were starting to feel exactly as exhausting as each other.

---

The message arrived that same night, already back at the hotel, while Voss still had his tie on and hadn't decided whether to take it off.

A file access request, filed through Furrer's office, dated three days earlier — before the session, not after. That was the first thing Voss noticed, and the hardest to swallow: Furrer wasn't reacting to today. He'd spent at least three days preparing something he hadn't used yet.

The requested file didn't carry Oliver's name. It didn't carry Maren's name. It carried a thirty-year-old operation code, from an era when Voss still signed his reports as a field agent, not an evaluator — and a place Voss hadn't said out loud since, not even to himself.

He stood looking at the screen longer than he would have admitted to anyone.

Furrer hadn't asked for anything about it in session yet. Not yet.
