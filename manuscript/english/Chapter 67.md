# Chapter 67 — Momentum

Voss summoned him to the office in the north wing at nine o'clock sharp. No advance notice, no evaluation language in the message — just a time and a floor.

Oliver arrived exactly on time. The door was open.

The office wasn't large. A centered desk, two chairs facing it in an asymmetric arrangement — one set slightly closer, one slightly to the side. Oliver took the one to the side. Voss didn't look up right away: he finished reading something on the screen, closed it, and leaned back. The gesture had the economy of someone who didn't calibrate his posture to make an impression — he sat that way because it helped him think.

"Sit," Voss said, though Oliver already was.

Silence. Then:

"I want you to understand what we're building."

Oliver waited.

"Standard field logic," Voss continued, "works as a sum of capabilities. Each agent has a Domain, and the team gets built around that. Strength plus coverage plus range. It's effective." A beat. "It has a ceiling. The ceiling is visibility."

Oliver recognized the kind of opening Voss used when what came next needed to be placed first.

"Park can operate alone with considerable tactical precision," Voss said. "Three years in the field, unsupported response missions, documented results. The problem isn't his capability. The problem is that there's a limit to how much information an active agent can process while he's still moving. Park sees what's in front of him. He can't simultaneously read what's behind him unless it announces itself, or what's building up in the environment before anyone's decided to act."

Another pause. Shorter.

"You change that math."

Oliver didn't answer.

"What the Registry gives us," Voss said, "is a layer of intelligence no field team has ever had. Not strength. Not extra coverage. Real-time information — which Domains are active, which are accumulating without manifesting, what the signature of something approaching looks like before it arrives. In an operation with multiple anomalous vectors, that isn't a marginal advantage."

Oliver processed the sentence. The structure was technically correct. What it didn't say was how much the cost of running it actually weighed.

"The question isn't whether you can do it," Voss said, as if he'd read the silence. "The question is under what conditions, and for how long."

Oliver recognized his turn.

"Reading multiple active signatures at once saturates me," he said. "I lose precision starting around the eighth or tenth minute, depending on the number of signals. With advance information about what to look for, I can choose my focus. Without it, under variable pressure, the threshold drops. I don't know exactly by how much."

"I know," Voss said. "Maren sends me updates every three days." A pause. "How would you describe the current state of selective attention?"

Oliver thought before answering.

"It works when I have time to choose. It still doesn't work when the environment chooses for me."

Voss nodded.

"Then that's what's left."

It wasn't an evaluation. It was the precise delimitation of an unsolved problem — the same tone he'd use to flag a component in a circuit that still failed under load.

"The team I'm building," Voss said, "can't depend on an intelligence layer that saturates in the field at eight minutes. You need to be able to operate longer without the threshold working against you. Not indefinitely. Just long enough for the information to reach them when they need it."

Oliver listened. He didn't answer.

The silence had a different texture than the one before — as if Voss were calculating how much more to give. The conversation had been generous by his standards, and that generosity was itself information: Voss needed Oliver to understand, not just to obey.

"There's something else," Voss said. "The evaluation protocol has a deadline. Brenner wants a deployment report before it expires. What I'm building here needs more time than that report is going to grant easily." A pause. "What you do over the next few weeks factors into that argument."

Oliver weighed the sentence. It wasn't a threat. It was operational context — the kind Voss gave when a piece mattered to someone else's decisions.

"What part aren't you telling me?" Oliver asked.

Voss looked at him for a moment.

"The part I haven't finished calculating yet." Then, with no further pause: "Twelve minutes. If you can hold functional reading for twelve continuous minutes under load, I can work with that." He said it the way you state a technical specification. "For now."

Oliver didn't answer right away.

Twelve continuous minutes under load was a number. But twelve continuous minutes wasn't the operation Voss was describing — not if selective attention worked the way Oliver believed it could. Twelve minutes distributed, with gaps where he read only what mattered instead of everything, added up to a different total in the field. Thirty minutes of operation. Maybe forty. Functional in a way no continuous number described accurately.

Voss said none of that. Neither did Oliver.

"Understood," Oliver said.

The conversation ended without a marker. Voss went back to the screen. Oliver stood and left.

He stopped for a moment in the corridor.

Twelve minutes was a very specific number to be arbitrary.

---

What happened that afternoon wasn't planned.

Oliver went to the gym at four because he needed to move, and because the Odem control exercise he'd been practicing for weeks worked better after sitting still too long. He didn't expect to find anyone there.

Park was in the back, without gear, in the open space between the mats and the east wall. He wasn't warming up — he'd already been at it a while. Slow strikes at deliberate speed against the bag, interspersed with static positions Oliver recognized from the fight against Yusuf: weight transitions, anchor points, redistribution from the center to the periphery and back.

Oliver read him almost before he decided to.

Park's signature was unlike anything he'd processed before. Asha was tension — a field that tightened inward or slackened. Riku was frequency — noise that modulated or didn't. Park's signature was neither a field nor a frequency. It was accumulation. Kinetic potential organized inward, with no outlet, layer over layer of something that kept growing and never manifested unless Park chose to let it. Park could be completely still — as now, in the transition between the bag and the static position — and the Odem signature kept climbing.

Oliver walked into the gym without saying anything and found a space to the side. He began the usual warm-up sequence. He didn't look directly at Park.

He didn't stop reading.

The first condition met itself: Park's Domain was active continuously, as a baseline state, not as a response to technique. Oliver let the exposure accumulate without forcing it — no deliberate attention yet, just sustained presence.

Seven minutes later, something changed.

It wasn't gradual. It was a point — the pattern crossed from readable signature into something that started charging inside him, and the difference from Static or Tension was immediate and completely distinct. Static had been adding noise to the field. Tension had been learning to feel a muscle that hadn't existed before. This was as if the weight of his own body increased half a second after every movement — a lag in his own momentum, a tenth of a second where his body didn't respond the way he asked it to, as if the momentum itself needed time to recognize it had changed direction.

Oliver didn't stop.

The first instinct was to stop. He didn't. He changed the rhythm — slower, more deliberate, movements where the lag would matter less — and kept going.

The load was physical. That was the first thing he noticed clearly: it wasn't perception, it wasn't a reading, it wasn't the weight the Registry usually produced behind his eyes. This was his body. His legs answered late. His arms took too long to complete the arc. The momentum he generated with each movement seemed to hang suspended for an instant before showing itself, as if his body needed to resolve a calculation that wouldn't quite close.

And while he processed that, he processed something else too: this was probably what Park carried every time the Domain was active. Not the Domain's effect — the cost of carrying it. The continuous accumulation of kinetic potential the body had to manage without releasing. That was why he trained this way, with that methodology of slow transitions and static positions that had struck Oliver as almost excessive. It wasn't to improve technique. It was to build tolerance for the load the Domain produced simply by existing.

Oliver kept moving.

Ten minutes. Twelve. The sensation didn't dissipate — it layered. Each layer of movement added something to the lag until it was no longer just delay but accumulated pressure, like a spring wound tight without ever reaching the point of release. There was a threshold there, and Oliver was grazing it without knowing exactly where it stood.

At fifteen minutes he went to the wall.

He braced against the surface with a flat palm, and that was when he saw it.

His vision clouded for a second — not gradual, but as if the contrast of everything dropped at once — and when it cleared, he looked at his own hand.

It was vibrating.

It wasn't muscle tremor. It wasn't the kind of shaking exhaustion produces. There was something unnatural in the frequency — the hand still against the wall, and yet with a movement in it that had no business being there. The momentum accumulated over the last fifteen minutes, charged into the tissue, with no channel, no direction.

He didn't know how to release it.

Static dissipated on its own with time. Tension relaxed if he stopped holding it. This wasn't that — this was mass in motion that hadn't finished moving. He searched for the mechanism and didn't find it. The Registry had no instruction for this.

He felt something he recognized as fear before he had time to name it.

"Go back to the start," Park said. "One step. Slow."

He said it from the far end of the gym, without turning his body, watching the mirror. It wasn't a warning or an alarm — it was the tone of someone recognizing something he'd already seen before and knew had a way out. The only words he directed at Oliver during the entire session.

The fear stepped back a notch. Oliver pushed his hand against the wall, shifted his weight to his right leg, took one step forward. Slow.

The vibration dissolved.

Not all at once. Like water finding a channel. The lag normalized. The weight became his own weight again. Oliver stood still a moment longer, his hand still on the wall though it wasn't needed anymore.

In the mirror, Park had gone back to the bag. The angle of his work was the same as before.

Oliver left at quarter to five. He didn't say anything. Neither did Park.

In the upper corridor, which looked into the gym through a supervision window, Voss stood with his hands behind his back. He wasn't looking at Oliver. He was looking at Park.

Oliver saw him as he passed. Voss didn't turn his head.

Oliver kept walking.

---

Back in his room, he sat on the floor because the desk chair was too rigid for what he needed.

He took out the notebook.

It wasn't a diary. It was an undated notebook with technical entries: observations of signatures, the Registry's reactions under different conditions, patterns from group training he wanted to be able to recover. Maren knew it existed. She hadn't asked to see it. Oliver hadn't offered it either.

He opened to a blank page and wrote:

*Static (Riku). Conditions 0–3 complete. The modulation that came on its own that first deliberate session isn't exactly Riku's — the contours touch but aren't the same. Practicable. Low cost in sustained use. I can use it without losing simultaneous reading, which is more than I can say for Tension. Pending: separate more clearly what part is the copy and what part is already mine.*

*Tension (Asha). Clean deliberate copy, three weeks ago. The residue from the first involuntary exposure — that session when Asha didn't know I was reading her — is degrading. I think the clean copy absorbed it, or it simply stopped having coherent structure. The cost of the first activation was real. Every use since drops, but never reaches zero. There's something in Tension the body never fully normalizes.*

*Impulse (Nadia). Conditions 0–3 complete — joint sessions with Maren, last three weeks. The structure is direct: straight-line force, origin at the hip. Practicable under controlled conditions. Moderate cost — above Static, below Tension. Requires clear line of sight and defined distance. No use at direct contact.*

*Anchor (Sabine). Conditions 0–3 complete — also in sessions with Maren. Dense signature, localized, digs into a specific target instead of expanding as a field. The cost is higher than I expected — Sabine uses it with a lot of precision and low frequency, and I think there's a reason for that. Still don't know how long I can hold it before the threshold starts moving.*

*Strength/Momentum (Park). Today: conditions 0–3 complete. The passive accumulation is in me now — I felt it for fifteen minutes, layering, until I didn't know what to do with it. Park saw the problem. He said "go back to the start." It worked. What's left: learning to release it in a controlled way. And then using it on something that isn't a hallway.*

He closed the notebook.

Selective attention was the knot Voss had named that morning without calling it by that name. The same one Maren had been documenting since winter without either of them knowing exactly how to solve it. The difference between seeing without reading and reading without control was, in practice, the difference between being a useful intelligence layer in the field and being an asset with a time limit that no one had precisely calculated yet.

The prerequisite Park had mentioned in the alpine courtyard, weeks earlier: know where your own Odem is.

Oliver set the notebook aside.

He turned off the desk lamp. Not entirely — he left the small lamp at the back on, the one that threw sideways light without lighting up the whole room. Enough to see posture, not enough to see anyone's Odem.

What Park and Yusuf had done in the opening exchanges of the demonstration fight — before the Domain, before Yusuf's signature changed in the ninth round — was redistribution of Odem inside the body itself. Concentrating the substrate at the points of contact before impact, moving it from the center to the periphery or from the periphery back to the core depending on what came. It wasn't Yusuf's Domain. It was its prerequisite. The base technique the Domain amplified, but that worked without it too.

Oliver had read it in the gym from the first day Yusuf trained in front of him. He knew the sequence: anchor at the center, extend to the point of contact, contract. He had seen it in Park in the early rounds. He had seen it in Yusuf before he activated the Domain in round nine.

He closed his eyes.

The Registry shut down almost completely without vision. What was left was internal — his own Odem, with no outside signature to compare against, no external signal to read. That, at least, was no longer the problem.

He found it without searching: there, at the center, the familiar density. Weeks of sessions with Maren had made the location immediate, almost automatic. Moving it wasn't hard either — he could shift it from the center to his hand, from his hand to his shoulder, feel the trace it left behind.

What he couldn't do was what he'd seen in the fight between Park and Yusuf.

In the early rounds, before Yusuf activated the Domain, the two of them had been redistributing Odem with a precision Oliver had read as overlapping patterns: not a single mass moving from one place to another, but simultaneous distribution across several points, each held independently, adjusting in real time to impact. Anchor at the center. Extend to the point of contact. Contract. And all of it while they moved, while they attacked and took hits, without the technique interrupting either one.

Oliver could do the first part. He could locate and move.

He couldn't hold two points at once.

He tried: center and right hand simultaneously. The center held. The hand arrived, and the center gave — it didn't disappear, but it lost density, as if the system didn't have enough for both sites at once.

He went back to the center. Stabilized.

He tried again.
