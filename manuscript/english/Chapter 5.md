# Chapter 5 — The Webbs


Gary Webb paid off the three thousand five hundred dollars in twenty-three months, one month ahead of the deadline he'd proposed himself, and for those twenty-three months Farid stopped thinking about him almost entirely. He was, in the most literal sense of the word, a good client: punctual, no excuses, needing no one to remind him of anything. Farid crossed paths with him on the street a couple of times, on his way somewhere, and both times settled for a nod, which Gary returned with a warmth Farid found, every time, faintly unsettling — as if the man hadn't fully understood that a paid-off loan wasn't grounds for friendship, only for a correctly closed account.

The shop, meanwhile, existed on the edge of what Farid considered his city: a small storefront in Shelter 9, with a hand-painted sign — GARY'S REPAIR, it said, with no ambition beyond being legible from the sidewalk — that Farid watched grow, literally, when Gary came back a year later to borrow, this time not money, but a contact who could get him cheaper storefront glass. Farid gave it to him. It wasn't generosity. It was the kind of small gesture that kept a good client feeling well treated, without costing Farid anything real.

Gary's reputation as the man who brought dead things back to life spread fast through Breakwater. He fixed the Baptist church's radio for free — the pastor was old, and that radio was, by the pastor's own account, the only real company he had between sermons — and in the weeks that followed, that single gesture brought him more clients than any paid ad could have. Farid heard about it in passing, from a client who mentioned Gary's name with a respect Farid found, in his own line of work, increasingly rare to earn.

What Farid didn't see — because he had no reason to be looking, and because the shop of a man who'd already paid him off was none of his business — was what happened inside those four walls during the years the business, against every reasonable expectation for a man with no training and no capital, actually worked. Gary had honest hands and infinite patience with broken things, and Breakwater had, in abundance, broken things and people with no money to replace them. By the time his son turned three, the shop was supporting the whole family, no help from anyone. Gary brought him to the shop some afternoons, set him on a low stool with an old disassembled radio in front of him — not because the boy could do anything useful with it yet, but because he liked watching him study the parts with a focus that didn't seem normal in something so small.

Farid walked past the shop once, on his way to collect something two streets over, and stopped just long enough to see, through the window, the boy with the disassembled radio in front of him, the parts lined up in a perfect row on the counter — by size, not by the order he'd pulled them out in. He thought, without giving it much weight, that the kid would go far if someone bothered to teach him the trade properly someday. He kept walking. He never thought about it again.

---

The illness came, the way almost all of them do, without enough warning to prepare for it. One diagnosis, then another confirming it, then a word — oncologist — neither of them had ever had reason to learn before. Gary closed the shop three days that first week, something he hadn't done once in all the years before, not for the flu, not for someone else's funeral.

Farid found out months later, and secondhand: Gary came back to Dunmore, the same folder as always, a little more worn, asking this time for one thousand eight hundred dollars.

"The rent on the shop, in the meantime," he said — no more detail than necessary, in the voice of a man who'd learned to explain only the bare minimum, so as not to have to feel the whole explanation. "And some expenses I can't cover with what's coming in right now."

Farid didn't ask what expenses. He didn't need to, and he wasn't interested enough to bother. He looked at the figure, looked at Gary's face — thinner now, with days of stubble the Gary from three years back would never have let himself wear to anyone's counter while asking for money — and approved the loan on the same reasonable terms as always, without adjusting for whatever was happening in that house in Shelter 9. A loan was a loan. Whatever compassion he felt, if he felt any, played no part in the transaction.

Outside, through the Dunmore window, Farid caught sight of the boy sitting on the curb, knees pulled to his chest, waiting however long it took without complaint. Five years old, he guessed, maybe six. He said nothing about it. It wasn't his business how a man arranged his son around an illness that wasn't his business either.

Gary signed, thanked him with less warmth than the first time — not from rudeness, but because he no longer had much extra to spare — and left.

---

What Farid didn't know — and had no path he cared to take toward knowing — was what happened in that house over the next two years. The nights Gary closed the shop at five to sit with her, then opened it again at nine, after putting the boy to bed, to get ahead on backed-up repairs by the light of a single lamp. The afternoons the boy — six by then — sat with her on the bed, unasked, describing everything he'd seen that day in unsettling detail, down to the exact route the mailman had taken, as if he needed to prove to himself that a whole day's memory could hold together, unbroken, against something that was taking pieces out of hers. No one in that house would have called it a gift. They'd have called it, if anything, the way the boy dealt with fear — a strange kid, the neighbors said, no malice in him, just an adult's memory and the habit of looking at things as if he were taking them apart in his head before he ever touched them.

She died in the spring, two years after Gary's first visit to Dunmore for the second loan. Farid heard it from Silas, who'd heard it in turn from a client who lived near Shelter 9 — the kind of news that travels through a small neighborhood without anyone having to announce it formally.

"That's a shame," Farid said, and it was, in the generic, correct sense that any early death is a shame to anyone who mentions it in passing. He felt no need to do anything with that shame. That same afternoon, he checked the state of Gary Webb's account, confirmed the payments were two months behind, and decided, without much internal debate, to let it slide a little further than he would have let it slide for anyone else. Not charity, exactly. Something closer to the patience a man grants a clock he knows, from experience, will start running again if you just give it time.

---

In the house in Shelter 9, the first months after were quiet in a way neither of them could fully name. Gary talked less. The boy talked even less, not for lack of words but because he'd learned, in his mother's last weeks, that words sometimes only filled the time it took for something to finish hurting. Some nights they sat together in the shop after closing, Gary taking apart something that didn't really need fixing, just to keep his hands busy, the boy watching from the same low stool as always, too big for him by now.

The shop never went back to what it had been. Not all at once — Gary kept opening, kept fixing radios and irons and the broken things Breakwater kept producing with the same steady regularity as ever — but something in the rhythm had broken in a way no tool in that shop could touch. He started closing late, or not opening at all some Mondays. The payments, increasingly irregular, ate through the margin that had once set him apart from any other client. Farid extended the term twice, not out of affection, but because a client in slow collapse was worth more, by careful calculation, than one shut down all at once. The interest kept running, indifferent to the reason behind each delay, adding month after month to a figure that, by then, neither of them checked very often.

The boy grew up inside that slow decline without anyone, not even his father, ever stopping to explain it to him in those words. He learned to fix things before anyone formally taught him — he'd take apart broken toys other kids would have thrown out, and hand them back working, with a precision Gary found equal parts proud and unsettling. He almost never failed, and when he did, he never left the attempt half-finished: he'd take the whole piece apart again, twice, three times, until he found exactly where he'd gone wrong, with a patience that didn't seem to cost him any effort at all, only time.

He also learned, without anyone calling it that, to keep books: by ten, he already knew what came in and went out of the shop every week better than his own father did, since Gary preferred not to look closely at numbers when the numbers had nothing good to say. He started, without Gary asking or fully noticing, sorting the receipts into a separate box, by date, because disorder produced a physical discomfort in him that he couldn't explain any better than saying things out of place "felt wrong."

It was the boy himself, not Gary, who found the folder of bank papers and Dunmore papers one day while looking for a tool in the wrong drawer. He was thirteen. He read every figure twice, sorted the dates in his head with no need for paper to write them on, and understood, with a clarity no adult in his life had ever bothered to give him, that the numbers hadn't added up in years — that every month the shop stayed open was, in terms his father would never have said out loud, one more month of loss dressed up as hope.

He said nothing that afternoon. He put the folder back exactly where he'd found it, the same dust on top, in the same order. He lay awake afterward, in his room, running through from memory the figures he'd seen only once — the same memory his mother had tested years before, describing the whole world to him so she wouldn't lose her own — and reached a conclusion he shared with his father neither that night nor any other: that the debt wasn't going to disappear on its own, and that his father, for reasons the boy wasn't yet old enough to fully name, had stopped trying to make it disappear.

---

Farid never learned the boy's name until long after he'd stopped being, in any useful sense, a boy. He'd seen him, maybe, two or three times over the years — a small shape sitting in a corner of the shop, or waiting on the curb — and none of those times left the kind of mark Farid reserved for people actually worth remembering.

The only time they spoke directly was an ordinary Saturday, when the boy — twelve, maybe thirteen, already almost as tall as his father — came with Gary to renew the loan term once again. He stood by the counter while the adults talked figures, studying the safe behind Farid with a curiosity too precise to be simple childhood boredom.

"How much does that weigh?" he asked, with no one having given him any reason to speak.

Farid, who didn't usually answer children's questions in the middle of a negotiation, surprised himself by answering anyway. "More than you could carry."

"I wasn't asking if I could carry it," the boy said, without a trace of insolence, with the same bored precision he might have used to correct a bad calculation. "I was asking how much it weighs."

Gary apologized for him. Farid said it wasn't necessary, closed the deal, and didn't think about the question again for years — not until much later, when it no longer made any sense to wonder why a thirteen-year-old wanted to know the exact weight of a safe he'd never get to open.

To Farid, Gary Webb's son was, at most, one more detail in an account that had gone years without standing out from the pile.

By the time the shop closed, the figure in Farid's notebook — the second loan, never fully repaid, plus the interest piled up across the years of delays — stood at around six thousand dollars. It wasn't a fortune, by Farid's standards; he'd been lending far larger sums for years without blinking. For a shop that no longer brought in any income, it was a mountain no one in that house knew what to do with.

The shop closed, quietly, with no warning and no formal letter, sometime in the months after that thirteenth birthday. One day the shutters were up. The next, and from then on, they weren't. Farid ruled out collecting the rest through the usual channels, waited the reasonable amount of time, and filed Gary Webb's account under the mental category he reserved for debts that would, someday, when there really was no other option left, end up in his black notebook like so many others.

He didn't know — he had no reason to know — that someone else had already decided, long before he had, that this account was never going to reach that page. That the boy who'd once asked him how much a safe weighed had already spent months thinking, with the same precision he used to sort receipts and take apart radios, about exactly how he meant to stop it.
