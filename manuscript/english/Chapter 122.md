# Chapter 122 — What Buys Silence

---

The heat arrived before the ground did, same as always. Daniel felt it seep through the helicopter door seconds before the wheels touched the same strip of packed earth as the last few times, the one someone, somewhere along the chain of favors holding all this together, still called a runway.

"Back so soon, Mr. Ashworth," said the usual man, stepping forward as the blades started losing power, the smile genuine this time, not the one practiced in a mirror. "We kept your usual spot in the shade."

"Appreciated," Daniel said, climbing down in the same light suit he wore in Geneva and in any room where his name opened a door without anyone having to ask why.

No tower, no lights, no one in a reflective vest. The same four men as always, one of them holding out a case of sodas before Daniel got around to asking. Four visits in four years were enough for them to stop treating him like a visit and start treating him like something closer to a good client who pays well and causes no trouble.

"How's the other settlement?" Daniel asked, as they walked toward the truck. "The one up north."

"Fine. Word came that the school finally has a roof."

Daniel nodded, filing the detail away with the same care he filed all the others. There were five sites like this one spread across three countries, each built with the same forty years of patience. He didn't need to visit them all with the same frequency. This one, by far, interested him most right now — not only for what Kaspar had built here, but for what the mountain, without Kaspar ever asking for it, had put within reach.

---

The village appeared after forty minutes on a road with no pavement, the same mist as always climbing the hillside. Before they reached the clinic, Daniel saw a young woman cross the square without coming any closer, changing course just enough not to have to greet anyone. A somewhat younger man, his left arm still immobilized against his chest, watched her pass without trying to stop her this time — he seemed to have learned, at some point over the last few days, that insisting changed nothing.

Kaspar was waiting on his feet by the clinic with no sign, alone, with the kind of stillness that isn't learned — it's inherited from something, or paid for with something, and Daniel had spent four visits without deciding which of the two it was.

Nothing in Kaspar's face changed at the sight of him. That, more than anything else, was what Daniel found genuinely interesting about him: the total absence of surprise, in a man who'd spent forty years as the one person with real reasons to suspect everything that came near him. A man in his sixties with the face of thirty-five, with no surgery in the world capable of leaving that particular kind of trace, without a single drop of sweat despite the same heat that had already soaked through Daniel's shirt twice since he stepped off the helicopter. AMAT had gone four decades without finding him. Daniel understood exactly why — not because Kaspar hid well, but because no one, looking at him standing there, so calm, would think to look for what was actually there.

"Daniel," Kaspar said, the same tone as always, neither warm nor cold.

"Kaspar." Daniel didn't offer his hand; they had enough history by now to skip that part. "How's everything here?"

"Still standing. That's the only thing I can promise you for sure at this point."

"I heard you had a problem recently."

Kaspar took a second before answering, looking toward where the woman had disappeared among the houses.

"Someone came looking for what they shouldn't have. It was difficult," he said, without elaborating more than strictly necessary. "But it got resolved with fewer complications than it could have had."

"And her?" Daniel asked, without needing to point.

"She didn't take it well. I doubt she ever will," Kaspar said, and something in his voice, for the first time all visit, sounded like something he hadn't decided to show on purpose. "Come in. The coffee should be ready."

---

They sat in the one room of the clinic with a door that closed all the way. Kaspar poured coffee neither of them drank, out of the same habit as always.

"Hesperia first," Daniel said. "The second round of funding cleared this week. The chief engineer is still complaining about the cement supply, but that's not my problem anymore, it's yours now."

"How many of the ones I've sent you are there already?"

"Enough that the place doesn't feel empty. Not so many that anyone outside would notice looking from a satellite." Daniel let a second pass. "That balance is the most expensive part of the whole project, and I don't mean the money."

Kaspar nodded, without further comment, and for a moment the silence settled between them with the comfort of something rehearsed many times already.

"Vandenberg left last year with a health problem that didn't add up to anyone," Daniel said, almost in passing, pouring himself a bit more of a coffee he had no intention of drinking. "Aoki got an offer from somewhere else that he shouldn't have been able to turn down so fast. And now Roswell decides, out of nowhere, not to run for re-election, right when the rest of the board was starting to lean my way."

"Things sometimes resolve themselves," Kaspar said, "when someone really wants them resolved."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Kaspar said, and something like a smile crossed his face, brief, never quite settling. "I just read the news, like everyone else."

Daniel didn't press. He didn't need to. There was a satisfaction on the table that neither of them was going to name out loud — Kaspar with the quiet certainty of someone who's just reminded another person exactly how much they're worth, Daniel with the patience of someone letting that certainty grow a little more before he needed it.

---

"A few months ago I started going through the Fund's books more carefully than I probably should," Kaspar said. "Four years of doing business with someone is enough time to trust them, but not enough to stop watching. I found a line that didn't square with the rest of it — a code name, in the internal reports that never make it to the public accounts. *Hunger.*"

Daniel said nothing.

"It didn't mean anything the first time I saw it," Kaspar went on. "It means a good deal more now, after what I found in the hills."

Daniel leaned forward slightly over the table, the gesture of someone deciding to weigh every sentence before letting it go.

"It's an old relationship. The Fund's, not mine directly."

"That's not what I asked you."

"I know," Daniel said. "It's what I can give you while I decide how much more."

"Start with something, then."

"It started small. About ten years ago."

"How small?"

"A girl," Daniel said. "Newborn. Someone decided she'd serve for something bigger than raising her."

Kaspar said nothing, waiting for the rest with the same patience he applied to everything.

"Animals, at first," Daniel went on, each sentence shorter than the last, as if giving up less at a time were the only way to keep giving up anything at all. "Then it stopped being enough."

"And then?"

"People no one was going to miss. The disappeared, the kind gang violence leaves loose. Easy to turn into something else."

"Who ran it?"

"A general."

"Does he still run it?"

"No," Daniel said. "He had a problem a few months ago. Cost him his life, and several of his people's."

"And the girl?"

"Still loose. If she's still that, she's still loose," Daniel said. "What's left of the general's people keeps feeding her, in the shadows, with no one left to tell them when to stop."

Kaspar looked at him for a long moment.

"That's a lot of detail for someone who says it never touched him directly."

"It is," Daniel said. "I never said it was little."

"You also didn't say whether you're the one who started it."

"No," Daniel said. "I didn't."

They stayed like that a moment, neither one rushing the other.

"It's in this village's hills, isn't it," Kaspar said, finally. "What you're calling that project."

"Yes."

"Then I know as much as you do, in practice. I was just missing the name."

"I'm not going to tell you yet how much of that is mine and how much I inherited from people who aren't around anymore to ask," Daniel said. "That's not yours to know yet, and it's not only for your sake that I'm saying so."

"That's not a no."

"No," Daniel said. "It isn't. But it's not a full yes either, and I'm going to ask you not to confuse the two just yet."

Kaspar looked at him for a long moment, the kind of look Daniel imagined preceded, for a lot of people who'd received it before him, something that didn't end well.

"I can wait," Kaspar said, finally. "I've spent forty years proving I know how."

"I know," Daniel said. "It's one of the reasons we're still partners, and not just someone who asks and someone who decides how much to give."

---

They spent the rest of the afternoon without talking business. Kaspar took him to see the new generator, proud in a way that had nothing to do with the money it had cost, and afterward they sat in the clinic's back patio eating something Marlene had left ready before heading out — arepas stuffed with whatever there was, real coffee this time, served without the ceremony of the earlier meeting. Kaspar talked about the region's coffee harvest, about a boy who'd learned to read that year thanks to the teacher the village no longer remembered not having, about nothing that resembled Hesperia or Hunger or the forty years he'd spent being someone else. Daniel listened most of the time, and when he spoke it was about things just as small — a delayed flight, a boring meeting in Geneva — with the same ease of someone who also knows, when it suits him, how to let the weight of everything else rest.

---

Kaspar walked him back to the truck as the sun lost what little strength it had left.

"Before you go," Kaspar said. "The accountant. The one I sent you three years ago, from Geneva. Still interested in how he's doing?"

Daniel stopped halfway.

"How is he?"

"Fine. Cooks for the second settlement. No one's come looking for him again."

"Good," Daniel said, and something in the exact way he said it didn't sound like a donor running down a list. It sounded like something else, closer to genuine relief, the kind that isn't practiced in a mirror before it's shown.

"Why that one, out of everyone who's come through here in four years?"

"It's not just him," Daniel said. "I remember all of them. Every one — which settlement they ended up in, whether they're still alive, what name they go by now that isn't theirs anymore. I don't keep it written down anywhere. I keep it the old way, because what gets written down can get lost, and this can't."

Kaspar said nothing for a long moment.

"That doesn't sound like philanthropy either," he said, finally.

"No," Daniel said. "But it's the only thing I have to offer you that resembles a real promise."

Daniel climbed into the truck without another word. Kaspar stood by the clinic, watching the lights recede toward the clearing where the helicopter waited, not quite coming close enough to see him off — the same distance he always kept, neither hostile nor warm, measured with the same care he applied to everything else in his life.

The helicopter took off, leaving behind a village, a hillside holding something neither of them had finished naming out loud, and Kaspar, standing there, with the satisfied certainty of having finally collected something real for four years of patience — the same certainty, Daniel thought as the village shrank beneath the helicopter until it disappeared, that Kaspar probably felt every time he convinced someone new that he'd finally found someone who saw him as a person and not a resource.

Daniel waited until the village lights disappeared completely under the clouds before letting himself smile — a small, private gesture, meant for no one, the kind that needs no witness to mean exactly what it means.
