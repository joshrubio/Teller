# Chapter 95 — Val Tremola

---

Val Tremola didn't resemble any terrain they'd used before — a real valley, not a groomed clearing inside St. Gotthard's secure perimeter, wooded slopes rising on both sides and a stream cutting across the bottom at an angle, wide enough to be a problem and narrow enough not to be a real barrier. The fog hadn't finished lifting when the two groups arrived, and for a while the only thing anyone could make out clearly was everyone's breath condensing at the same rhythm, twenty-odd people standing in silence, waiting.

Oliver saw them before Reber started talking — Dax's whole group, lined up on the other side of the clearing, and among them, with no one needing to point them out, the faces he already knew from a storeroom with no official name. Yegor. Milan. The other two, still nameless to the rest of the group, but not to Oliver. Beside him, he felt Riku go rigid the exact second he recognized them too.

"This isn't a standard field exercise," Reber said, standing between the two groups, his voice cutting through the fog with its usual dryness. "It's direct confrontation. Two flags, one per side, hidden on home ground. You win by capturing the other team's and bringing it back. Domains allowed, same limits as always — nothing that puts someone out of the fight for real. Everything else is fair game."

Nobody asked what happened if a team reached "everything else." The phrase already carried its own weight without needing clarification.

"The board records the result like any other field test," Reber went on. "But this isn't just points. It's the first time we're putting you up against specific people, for real, not an abstract exercise. Make use of that or don't. Makes no difference to me which you choose."

The teams were already decided before anyone asked: Valais against the whole of Dax's group, no exceptions this time — which meant, though nobody said it out loud, Valais against Yegor, Milan, Lukas, and Rasmus too. Oliver looked for Dax among the rest of the enemy group and found him already looking back, unhurried, with the same evaluating calm he'd been aiming at him for weeks.

"I know what you're thinking," Reber said, as if he'd read every face at once. "It's not an accident. Learn to compete against people you don't like. It's a skill, not a luxury."

---

They gathered apart, at the edge of the woods, before the start signal.

"We lose if we play their game," Asha said, no preamble. "Physically, they outmatch us in almost every one-on-one matchup. If this turns into a series of individual fights, we lose the flag and probably something more."

"Then we don't play that game," Petra said. "We play ours."

They laid out what Asha had gathered from her digging days earlier like it was tactical intelligence, not gossip: Milan, strong but predictable, old resentment that made him impatient. Lukas, all weight and no finesse. Rasmus, the most patient of the four, the one who waited for someone else's mistake instead of forcing it. Yegor, the only one who could really improvise if something went wrong.

"None of us wins a clean physical matchup against any of them," Asha said. "So none of us is going to try. We decide where they fight, not fight where they want."

What came out of that wasn't an attack plan. It was a map of who neutralized whom, and how, without anyone needing to win a fair fight to do it.

"Asha, you've got Milan," Petra said, calling out the assignments loud enough for everyone to hear. "Impatient, he'll go straight in. Give him a reason not to arrive."

"Dara, Rasmus," Asha continued. "He waits. Don't give him anything to wait for."

"I've got Lukas," Nadia said. "All body, no head. I don't need to beat him, I just need the ground to do its part."

"Dax is mine and Pier's," Petra said. "Between the two of us, with luck, he never gets close enough to see the flag."

That left Yegor, and it left Oliver, and nobody said out loud that the two of them had already met before, somewhere with no official name. Nobody needed to. Everyone in the group already knew.

"I'll take whatever falls to me," Oliver said, without asking to be assigned anywhere else.

"If this goes wrong," Dara said, already on her feet, adjusting her jacket, "at least it goes wrong as our mistake, not his."

Nobody argued it. They scattered to their positions when the signal sounded.

---

Asha found Milan exactly where she'd expected to — covering the east flank, the most direct route to Dax's flag, exactly the kind of position someone impatient would pick because it promised fast action. Milan saw her coming and smiled, already sure how this ended.

It didn't end that way. Asha didn't get close enough for his strength to matter. She applied Tension to the makeshift log bridge Milan had to cross to reach her, loosening the bindings just enough for it to give under his weight without fully breaking — Milan went down into the stream's shallow water, furious, soaked, and by the time he climbed out, Asha was no longer where he'd last seen her.

"Physically he would have wrecked me," she told Dara afterward, no particular satisfaction in her voice, just the report of a problem solved. "That's why I didn't let the fight be physical."

---

Nadia found Lukas planted in the middle of a narrow trail between rocks, the only usable route through that stretch, with the patience of someone who knows he doesn't need to move to win. He didn't move when Nadia got closer. He didn't need to — until she fired Impulse in a straight line, aimed at his chest, without touching him. Lukas didn't fall off the trail, but he stepped back half a pace, just enough for his own weight to do the rest on the loose gravel at the edge.

He recovered fast, faster than Nadia expected, and came at her again, head down, all charge and no technique. Nadia hit him again, aiming lower this time, at the knees — same straight line, same lack of curve, but this time enough to knock him off balance completely and send him sideways into the rock wall.

He didn't get up in time. Nadia was already past him.

"All weight and no finesse," Asha had told her before they split up. "You don't need to beat him. You just need the ground to do it for you."

She'd been right.

---

Dara found Rasmus exactly where Asha had predicted he'd be — still, watching, waiting for someone else's mistake instead of forcing his own. Against someone like that, patience didn't work. Dara activated the Double fifteen meters out, letting it cross the clearing with the exact same stride she would have used herself, and stayed back in the shadows while Rasmus moved to intercept it.

It took him three seconds to stop chasing the Double and turn toward where the real Dara should have been. Three seconds was enough.

By the time he found her, she'd already crossed.

---

Pier and Petra found Dax together, because neither of them, alone, had any real chance, and they'd both known it before they started.

Pier went first — not against Dax directly, but against the terrain he needed to cross. He increased the weight of the ground under every step, not enough to pin him down completely, just enough that every stride cost twice the effort it should. Dax noticed right away, irritation crossing his face an instant before he decided to ignore it and keep pushing forward anyway, brute strength making up for what the terrain was taking from him.

He almost made it. Petra didn't let "almost" count.

Every time Dax got close enough to see Valais's flag through the trees, Petra activated Swap — not against him directly, but between Dax and some already-scouted point on the ground, twenty or thirty meters back, comparable mass, direct sightline the second before the exchange. Dax reappeared where he'd been minutes earlier, having to cover the same stretch again, one Pier had already made heavier than the time before.

"This is exhausting," Dax said, the third time he found himself back at the start of the trail, not quite sounding like a complaint. It sounded more like an acknowledgment.

"That's the idea," Petra said, not moving any closer than she needed to keep her sightline.

The fifth time, Dax didn't charge straight back in. He calculated a different angle — shorter, more direct, cutting downhill instead of following the trail Pier already knew as well as he did — and for one real instant it looked like he might make it: two strides beyond where Petra could calculate the next Swap without losing her line of sight.

"I need backup here, now!" he shouted, without turning, without aiming it at anyone in particular, his voice carrying across the whole valley. He had no way of knowing who among his people was still free to answer. It wasn't a call to Yegor. It was a call to whoever.

It was Pier who stopped him in the end, not with weight this time, but by cutting out the one solid foothold he had left on the slope — the ground itself giving way under his planted foot at the exact moment. Dax went down on his knees, got up fast, but it was already too late: the shout had already crossed the whole valley, with no addressee, no guarantee anyone would answer.

They didn't beat him completely, not even then. But in the time it took him to get back up, the rest of the terrain finished deciding itself without him.

---

Dax saw it first. One of the times Petra sent him hurtling back to the start of the trail, the highest point of his whole back-and-forth route, he got one clear second of sight over the secondary approach where Yegor had planted himself alone — and over Oliver, closing on that same position without Yegor having noticed yet. He could have shouted a warning. He had the second for it, even with Pier already loading the ground back under his feet. He didn't. He kept watching a moment longer than necessary, then charged back into his own problem, as if whatever was about to happen to Yegor wasn't his concern, or as if it was, precisely, the kind of thing he preferred not to prevent.

Yegor saw Oliver before Oliver saw him, and in his head that was already an advantage worth keeping.

He'd pulled away from the rest of his group on purpose, covering a secondary approach nobody else had thought to watch — the kind of position a real ringleader picks when he'd rather settle things alone. He didn't know Dax had seen him, one full second, and decided not to shout anything. Oliver appeared through the trees minutes later, no visible backup, and Yegor felt something close to relief: finally a fight that needed no strategy, just body.

"You again," Yegor said, no real surprise in it. "Good. Nobody's blocking your way out of this one."

He didn't wait for an answer. A short jab, almost lazy, the kind of punch that isn't trying to connect — just measuring how fast Oliver reacted. Oliver shifted half a step left, just enough to let it graze past. Yegor nodded to himself, like he'd just confirmed something, and got serious: a straight hook that Oliver did block with his forearm, the impact hurting more than he expected, a physical reminder that this body carried weeks of training, not years.

Two more punches came behind that one, one to the side and another Oliver deflected by turning his shoulder at the last instant. Neither clean. Both meant for real.

Oliver didn't retreat once. Every punch that connected, he let it in instead of blocking it fully, absorbing the impact instead of stopping it, a step forward each time, closing distance instead of opening it. Yegor read it wrong — took it for clumsiness, thought Oliver didn't know how to cover himself. He picked up the pace, sure the next round would finish it.

That was the pattern Oliver had been hunting for three exchanges now: Yegor loaded his weight onto his back foot half a second before any punch meant to end the fight for real. The jab didn't have it. The hook did.

Oliver dropped his guard on the left side, just barely, a gap he shouldn't have let show.

Yegor took it. He loaded his weight back, his whole body behind a straight cross aimed at the gap — the punch that really meant to finish this.

It wasn't the punch Oliver was expecting to block. It was the one he was expecting to *use*.

He pivoted on his left foot, all the impact he'd absorbed over the last three punches releasing at once into the turn, his body moving faster than any muscle of his own, alone, would have allowed — the same principle he'd watched in Park over and over without fully understanding it until now: you don't stop someone else's force, you keep it and give it back on your own timing, not theirs. Yegor's cross swept through the space where Oliver no longer was. The turn left him at Yegor's right flank, his arm still extended, nothing behind it to defend with.

Oliver hit there. One short, dry punch, to the extended forearm, at the exact point where the bone ran closest to the skin. He remembered, with no real time to think about it, the one session Park had given him on this: *Don't spread it across the whole body. One point. The smaller, the worse for whoever's on the other end.*

Everything he'd been absorbing — three punches, plus half an hour of silent buildup before that — came out through that single fist, concentrated into an area smaller than a coin. Yegor's arm gave with a crack that wasn't bone, but close enough that the pain blanked his vision for a full second.

He didn't go all the way down. And he didn't stay still.

With his good arm still working, Yegor threw himself forward instead of back — not a punch, a full-body shove, the kind of desperate move nobody trains, aiming only to slam Oliver against the nearest trunk before he could reset. It half worked: Oliver felt the bark against his back, the air leaving his lungs all at once.

He had little left. Enough. One more shove, smaller than the last, barely the leftover of what he'd already spent, was enough to take away what little balance Yegor still had on one stable leg. Yegor stumbled backward, his broken arm knocking against his own side, a sound that tore a short, wordless shout out of him.

*I need backup here, now!*

Dax's voice crossed the whole valley, distant but unmistakable, naming no one — and neither of them had time to decide anything calmly. Yegor had no real obligation to answer a call that wasn't for him, with a useless arm and a fight already lost, but the reflex of his own captain's voice calling moved him anyway, a second before the reasonable part of his head caught up with the part that was already running. He held his arm against his chest and stumbled toward the trees, toward the shout, without looking back. Oliver didn't follow — not because he decided against it coolly, but because he was still catching the breath the trunk had knocked out of him.

By the time he could straighten up fully, Yegor was gone. What was left was his own trembling arm, half an hour of buildup spent all at once, and Dax's voice still echoing somewhere on ground he still had to find.

---

Riku was already close to Dax's flag when the shout crossed the valley — close enough that it reached him almost as loud as if Dax had been shouting right beside him. He didn't stop to think what it meant, or who it was for. He used it.

Everyone still standing on that ground turned their head half a second toward where the shout came from — a reflex, nobody decided to do it, it just happened — and that half second was the whole gap Riku needed. He crossed the last few meters while the rest of the valley still had its attention elsewhere, took the flag without anyone seeing it move, and was already running back when someone from Dax's group finally reacted and shouted that it was being taken.

Too late. By the time he crossed the finish line with the flag still in hand, the rest of the Valais group was arriving too, from different directions, out of breath, none of them entirely sure what had just happened in the last thirty seconds except that it had happened fast.

Dax arrived last, Pier and Petra still on his heels, nobody behind him having answered his own shout in time. He stood looking at the flag in Riku's hands with an expression that wasn't quite anger. It looked more like recalculating something.

"You beat us on strategy," he said, not as a complaint. "Not on strength. First time that's happened to me at St. Gotthard."

Nobody in the Valais group said anything. They didn't need to.

---

The hangar screens, that afternoon, showed the full board updated for the first time in weeks with an entire row of green arrows.

Asha climbed from #31 to #27 — four places. Dara from #156 to #149 — seven. Nadia, who had neutralized Lukas alone on the rock trail, climbed from #183 to #170 — thirteen places, proof that resolving a one-on-one matchup without help counted for just as much as anything else. Pier and Petra, who had held Dax between the two of them through the whole exercise without letting him reach the flag even once, climbed evenly — Pier from #47 to #42, five places; Petra from #82 to #75, seven. Riku, the flag still fresh in everyone's memory, climbed from #171 to #158 — thirteen as well, the exact same jump as Nadia, as if the board had decided that capturing and holding deserved equal weight.

Oliver climbed from #91 to #68 — twenty-three places, the biggest jump in the group by a wide margin, the kind of number that made people around him stare at the screen a second too long before saying anything.

"It's Momentum," Asha said, without anyone needing to ask, looking at the number with the same analytical attention she gave everything. "You're starting to fight like Park. Not the style — the calculation. He doesn't win by being faster either. He wins because he lets the other person wear themselves out hitting him and gives it all back, once, exactly where it hurts most."

Oliver said nothing. He kept looking at the number a moment longer, thinking about the crack under his hand, about Yegor's short, wordless shout as he stumbled toward the trees, about the half hour of silence he'd been carrying without telling anyone — not even himself, until today. He didn't correct Asha. He didn't say she was right either. He filed the comparison away for later, somewhere private, with more care than he'd have liked to admit in front of anyone.
