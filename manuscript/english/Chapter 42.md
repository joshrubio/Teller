# Chapter 42 — Outnumbered

The pumping station was a single-story brick building, sunk half a floor below street level, with two rusted generators that hadn't turned in years and a perimeter fence someone had cut clean through at one corner. Katalin parked a hundred meters back, lights off.

"If they've got something rigged up in there that connects to the river, this isn't just another abandoned factory," she said. "It's a direct access point."

Park was already out of the car, crossing the empty lot toward the cut fence with the fast, silent step of someone who'd done this enough times not to have to think about it. The air near the building already carried the ambient weight he'd been recognizing all night — denser here than at the factory that afternoon, more concentrated, as if something were actively pushing it instead of just letting it build up by neglect.

---

The inside smelled of rust and stagnant water. A single bulb, hanging from a bare wire, gave off just enough light for Park to see the two shapes before they saw him — a tall man, his right arm wrapped in bandages to the shoulder, and someone younger, more nervous, hands shoved in the pockets of a jacket that wasn't his.

"Tamás," Park said, quietly, more to confirm it to himself than to anyone else.

The bandaged man turned before Park finished saying the name.

"The Korean agent," he said, no surprise, his voice carrying the same quiet calculation Park remembered from Báthory in Lisbon. "Zoltán said you'd probably find us before we finished. He didn't say it'd be this soon."

"Where is he?"

"Nowhere it'll do you any good asking about," Tamás said.

The younger one said nothing. He stretched his arm toward the air between them, and the air itself seemed to thicken there, a visible density that bent the bulb's light like dirty water — a crude attempt to compress ambient Odem into one point, with no discipline to hold it for even a second. The dense mass of air trembled, unstable, the kind of thing that could collapse on him just as easily as on Park.

---

Park read the younger man's stance an instant before moving — feet planted wrong, weight thrown back, concentration shaking instead of settling. This wasn't someone who'd done this many times. This was someone who'd been told to try.

Park didn't wait for him to decide to release it.

He closed the distance to the younger man first — the less predictable target, not the more dangerous one — and caught his wrist before the compressed mass finished settling, twisting the arm up at an angle that broke the concentration without breaking the bone. The dense air dissipated all at once in a weak spark, and the kid dropped to his knees, holding his arm, and didn't get up again.

One.

Tamás was already moving by the time Park turned — faster than his bandaged arm should have allowed, making up with his whole body what his right hand couldn't fully give anymore. The first strike, a left elbow, carried raw Pressure loaded behind it, the same trick he'd used against Ferreira in Lisbon — the air compressed an instant before impact, nearly connecting with Park's temple; he dodged it by a margin he wouldn't have liked to measure in any other context, feeling the pressure wave graze past his ear like the edge of a blow that never touched him but still left its mark on the air.

"I learned to fight without my good arm these last few weeks," Tamás said, already stepping back to gain distance. "Your friend in Lisbon made sure of that."

"I can fix the other one too, if you insist," Park said.

Tamás smiled, no real humor behind it, and closed in again.

---

Katalin came in just as Park dodged the second strike, and instead of joining the fight went straight for the back wall, where a row of old valves had been recently tampered with — nuts without rust on a panel that had gone untouched for years.

The kid on the floor, still holding his arm, saw her pass and stuck out his good leg in a clumsy attempt to trip her. Katalin dodged it without so much as looking at him, a sharp twist of the hips Park recognized — this wasn't the first time she'd had to fight her way past something that didn't want to let her somewhere.

"They're draining pressure from the system toward some point downriver!" she shouted, already forcing the first valve against a mechanism that hadn't been greased in years. "Not here — somewhere else!"

Park didn't have time to fully process that. Tamás changed the angle of the attack mid-exchange — a feint at the torso that Park read, and the real strike going for the knee instead of higher, Pressure concentrated in the toe of his boot instead of his fist this time, aiming to take out Park's footing before his breath. Park felt it connect harder than a normal strike should, a sharp pain shooting up his leg, and instead of pulling back he turned with the impact, absorbing what Tamás's Pressure had thrown at him with Momentum, the borrowed force adding to what he'd been accumulating since Lisbon.

The punch that came out of that rotation landed square in Tamás's side, right below the ribs — not elegant, not calculated with surgical precision, but enough. Tamás staggered back two steps, out of breath, and for the first time something like doubt crossed his face.

"You're not winning this with one bad arm," Park said.

"I'm not here to win it," Tamás said. "I'm here to make sure you take long enough."

He didn't wait for Park to process that. With his left arm he swept a rusted toolbox off the floor, and instead of throwing it as a projectile he used it as a shield, a thin mantle of Odem hardening the metal's surface just enough that it wouldn't dent against his own arm as he closed the distance behind it — something Park hadn't seen coming from anyone yet, not in Lisbon, not here. The strike that followed, hidden behind the metal until the last instant and loaded with the same Pressure as before, connected with Park's shoulder before he could fully read it.

The pain ran down his whole arm, dull, familiar — the same shoulder he'd already been carrying since the machine room in Lisbon. Park didn't have time to assess it. He turned with the strike's momentum, let the toolbox slip out of its path, and countered at the exact same point he'd already hit — the side, below the ribs, twice in the same spot in under ten seconds.

---

Katalin managed to close the main valve at the exact instant Tamás said that, a metallic screech followed by a sudden silence where a low, constant hum had been that Park hadn't even noticed until it was gone.

"Closed!" she shouted. "Whatever they were draining, not anymore."

Tamás looked at the valve, then at Park, and something in his shoulders dropped — not surrender, calculation. The kind of man who knows how to read when an objective's already been met and when a lost fight isn't worth holding onto out of pride.

"You took long enough anyway," he said, and let himself slide down against the wall, hands open, not trying to get up.

Park didn't lower his guard. He looked at the kid, still on the floor, no strength left even in his good arm; looked at Tamás, breath ragged against the wall, the humorless smile still in place. Neither of them looked ready to try anything else, but two years in the field had taught him not to trust that just because it looked that way.

"For what?" he asked, not relaxing his stance.

"So he's not where you think he is anymore," Tamás said. "He left half an hour ago." He tipped his chin at the closed valve. "That was only so the whole city would start feeling something tonight, everywhere at once, while he finishes what he went to get."

"To get where?"

Tamás didn't answer right away. He looked at the kid still kneeling on the floor, holding his arm, and something like pity crossed his face before he looked back at Park.

"Where all of this started for our family," he said. "Ask Miss Báthory what's beyond the house she showed you. The new house isn't the first one."

Katalin's phone buzzed three times in a row while Park was still processing the sentence — a message from Ilona, then another, then a third. A market in Buda with five people manifesting raw Domains among the vegetable stalls. A metro station shut down after a passenger collapsed part of the platform ceiling without touching it. An office building in the financial district evacuated after someone set an entire boardroom on fire without striking a match. Three incidents, almost the same hour, in three different parts of the city with nothing in common except the exact moment they'd started.

"Everywhere at once," Park repeated, looking at Tamás.

"I told you," he answered, no satisfaction in his voice. "This was never about this station. It was about the whole city feeling something tonight, all at the same time, while he finished what he went to get."

Park called the local police from Katalin's phone, giving only the address and a bare-bones description of two injured men who needed attention — enough for someone to come, not so much that anyone would ask questions Park didn't have time to answer that night. Tamás didn't resist when Park tied his hands with the cord from the broken bulb. The kid didn't even seem to fully understand what was happening around him.

---

Katalin stood still for a full second, already back at the door, the adrenaline of the fight still visible in the slight shake of her hands.

"The ruin," she said, almost to herself. "Before 1838 they lived closer to the water, not where the house is now. They moved after that night, further inland, further from the river. Nobody's gone to those ruins as far back as I can remember. Not even us."

"What's there?" Park asked.

"I don't fully know," Katalin admitted. "But if Zoltán went there first, before coming to Budapest to do this, it's because he needed something that only existed at the origin. Not in the house that holds the channel now. At the place where the need to hold it began."

"Can you take me?" Park asked.

"I can try. Nobody in my family has gone there in generations, not even to look. There's no marked path, only whatever's left of my great-aunt's memory, if she still has it." She dragged a hand down her face, suddenly exhausted. "If Zoltán found something there his own family buried on purpose, Agent, it isn't going to be anything we want him to have."

Park thought of the thing Ilona had mentioned that morning without knowing she was mentioning it: the file no branch fully shares, not even among themselves. He wondered how much of what he was about to find would still have a name once it was in front of him, and how much of what he'd seen that night — Rafa's tremor, Báthory's detonation, now this — was still, underneath it all, the same unanswered question from Hartford: how long something old takes to stop pretending it's asleep.

---

Fifteen kilometers away, on ground that no longer appeared on any tourist map, the ruin was barely distinguishable from the forest that had grown over it across nearly two centuries — a low stone wall covered in moss, the remains of what must have been a staircase sunk under the dirt, not a plaque or a sign to warn anyone that something had stood there before the water of 1838. The only sound was the river, closer than it should have been so far inland, as if the channel still remembered an old path it no longer followed.

Zoltán knelt in front of the lowest section of wall and, for the first time in fourteen years, felt like he wasn't improvising. Fourteen years of small trials across half of Europe, each one calibrated a little better than the last, and none of them had ever brought him to this exact place before — because until a few months ago he hadn't even known it existed. Nobody in his family talked about this. Not even Katalin, with everything she'd dug up on him these four years, knew this place was still standing.

His arm still ached where the fire in Lisbon had caught him, the skin pulling tight under the bandage every time he bent his elbow. He didn't care. He reached into the gap already knowing exactly where to look — his family had never talked about this place, but something in his blood, something he'd felt without a name since childhood, had led him straight here the first time he'd tried, months earlier, long before Lisbon.

He pulled out an object he shouldn't have been able to pull out of that place — something his family had assumed lost for generations, buried on purpose by someone who'd wanted it to stay there forever. The Lidérc, inside him, stirred at the recognition, a hot pull that ran up his whole arm and reminded him, with a clarity that almost hurt, why he'd come here in the first place.

He didn't need his family's channel. He'd never fully needed it — he'd told himself that so many times in fourteen years it almost sounded like truth instead of excuse. The channel was slow. The channel was generational, disciplined, a luxury for people with time to spare and enough fear to spare not to use it fully. He'd never had either of those things. He'd only ever had the gift, raw, unfiltered, and fourteen years deciding what to do with it while no one else in his blood was willing to tell him how.

He got to his feet, tucked the object against his chest, and walked back toward the car waiting for him on the road, not once looking back at the ruins — not seeing, because he'd already stopped looking, the way the river's water seemed to follow him a stretch further than the current should have allowed before settling back into its normal course.

Behind him, the low stone wall stayed where it was, indifferent, covered in moss, as if none of what had just happened belonged to it at all. Almost two centuries guarding the same thing with no one ever coming to ask, and now, finally, someone had walked away with the answer before the question had even fully formed. Somewhere downriver, someone else — a Korean agent who still didn't know exactly what he was chasing, a woman with the same mark on her wrist that her great-aunt could barely use anymore — was going to have to figure out what to do with what was left.
