# Chapter 13 — Contact

The channel opened four seconds later.

"Copy," Hendricks said.

In the background, behind the line, Holt's voice was asking questions. Not into the channel — at the panel, at the technicians, at the readings of a system that had spent two minutes telling him the north sector was within normal parameters while a rear agent lay against the east facade with his neck at an angle normal parameters didn't cover. Park heard it clearly enough to understand what Holt was trying to do, and clearly enough to rule out that he'd manage it in time: the most precise diagnostic Domain in the Hartford division, up against an event the system had no way to register. Holt's questions were the right ones. They weren't the ones that solved the problem.

The channel closed.

Park calculated: thirty seconds for Hendricks to make a decision, two minutes to gear up a tactical team, four minutes to the north sector if they skipped perimeter check protocol. Extraction wasn't coming from control.

---

The Hartford control room occupied the second floor of the building on the perimeter's east corner. Four monitors with real-time sector sensor readings, a central board, three technicians. Holt stood in front of the panel, the north sector reading showing what it always showed: high saturation, unstable on the northwest axis, exactly within the margins seventy-two years of monitoring had learned to classify as background noise.

His Domain wasn't active perception — there was nothing to activate or sustain. It was a constant ambient presence, the kind of reading that after enough years stops being distinguishable from the ordinary experience of standing in a room: saturation gradients, concentration shifts across the four blocks, the specific weight of the north sector's Odem at two in the morning. Holt had spent fourteen years reading Hartford at that precision. He knew its baseline better than any instrument on the panel. Tonight the north sector had told him the same thing it always told him, and the locator recording was showing him that something had been there anyway — something that hadn't emitted Odem, but had used it. The difference was small in the reading. It was enormous in everything else.

Hendricks walked into the equipment room without anyone giving the order.

He called Reyes by surname — one syllable, no context, the tone of someone who knows that's all it takes. He called Morales and Brandt the same way: names with no instructions attached, because eleven years of shared shifts had made any instruction redundant between them. The four of them geared up in silence. Hendricks checked the zone locator while he closed the last strap on his vest: direct feed from the sector, real-time readings, no active pattern match in any quadrant.

Holt picked up the intercom.

"Extraction team's moving," he said into the field channel.

---

The north sector had the same silence as when they'd gone in.

Park used it as a reference point: if the quality of that silence changed, he'd know before the locator told him, which right now was doing exactly what it had been doing for the last four minutes — nothing useful. The point agent was to his left. Twelve meters between them, staggered position, line of sight on the north corner. The locator open, the screen angled toward what mattered most. No reading.

Park checked his Domain reserves for the third time since they'd called for extraction. Twelve days since Grayhaven — twelve days suppressing the inertia his own body generated with every movement, holding onto it instead of letting it disperse, until walking had turned into what it always turned into during accumulation: resistance with no source, water that wasn't there. The price of filling the reserves. By his estimate, he had about forty minutes if he managed it well.

Maybe enough to neutralize a Level 3 Authority — assuming that's what this turned out to be.

The silhouette appeared with no warning.

It didn't rise out of the ground. It didn't cross the space between buildings. It was just there — nine meters out, dead in Park's line of sight, in a space where a second earlier there had been nothing for the eye or the locator to register. He took the details in the order they arrived: first the body's proportions, slight, without the density of a fully built adult. Then the hair — white, short, no residual color, not bleached but white from the root. Then the age: sixteen, seventeen at most. The kind of face that hasn't finished becoming the face it's going to have.

The locator showed nothing.

Park had about a third of a second before the silhouette moved.

It was enough.

The acceleration he released was lateral. A fraction of a second before his body moved, the muscles in his right leg contracted — not instinct, preparation: the body anticipating the demand the Domain was about to place on it before the Domain placed it. The reserve discharged through the hip-knee-ankle axis on his right side, turning the push of his foot into something that shouldn't have matched human speed without a running start. Six meters. The cost arrived immediately: his right knee registered the acceleration spike as tension in the lateral tendon, the kind of signal the body sends when a joint has absorbed more than its normal range allows. Functional. Noted.

The silhouette hit the spot where Park had been standing.

Not with a fist. With something that was a fist and also something else — an impact that left a radial fracture in the asphalt within a half-meter radius, the kind of damage that implies a mass in motion several times greater than what the silhouette visibly carried.

Park landed and reoriented.

The silhouette was already gone.

---

The point agent turned toward him. Park read his face: jaw tight, eyes already back on the north sector, not on the rear agent against the east corner wall. A visible decision about where to put his attention. The kind of face that doesn't deny what it just saw — it chooses when to process it.

"You saw it?"

"Yes."

Three seconds of stillness.

The silhouette reappeared to the point agent's right, two meters out. Not from any direction — from no prior location at all. He turned with his weapon already raised. Fired twice. The shots were the loudest sound the north sector had made all night.

The silhouette took the hits.

One in the shoulder. One in the side. The clothes — dark jeans and a T-shirt that didn't match Hartford's November temperature — absorbed the shots in a way bodies don't absorb shots. No recoil. No shift in posture.

Then the silhouette extended its arm.

Contact lasted less than a second. The point agent went backward with his torso leading the rest of his body — head, arms, legs trailing the chest in the wrong order, the order of something that's had its skeleton yanked out from under it. Eight meters, horizontal. He hit the west corner building's facade shoulder first, then hip, then his head against the brick, and the sound it made was the kind with no scale until you've heard it and know exactly how much a human body weighs when it's thrown that fast into a surface that doesn't give. He fell. He got up — slow, with the movement of someone carrying serious damage, but he got up. Park registered it without taking his eyes off the silhouette: the agent had used something in that impact. Active Odem. Park had no access to Odem, no way to confirm it, but he had the consequence, and the consequence was that someone hit like that with no active Domain wouldn't be getting up.

The silhouette didn't go after him.

It was looking at its own hand.

Park watched the pause: the silhouette motionless, head tilted slightly down, left hand open in front of its chest. It wasn't a combat stance. It wasn't alertness. It was the gesture of someone who's just done something they'd never done before, working through the difference between knowing you can and having done it.

Park read it the only way he could read something happening on the Odem plane without access to that plane: by its visible consequence. The force that had moved the point agent hadn't been purely physical. It had been Odem moving through the silhouette's body, and the silhouette had felt it happen — not the arm's motion, but the other part, the transfer that had occurred in a register Hartford's protocol had no section to name. It was processing that feeling. Not the outcome. The feeling of having caused it.

The locator still showed nothing.

The silhouette looked up at Park.

---

The second exchange was different.

Not because Park had changed tactics — because the silhouette had. The first attack had been direct, no variation, no prior read on the target. This was something else: it looked for the angle before moving, processed Park's position with something he would have called analysis if that word hadn't sounded imprecise applied to a Level 3 Authority in Hartford's north sector.

It didn't sound imprecise. It sounded exactly right.

Park gave ground toward the north — not because he needed the space, but because he needed the silhouette to use it and show him what it did with it. It did: repositioned twice before attacking, hunting for the angle that broke the point agent's line of sight in the background. It picked the right one.

Park wasn't in it.

The exchange lasted ninety seconds. Park threw three attacks of his own — the first was instinct, the second was calibration, the third was the kind of decision you make when you already know it won't solve the problem but it'll tell you something about what resists it. The silhouette dodged all three. Not cleanly: the third grazed its left shoulder and produced a recoil that didn't match the force Park had applied, as if the body hadn't yet learned the relationship between taking an impact and compensating for it.

As if it were learning, in real time, what to do with its own power.

The shot came from the left with no warning on the channel.

A single shot. Park processed it late — he was mid-trajectory on one of the silhouette's attacks when it fired, and the fraction of a second he spent registering the source was enough for the silhouette's next move to catch him at the wrong angle. He adjusted. The cost was his left knee.

The silhouette had taken the shot dead center in the chest.

It was bleeding.

Not the amount that caliber should have produced, but visible blood, dark, soaking the fabric from the entry point. The silhouette looked down at it. The same gesture as before — head tilted slightly, attention fixed on the entry point as if what it was looking for wasn't on the surface but somewhere deeper. Park had already reached a hypothesis: it wasn't pain it was processing. It was information. The bullet had disrupted something in the Odem structure that held it together, and the silhouette had felt it the same way it had felt the force transfer with the point agent — not as damage in the way a human body knows damage, but as data on what could affect it, and how much.

Then it looked up at the point agent.

Park recognized the shift in target.

---

The extraction team crossed the perimeter at that moment.

Hendricks didn't announce their entry. Morales and Brandt took the flanks without being told — coverage angles four campaigns in active zones had turned into reflex. Reyes fell in behind Park, to his right. His eyes were on the ground. He'd been in the north sector less than forty seconds and was already recalibrating — Park could tell by the way he shifted his weight foot to foot, not instability but the visible processing of someone whose Domain had just taken in more signal than the briefings had known how to describe. The ground of Hartford moved constantly for Reyes. Tonight it moved differently.

"It's not coming from one point," he said quietly. "It's in the whole zone. Like the sector is it."

The silhouette looked at them. Not the way it had looked at Park in the second exchange. With something else — the same pause as after the fist, the same as after the shot to the chest. New data. Visible processing. Six people instead of two.

Hendricks activated his Domain.

Park had no way of seeing it. He had a way of feeling the consequence: the sector compressed by one more degree. And the silhouette — which for the last four minutes had appeared and vanished without warning, with no prior position, nothing the locator could track — didn't vanish.

It was there. It stayed there. For the first time, the locator started registering it: four units above threshold, then six, then nine.

"Morales," Hendricks said.

Morales advanced on the left flank. The silhouette saw her coming. It didn't attack — it repositioned: two meters of physical displacement inside Hendricks's field, plain movement with no phasing, distance held steady between itself and Morales while keeping its line on Park and Brandt. Morales adjusted. The silhouette repositioned again. It wasn't going to let her close the distance.

Brandt picked that moment. Park caught the shift in his stance — a step back, center of gravity dropping — and pulled the silhouette's attention for the one second available, throwing an attack toward the angle it had already learned to anticipate. Not to land it. To occupy it.

What Brandt did didn't look like a strike. It looked like the space within a two-meter radius of the silhouette had simultaneously decided it would rather occupy a smaller point. Asphalt dust, loose fragments from the first impact's fracture, the air itself — all of it pulling inward, toward one center, in under a second. The silhouette went with it.

The locator hit sixteen units.

The silhouette absorbed the collapse.

Not without effect — the edges of its torso lost definition for a moment, the shape imprecise, something the eye couldn't quite parse. But it pulled itself back together. Sixteen units dropped to twelve, then to nine. The mass of Odem making it up was enough to absorb an implosion and still be itself.

The silhouette went still.

Park recognized that stillness. The same as after the hand, the same as after the shot to the chest. But longer. As if the decision it was making belonged to a different category than the ones before.

The edges of its body began to blur.

Not like the phasing Hendricks had blocked. This was slower, more deliberate. It wasn't moving through Odem. It was releasing coherence — letting the Odem that made it up disperse back into the north sector's saturation, the same saturation Hartford had spent seventy-two years logging as background noise.

Hendricks noticed it before anyone else.

"I don't have it," he said. There was no alarm in his voice. It was a report.

The locator dropped. Not to zero. To baseline — the same old yellow in the north sector, the same saturation within parameters. On the asphalt, eight meters from Park's position, the radial fracture from the first impact remained. Four meters from that, the blood from the shot to the chest.

No one said sector clear.

Reyes said it, finally, quietly, in the tone of someone reporting what the team needs to hear in order to leave.

"I don't feel it moving anymore."

The team crossed the perimeter in silence. Park's device buzzed halfway through — encrypted channel, no visible sender name. He put it away without opening it and kept walking. On the asphalt of the north sector, behind them, the radial fracture from the first hit and the dark stain from the shot to the chest remained. The locator read baseline saturation. The locator had always read baseline saturation.

---

Holt was on the second floor in front of the reading panel when Park came up. The north sector, yellow.

"The system can't close the log," he said, not taking his eyes off the panel. "There's no signature to record. No entry, no partial match. What the locator caught tonight doesn't correspond to anything in the catalog."

Park didn't answer. He looked at the panel. The same yellow as always.

Hendricks came in two minutes later, locator in hand. He gave Holt the summary in four sentences — no elaboration, no analysis, the facts in the order they'd happened. Then:

"Tomorrow we go through the whole week's readings. North sector, hourly."

"Within parameters," Holt said. "The north sector readings. All week. Tonight too, right up to 22:41."

Hendricks didn't answer. He closed the locator.

It wasn't confusion Park was reading on Holt. It was the posture of someone who'd spent fourteen years reading Hartford with instrument-grade precision, someone whose instrument and whose reality hadn't lined up tonight — and who knew, without anyone telling him, that the instrument wasn't what had failed.

Park left before the conversation ended. In the hallway, he opened the encrypted channel.

The message was from Ji-ho. Four words in Korean, no signature, no context. He read it once. Closed it.

The blood was still on the asphalt of the north sector. The system had no entry for what had left it there. And whatever had left it there had chosen when to go.
