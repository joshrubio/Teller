# Chapter 34 — What the Ground Woke

Park saw him ten meters off, against the facade of a building under scaffolding: a middle-aged man, shoved into the framework by the crowd, hands open in front of him as if holding something that wasn't there. Between his fingers the air compressed, visible for a second, and the wave crushed a length of metal scaffolding against the stone with a screech that carried over the screaming. The man stood there staring at his own hands as if they belonged to someone else.

Three meters further on, a young woman dropped to her knees with no one pushing her, hands pressed against her ears, screaming something that wasn't physical pain but something closer to hearing too much at once — the same unfiltered noise Bruno still carried with him in a hospital bed, now flaring up all at once in someone who'd never asked to have it.

"Radio," Park said, touching his earpiece, not stopping. "We've got live awakenings. Civilians. At least one with something physical, near the scaffolding on Rua do Carmo—" he said, catching the street name painted on a small white plaque bolted to a cornice, "—another with uncontrolled perception, no way to defend herself."

"I know," Ferreira's voice, tense, cut by background noise. "Not the only one. Vitória's got two more near the convent stairs."

"Got him!" Vitória, sharper than usual, cutting into the channel. "Báthory, southeast, heading down toward Chiado. Walking. Not running. Doesn't move like someone running."

A different scream broke the line — Costa, her voice cracking into something that never became a word.

"Costa!" Ferreira said.

"I'm okay," she managed, breath forced, a metallic crash behind her. "One of his. Rua Garrett, by the café. Not a civilian, he's got something real, watch the hands, don't let his hands get near yo—"

The channel cut to static.

"Rui, with her, now!" Ferreira's order left no room for anything else.

---

Ferreira couldn't go. Both hands were tied up holding a thread already shaking between his fingers, strung between him and a tall, blond man advancing toward the mouth of the Largo do Carmo as if the stampede around him were nothing but background noise.

The man wasn't in a hurry. He wasn't afraid either. He moved with the economy of someone who'd done this before, in more than one country, and when Ferreira's first thread grazed his forearm, it left a thin red line, as if the cut had come from nowhere at all. The man stopped, didn't drop his guard — barely a gesture, his head tilting, gauging where the cut had come from.

"Eleven years serving him, and nobody's ever sent anyone with this against me," he said, almost with curiosity, raising the cut arm as if it were one more data point to log. "Slow. But it cuts clean."

Ferreira didn't answer. He traced a second thread, lower, aimed at the knee the man was leading with on each step — an attack that needed a direct line and just enough time to draw taut before the leg finished moving.

The man didn't move aside. He let the filament reach him, and at the last instant stamped his support foot into the ground — not to dodge, to break the angle: a short discharge of raw Odem through the sole, enough to raise a cloud of dust and debris between them that Ferreira had to split into two separate threads, one for the dust, one for the leg, and holding both at once cost him a visible tremor in his right hand.

"Two at once already weighs on you, doesn't it?" The man smiled, and closed the distance in the instant Ferreira was still holding both filaments, a fist loaded with raw Pressure heading straight for his sternum.

Ferreira released the leg thread to move — a forced choice, not a real one — and twisted his torso enough for the fist to graze his ribs instead of landing full. Even so, the air displaced by the blow threw him against the nearest building wall, his back slamming stone, a dull pain spreading from his shoulder blade.

"I don't need it to cut clean," Ferreira said, already back on his feet, voice strained. "I just need it to cut what you're not watching."

The third thread wasn't aimed at the body. It was aimed at the coat sleeve, a single precise pull that split it open from shoulder to elbow, and the filament that came out of that — shorter, faster to trace because it didn't have to cut through fabric — found the tendon in the arm the man had just used to strike. It didn't sever it fully. It was enough that the man's right hand lost strength halfway through his next punch, the fist arriving weak, with nothing behind it.

The man stepped back, looking at his own hand the way Ferreira had looked at his shoulder blade a moment before.

"That," he said, no resentment in it, almost respect, "I didn't see coming."

---

Costa had done the one thing her Domain knew how to do well: seal off the problem before the problem sealed her in.

The man had cornered her against the lowered shutter of a café on Rua Garrett, the metal terrace table overturned between them — younger than Costa had expected, his clothes too ordinary for what was coming out of his hands. Costa raised both hands and the air between them hardened, an invisible barrier that cost her every ounce of breath to hold, firm enough that the man's first punch rebounded off something that hadn't been there a second earlier.

"What's this supposed to be?" The man hit the barrier again, harder, and this time a visible crack of tension crossed the air like a fracture in glass.

"Containment," Costa said, her voice already strained. "It doesn't last forever. It lasts as long as I can hold it."

The barrier gave on the third hit, not all at once but like a seam splitting from one end, and the man's arm came through before Costa could seal it again. His knuckles grazed her jaw, not full on, enough to send her stumbling into the metal shutter, the hollow clang echoing down the street.

Rui came running around the corner of Rua do Carmo just in time to see the second punch coming straight for Costa's side, no barrier left to stop it.

He said nothing. He reached a hand toward the ground under the man's feet, and the cobblestone, instantly, weighed three times normal — not enough to sink him, enough that the leg he was stepping into the punch with stuck half a second too long, his balance breaking exactly when he needed it most.

Costa used that half second to finish what the barrier had started — not an elegant strike, a direct elbow to the throat, with the full weight of someone who had nothing left for a second chance.

The man dropped to his knees, out of air, and didn't get up again.

"Late," Costa said, leaning against the shutter, her jaw already swelling.

"On time," Rui said, and it was the longest thing he'd said all night.

---

Park calculated the distance to Rua Garrett anyway — four minutes, maybe less if nothing blocked his path. Rui was already headed there. Costa had backup. Báthory, on the other hand, was walking alone toward Chiado with nobody covering him yet, and that was exactly the kind of decision Cartwright had been assessing in him all along: not the one made with more force, the one made with the full picture in front of you.

Park didn't have to decide who to go to. That decision had already been made by someone else, and he had his own.

That was when he saw her.

Mariana, two streets down, wasn't searching anymore. She walked with the kind of direction only someone has who's just found exactly what they were looking for, cutting diagonally against the current of people fleeing downhill, not looking back once.

Park went after her.

---

The woman went down a service alley Park hadn't seen before, behind the iron-and-glass structure of the Santa Justa elevator — the Victorian tower rising from the Baixa up to the Carmo's height, evacuated now, the lit, empty car hanging halfway up as if the panic had caught it mid-climb. Mariana pushed through a maintenance door, its lock already forced, and went down a metal stairway toward the machine room.

The space was narrow, low-ceilinged, older than its function let on: the original late-nineteenth-century motor still in place, thick cables coiled on wooden spools, a modern electrical panel bolted over the old one without much care, aged insulation tape peeling at the splices. In a corner, two drums of oil for the mechanism, one with its lid not properly closed. It smelled of grease, hot metal, something electrical that had gone too long without inspection.

Báthory was already there, his back to the motor, as if he'd been waiting for both of them.

"Mariana." No surprise in it. "I knew you'd find me before they did."

"To stop this," Mariana said, and it didn't sound like a threat. It sounded almost like a plea. "You can still stop this."

Something in the air changed before Park understood what. Mariana closed her eyes for an instant and the dense undertow Park had been feeling all night shifted — not toward her, through her, a deliberate drain pulling the grief concentrated in the room toward some point Park couldn't see. Báthory tensed, his hand going instinctively to his chest, like someone feeling the air pulled from their lungs without being touched.

"That's not fair," he said, almost amused, almost hurt. "Using your own house against me."

"It's the one thing I have that you don't," Mariana said. "I'm not going to apologize for using it."

"And what are you going to do about him?" Báthory gestured at Park without fully looking at him. "Let him kill me in front of you? Or are you going to stop him too?"

Mariana didn't answer right away. The crack Park had heard in her voice an hour earlier, searching for Rafa, opened again, wider than before.

"You know where he is," she said, her voice dropping to something almost inaudible. "Tell me you don't know."

Báthory didn't deny it. Didn't confirm it either. But something crossed his face — a quick calculation, hidden almost instantly, the exact look of someone who knows more than he's going to say — and Mariana seemed to recognize something in that second Park couldn't fully read. A certainty crossed her eyes, and she didn't say it out loud.

"I still love you," she said then, almost like a confession that escaped her before she could decide whether it was worth making. "And that's the one thing stopping me from doing what I should be doing to you tonight."

"I know," Báthory said, and for the first time all night something in his voice sounded genuinely tired, not calculated. "It's the only thing I'm counting on to keep you from it."

Park didn't wait for them to finish.

---

He had nothing holding him back. No history with this man, no broken love weighing on the scale. Just Cartwright, somewhere at the back of his mind, asking him why he hadn't detained Mariana in Alfama — assessing his judgment, not his strength. This time the judgment was simple: this man had made an entire city shake to feed on what shook, and he was three meters away, his back to the only obstacle in front of him.

Park went straight for the center of the room, but not straight at the man. He faked toward Báthory's left first — an extra step, his weight shifting forward as if he meant to circle that way — just to see how he'd react. Báthory turned his shoulders to cover the angle, not his hands. There was the data point: he read distance and trajectory, not the real point of contact yet. Someone used to people arriving head-on and staying still long enough to be charged.

Park changed the angle mid-step and went straight in, for real this time.

Báthory raised his right hand, palm open, and Park recognized the stance an instant before it finished — the same gesture that had left Bruno unconscious in a hospital, now aimed at him. He didn't try to fully dodge it: he turned his shoulder, let the palm graze his sleeve instead of bare arm, and used the minimal contact to read what Báthory was doing instead of just avoiding it. Concentration, not force — a charge point building before release, exactly the way Ferreira had described it weeks earlier.

That gave him the window. Park closed the distance while the charge was still forming, too soon for Báthory to release it clean, and drove his elbow into the raised forearm — not a strike to injure, one to break the concentration before it finished.

It half worked. The detonation still went off, deflected, into the motor instead of Park — a visible compression of air, brief, that left a dark dent in the metal housing and a smell of ozone in the closed space. A spark jumped off the badly installed electrical panel, brief, dying on its own.

"Interesting," Báthory said, shaking out his hand, the knuckles already reddening. "Nobody interrupts my concentration this close. Usually they're too busy dying to try."

He didn't get to admire his own line. He changed angle — the second detonation came faster than the first, without the same careful calibration, cruder, bigger. Park didn't read it in time. He absorbed the edge of the impact on his forearm, weeks of accumulated inertia finally finding an outlet, and felt the bone protest under the skin with no Class-R to protect it this time. The pain climbed to his shoulder, a dull throb that didn't fade.

Mariana moved between them.

She didn't attack either one. She put one hand on Báthory's chest and, an instant later, the other on Park's shoulder — the exact same gesture, no preference, not deciding which side she was on. The drain that had been pulling the room's grief opened wider, swallowing part of the charge Báthory was building for his next strike.

"Enough," she said, her voice breaking on the word. "Both of you. Enough."

"You can't protect both of us, Mariana," Báthory said, no cruelty in it, almost with pity. "Sooner or later you're going to have to let go of one."

She didn't answer. She didn't let go of either one.

Báthory used the second of hesitation to break free of Mariana's hand with a twist of his wrist and turn back on Park — faster than the two previous times, giving him no time to fully recover from the last hit. Park read the feint toward his shoulder an instant before the real blow shifted toward his ribs, but he didn't manage to dodge it fully. He took it on the side, the air punched out of him, and instead of absorbing it still he turned with it — his whole body rotating on his left foot, loading into the turn what he'd just taken.

The punch that came out of that rotation wasn't clean or elegant. It went straight into Báthory's chest, all the inertia of the previous hit returned in a single point of contact — and Báthory, who hadn't expected his own blow to come back to him transformed, took it full on. His body left the ground half a meter before hitting the motor's housing, the already-dented metal giving a little more under the impact.

Báthory didn't stay down as long as Park needed him to. The impact against the housing had knocked loose a length of thick cable, half a dozen coils of copper and old rubber falling free beside his left foot — and Park scooped it up without breaking his advance, his hand closing on the cable in the same instant his foot touched the ground, loading into it the residual inertia from the last turn. He didn't aim for Báthory's center. He aimed for the right hand, the only one that had charged every detonation that night.

It never connected.

Báthory, still half-fallen against the motor, turned his palm toward the machine instead of toward Park — a movement that had nothing to do with what Park had just done, something he'd already decided before, and that Park understood a second too late: he wasn't charging to defend himself. He was charging against the badly sealed drum a meter from his own hand.

"The oil!" Mariana screamed, seeing the same thing Park saw at the same instant.

Park's cable reached Báthory's wrist half a second after the detonation had already fired — in time to deflect the arm, too late to stop what the arm had already released. The whole motor spat a short tongue of blue flame that licked the electrical panel before dying against the metal floor — not an explosion, not yet, but a clear warning of what that room could become if someone let off something like that again with less control, or less luck.

"You saw what's down here now," Báthory said, getting slowly to his feet, his voice not quite steady. "Next time someone loses their temper in this room, we won't be talking about a punch."

Park pressed a hand to his side without thinking, breath short, his forearm already swelling under the sleeve where no Class-R covered it. Nothing broken — he could still close his fist, still move — but his whole body was asking for a second he didn't have to give it.

---

The service door slammed open.

The boy Park had seen sitting around a candle in a Mouraria courtyard, the one who never fully opened his eyes, the one Mariana looked at a second longer than the others — stood in the doorway, breath ragged from running, his eyes moving from Báthory to the floor, to Mariana, to Park, and back to Báthory, still undecided.

Mariana went breathless at the sight of him.

"Rafa," she said, and this time she said it out loud, in front of everyone.

The boy didn't answer. He took a step forward, toward the man who a while earlier had held his face between both hands like a proud father, and something in his expression — resolve, not fear — told Park, before anyone else understood anything, that he was about to step in.
