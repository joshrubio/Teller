# Chapter 45 — What the Shackle Couldn't Pay

The Buda end of the bridge opened onto a narrow street of nineteenth-century facades, most of them dark at that hour, a small corner shop still lit. Zoltán ran straight for it, and Park followed eighteen meters back, closing ground slowly, not spending what he had left to reach him before it was time.

Zoltán stopped dead level with the shop, turned, and Park saw up close for the first time what was happening to his left arm. It wasn't skin over muscle anymore. Something underneath moved in layers, dark, wet, as if the whole arm had decided to stop pretending to be an arm.

"You don't need to keep running," Zoltán said, his voice rougher than before. "I don't know if I'll be able to stop when I get there."

He attacked before he finished the sentence. The transformed arm stretched half a meter past what a human arm should allow, and what came wasn't a punch — it was closer to a whip crack, the tip grazing Park's cheek by a margin under a centimeter.

Park felt the air split, not his skin. One more centimeter and he'd have lost half his face.

He countered at the elbow of the extended arm — not the torso, not the head, the point where the extension bent, the place where whatever wasn't quite bone anymore had to give if it was going to give anywhere. It connected. The arm snapped back with a wet crack, shorter than it had been a second earlier, and Zoltán screamed — real pain, not just the entity's echo.

Zoltán didn't give Park time to reset his weight. The second attack came from an angle Park didn't expect — not the transformed arm, a normal right-handed punch, loaded with the same Pressure Tamás had been using all the previous night, only multiplied several times over. Park read it late. He managed to turn his torso just enough for the blow to graze his already-bruised ribs from the bridge instead of breaking them clean.

The pain doubled him forward for an instant. Zoltán didn't wait for him to recover — the good arm's elbow came down toward the back of Park's neck, a strike meant to end the fight right there.

Park felt it coming through the displaced air, not sight. He rolled forward, under the blow, and came out of the roll with his leg extended, sweeping Zoltán's footing out from under him. It didn't connect clean — Zoltán half-jumped the sweep — but the imbalance cost him a full step, the first position error he'd made since the bridge.

The corner shop lost its entire storefront in the next blast — Zoltán slammed his good palm into the ground, the pressure wave shattering the glass out into the street in a rain of fragments Park dodged by turning his body, one shard's edge opening a shallow cut across his forearm.

Behind them, a hundred meters back, Mariana and Katalin had already crossed the bridge, following the trail of destruction instead of the fighters themselves. Katalin, with only one good arm, shoved a man back who'd come out to see what was happening just as the second lash of Zoltán's arm passed through where his head had been an instant before.

"Inside, inside!" she shouted, pushing the man back into his doorway.

Mariana didn't try to reach Park. She spread the Void in a wide arc behind the two men — not to fight, to muffle. The street noise dropped in a twenty-meter radius, enough that no one else would come out to look before it was over.

Park was starting to read the pattern. Every time Zoltán let go of something big — the pressure wave, the arm's lash — the momentary blankness in his eyes lasted a little longer than the time before. One second. Then a second and a half. The man inside all of it had less time with each release.

That didn't make him slower. It made him more careless with his own body, and that was worse.

Zoltán closed distance with a short jump, three meters, and the strike that followed didn't go for Park's body — it went for the ground under his feet again, but this time with the transformed arm driving in like a stake before the wave broke free. The crater that opened was twice as deep as any before it. A parked car near the edge tipped into the hole, the engine dropping with a low metallic crash.

Park jumped back just in time — the crater's edge grazed his heels, and if he hadn't judged the jump to the centimeter he'd have gone down with the car itself.

The car finished falling a second later, the metal folding against the bottom of the crater with a boom that rattled windows the length of the block. On one of the upper floors, someone flicked a light on and off again fast, as if they'd decided that whatever was happening down there was better not seen.

Katalin, still trailing them a hundred meters back, had to drag a second bystander clear of a lamppost the crater's own tremor had knocked loose — the whole pole, five meters of metal, landing where the man had been standing an instant before. She did it with one arm, the other hanging useless, and the effort pulled a groan out of her she didn't want Park to hear.

"This isn't a fight you can keep tracking up close anymore," Mariana said, helping her up.

"Then let's clean up fast," Katalin answered. "Before there's no one left to clean up for."

---

"That could've killed you," Zoltán said, almost like an apology.

"It hasn't yet," Park answered.

"Give it time," Zoltán said, and something in his tone had stopped sounding like a threat. It sounded like a sincere warning — the kind only someone who knows exactly what comes next, and can't do a thing to stop it, can give.

He closed distance again, faster than the last exchange — the pattern Park had already seen on the bridge, every approach leaving less room to react than the one before. This time the attack came combined: the transformed arm low, going for the legs, the right hand high, going for the face, both at once.

Park couldn't block both. He chose the high one — losing his face wasn't an option, losing his balance was — and let the transformed arm wrap around his ankle. What he did next cost him something: instead of pulling back to break free, he shoved his whole weight forward, using the grip itself as a fixed point, and spun in the air so the right-hand punch passed over his head instead of through it.

He came down on Zoltán elbow-first. The impact sounded like something cracking that wasn't asphalt.

Zoltán shoved him off with more raw force than technique, and both of them rolled in opposite directions across the glass-covered ground from the shattered storefront. Park felt the fragments opening small cuts across his back through his clothes, none serious, all of them adding up.

He got to his feet first. Zoltán took half a second longer — the transformed arm, still retracting from the elbow strike, wasn't answering as fast as before.

"You're running out of parts that don't hurt," Park said.

"And you're running out of time before it stops mattering to me whether mine do," Zoltán answered.

They read each other for a moment. Zoltán faked toward the torso with the good arm — the same gesture Park already knew, the feint that opened the way for the real strike. Park didn't take the bait this time. Instead of blocking the torso, he planted his feet, let the feint sail past, and when the transformed arm came in from the flank a second later — the real part of the attack — he was already turning to take it on his shoulder instead of his face.

The impact drove him back four meters, his feet dragging across the cracked asphalt. It hurt. But it wasn't bone that gave this time.

Zoltán didn't give him four seconds to catch his breath. He came again, and this time the transformed arm split into something that wasn't quite fingers — three blunt points, each moving independently, going for three different angles at once. Park hadn't seen anything like it all night.

He read the three trajectories in the air, a calculation he had no time to do in words. Two of the points would miss him by a hair if he didn't move. The third was headed straight for his sternum.

He turned to the side, letting the first two pass grazing his jacket, and caught the third between his forearm and his ribs, closing the angle before it finished extending. The appendage twisted against his body, trying to break free, and Park felt something cold and wet seep through the fabric before he let go of it.

"That shouldn't be able to touch me without burning," he said, more to himself than to Zoltán.

"None of this should be happening," Zoltán answered, his voice already further from himself than a moment before. "And yet."

The building on the corner, the same one with the shop, lost part of its cornice when Zoltán's next strike missed its target for the first time all fight — Park got clear in time, and the transformed arm slammed into the facade instead, tearing loose stone and dust in a cloud that took several seconds to settle. A fist-sized chunk of masonry landed a meter from where Mariana and Katalin were watching from the careful distance they'd kept the whole chase.

"Every miss is worse than every hit," Katalin said, pulling Mariana clear of the fall radius with her one working arm. "He's stopped caring where this lands."

Mariana didn't answer right away. She was watching Park, not Zoltán.

"It still matters to him," she said finally. "That's the only thing we've got left going for us."

Park answered with something he hadn't used since Lisbon — not a fully charged strike, but something measured, a wave of Momentum released in a controlled radius from the point of contact of his own foot against the ground, meant to destabilize without destroying. The asphalt under Zoltán shook just enough to knock the support out from his bad leg, and for the first time in the whole chase, Zoltán went down to his knees without anyone touching him directly.

Katalin, thirty meters off, saw the move and something changed in her face.

"That," she said, almost to herself, "is a terrain read, not a strike. He's measuring him. He's waiting for something."

She was right. Park hadn't spent that wave to knock him down — he'd spent it to see, from the fall, exactly how long it took Zoltán to get back up, and from where.

Zoltán pushed himself up using the transformed arm like a third leg, something no human bone should allow, and for an instant looked more animal than man — braced on four points, head low, evaluating. What was left of his eyes wasn't blinking quite right anymore.

"Last question," he said, his voice split between two registers, one his own and one that wasn't. "Is it worth it, agent? Whatever this is about to cost you, climbing higher."

"I still don't know," Park said. "But you didn't know either when you started. Look where you are."

Zoltán laughed, a sound broken halfway through, and charged one last time — not a calculated attack, something desperate, everything he had left put into a single strike with the whole transformed arm, meant to split Park in half if it landed clean.

Park let it come. Not because he couldn't dodge it — because he'd spent two full exchanges waiting for exactly this: the strike where Zoltán, finally, would put everything he had left into one point, holding nothing back for whatever came next. He crossed both forearms, the same guard he'd watched Zoltán use against him on the bridge, and absorbed the full blow with Momentum — all of it, no redirecting, no waste, holding it whole in his body for the first and only time that night.

The impact drove him back two meters, the bones in both arms protesting under the Class-R already gone nearly translucent at the edges. But he stayed on his feet, and for the first time all night felt the full reserve — his own, built up since the pumping station, plus what he'd just swallowed whole from Zoltán himself — gathered in one place, waiting for an exit.

He didn't aim for the body. Aiming for the body was what he'd been doing all night, and the body wasn't where the real problem was anymore.

He closed the distance in a single step and concentrated Mass and Momentum at once in his right fist — full Stage 3, more than he'd ever released in a single discharge, Lisbon included — and put it, with every last drop of precision he had, on the Shackle itself, the exact point where the iron closed around Zoltán's wrist.

There was no hairline crack this time. Two nights of holding it without a channel, without a filter, found in that single point of contact the shortest way out, and the Shackle itself didn't hold — it didn't crack, it burst, and everything it had been about to release all at once went out through where Park had just struck.

The arm disappeared from the fist to the shoulder in a fraction of a second too fast for anyone, even Park, to finish processing what they'd seen. It wasn't a clean cut. It was closer to what had happened to the storefront, to the facade, to everything else the night had destroyed — flesh and bone and whatever the arm had been carrying inside it, undone from within, taking part of the shoulder and a strip of his side with it. The resulting shockwave passed through Zoltán and kept going, into a brick house eight meters beyond, leaving a gap that looked more like a bite than a hole.

Zoltán fell backward before the sound of it fully reached Park's ears. He didn't scream. The blow had torn the air out of him along with everything else.

---

Park stood for one full second, his fist still extended, not knowing what to do with his hands now that there was nothing left to hit. What was in front of him wasn't a fight anymore. It was a man bleeding out on the cracked asphalt, his breath short and wet, his right hand — the only one he had left — pressing on a shoulder that had nothing left to press back against.

"That wasn't what—" Park started, and didn't finish, because there was no way to finish it that sounded like anything he'd actually wanted.

Katalin ran up, her good arm out front, and didn't hesitate a second before dropping to her knees in the blood.

"Zoltán. Zoltán, look at me."

"Hurts less than I thought it would," he said, his voice already faint, slurring. "That's gotta mean something bad, right?"

"Shut up and breathe," she said, her hands already searching for somewhere to press without knowing if there was anything left worth pressing.

Mariana arrived a moment later, and didn't stop ten meters out this time. She crossed the last stretch running, something Park hadn't seen her do all night, and dropped to her knees on the other side of Zoltán without caring about the blood on the hem of her coat.

"No," she said, very quietly, almost to herself. "No, no, not like this."

"Mariana," Zoltán said, and something in his face, for the first time since the bridge, relaxed completely at the sight of her, as if that were the one face he'd been waiting to see before he could stop having to try at anything. "You came, in the end."

"Shut up too," she said, her voice breaking. "Save your breath."

"Not much left to save," he said, and smiled, a small gesture, almost calm. "Tell Katalin she did learn. That she can do it. Don't tell her now. Tell her in a year, when it won't be a comfort to her anymore, so she knows it's not just something people say at a moment like this."

Katalin, who was hearing every word, said nothing. She pressed harder where there was nothing left to press.

Zoltán's chest rose once more, slow, a short wet sound behind the words. It didn't rise a second time.

---

No one moved for a while. Mariana stayed with one hand on Zoltán's face — not on the wound, there was nothing left to do about the wound — and the other closed around his good hand, which no longer had the strength to return the gesture.

"You said you hadn't decided if you came here to save him or finish him," Park said, quietly, almost not wanting to say it out loud.

"I didn't decide it," she answered, not looking away from Zoltán's face. "You did, a minute ago, without knowing that's what you were deciding. And now I have to find a way not to hate you for it while I thank you at the same time, because we both know that if you hadn't done it tonight, I would've had to, or Katalin, or no one, and then it would've been worse for everyone still alive tomorrow."

Park had nothing to answer to that. He stood a little apart, letting the blood on the asphalt be, for now, the only language the three of them needed.

The street around them had a shattered storefront, two wrecked cars, a crater the size of a small room, and a silence that didn't resemble any silence he'd felt that night — not Hartford's after the Saturation, not the imugi's in Korea. This one had a name and a face and a cousin still kneeling beside it, her hand not letting go of a man's who could no longer return the gesture.

Far off, over the bridge, the north tower's groaning had stopped entirely — not fixed, just relieved of whatever had been pushing it to its limit. Sometime tomorrow, or the day after, someone from an office Park didn't know would have to explain that bridge with words that weren't the true ones. Tonight it still wasn't his job to think about that.

Katalin didn't move from where she was. Park didn't rush her. He stood, carrying the silence alongside them, and for the first time all night felt no need for anyone to tell him what to do with it — only the uncomfortable certainty that, this time, he knew exactly what he'd done.
