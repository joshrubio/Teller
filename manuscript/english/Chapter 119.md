# Chapter 119 — The Quay

---

Of the night at the stadium, Oliver kept fragments, not a whole story.

He kept the helicopter — two of them, really, one for the people who could still sit up on their own and a slower one for those who couldn't. He kept the white room they were herded into the moment they landed, somewhere nobody bothered to name, and a doctor's hands checking his pupils with a penlight while someone at the back read names off a list in a low voice, crossing them out. He kept having slept — truly slept, with no dreams he could recall — for what they told him afterward had been almost fourteen hours.

Above all, he kept never having seen St. Gotthard again.

Nobody took him back to collect anything. A bag with his things turned up already packed at the foot of a bed that wasn't his, in a room that wasn't his either, and that was the whole of the explanation he got for the fact that the center where he'd spent a full month of his life had closed behind him without his noticing it shut.

The transfer itself he remembered better than the rest, because by then he was awake enough to pay attention: a windowless van to a small airfield, a short flight, another car, and roads that dropped from the mountains toward something green and flat that none of the six of them recognized. No one explained the destination on the way. Someone — Hana, over the radio, the only institutional voice left on the other end of all this — said just a name, twice, as if repeating it would make it less strange the second time.

The Quay.

Officially it was the Lake Léman Center, a private medical research institute with a discreet plaque beside the gate, the kind of place nobody looks at twice from the road. A villa from before the First World War, with high walls and gardens that blocked any view from outside, and a private dock on a lake Oliver had only ever seen in photographs of other people's autumns. AMAT had bought it seventy years ago, when the market for large properties collapsed and no one else wanted a villa that size. By the little anyone had bothered to tell them, it served as a way station — the last step before a normal candidate, a European with years of training behind them, earned official access to AMAT.

The Valais group didn't fit that description, and all of them knew it from the first day. They hadn't come as candidates for anything. They'd come as what was left of a center that no longer existed, with no clear category to put them in, and that — more than the lake, more than the gardens with no fence — was the first thing Oliver understood about the place: nobody here knew quite what to do with them, and for once that uncertainty wasn't dressed up as protocol.

---

The Quay was emptying out, a little at a time, without announcements.

Oliver noticed it first in the dining hall. On the first morning they'd had to wait for tables; by the fourth, there were empty seats at every one. Names disappeared from the board in the annex in small batches, two or three at a time, with a new label pasted over the old one — *reassigned*, always that word, never where to. No one said goodbye. People just stopped turning up at breakfast, and breakfast went on running exactly the same.

The six from Valais stayed on the list.

No one had told them why. No one had told them anything, really, since they'd been brought down from Milan to a building with gardens and shown where the rooms were, as if an exam half its participants had left on stretchers were a formality that closed on its own with the key to a bedroom.

It was June. That was new too.

In the Valais the cold had held until the end, and at St. Gotthard it was a condition of the place, part of the program like the schedules. Here the air was warm at seven in the morning. The grass on the south lawn ran down to the water with no fence and no painted line, and the lake was simply there all day, changing color with the hour, and nobody told you which hour to look at it.

Nobody told you anything. That was the hard part.

Oliver had been waking at five-thirty for so long that he no longer remembered deciding to, and now he stayed in bed, awake, listening to the building and waiting for something that didn't come. The first morning he went down anyway. He walked the perimeter of the gardens twice, found the dock, came back. Nobody stopped him. Nobody asked where he was going.

It took him three days to understand that this wasn't surveillance in disguise. Nobody cared. That was all.

---

Nadia had worn splints on both hands until the middle of the week. Now she wore only the left one, with the right bandaged more lightly, enough to leave her three fingers free, and she spent most of each morning on the stone bench in the east garden trying small things.

When Oliver got there, she was fighting an apple.

"Don't ask," she said, without looking up.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to offer, which is worse." She managed to pin it against her thigh with the back of her right hand, which left both hands occupied for an operation she'd have done without looking a month ago. "The physical therapist says it's a matter of weeks. She didn't say how many."

Oliver sat down a meter away. The apple slipped away from her once; she caught it against her knee and kept going.

"Does it hurt?"

"Not like I expected." A bite, finally, with the apple still clamped against her leg. "It hurts when I try something and it doesn't come out. The hand doesn't hurt. The trying does."

They sat like that for a while. Out on the water, past the grass, someone had taken out a boat with no oars and was letting it drift, which was the kind of thing that didn't need permission here.

"I tried to push," Nadia said at last. "With the Domain, I mean. The day before yesterday. Nothing big. Just moving a chair across the room."

"And?"

"Nothing." Another bite. "It's not that it failed. I couldn't find where to start. I've spent two years steering that thing with my arms without realizing it was my arms doing it."

She said it without self-pity, with the same precision she'd always used to recite her father's procedures. Oliver recognized the tone. It was the voice of someone cataloguing a problem so she wouldn't have to feel it yet.

"You'll tell someone."

"Who, Oliver?" She looked at him for the first time, with something that wasn't blame. "There's no evaluation here. No session. Nobody's writing anything down. I've spent four days looking for someone to tell, and I've come to the conclusion that the only reason I want to tell anyone is that if nobody writes it down, I don't know whether it counts."

---

Riku stayed away from the water.

Oliver noticed it on the second day and said nothing until the fifth, when he found him on the strip of grass farthest from the lake, standing, moving his hands slowly through the air — not strikes, exactly. Placements. A foot that planted, lifted, and planted again.

"Foot first," Oliver said.

"Foot first," Riku agreed, without stopping. "I mean, no. Foot and charge at the same time. If the charge comes after, it's useless, it just leaks out into the air." He planted again, and this time the grass under his shoe made a dry, tiny sound, like a small branch snapping. "That."

"How long have you been at it?"

"Every day." He stopped. He looked at his left hand, opened the fingers, closed them. "I'm not training. For the record. If anyone asks, I'm not training."

"There's no one to ask."

"Right." Riku dropped onto the grass without warning, the way he always did when a conversation started to matter to him. "It's weird, isn't it? Since I was fourteen, somebody's been telling me how far I can go. Literally. With paperwork. Approved level, maximum authorized radius, do not activate without direct supervision." He pulled up a blade of grass. "And now nobody's watching, and it turns out I'm still doing exactly the same drill I'd be doing if they were."

"Maybe that means it's yours now."

"Or that I don't know how to do anything else." He said it lightly, but it wasn't light, and they both knew it. "I owe it to someone who gave me a black eye, Oliver. I don't know where to put that, either."

---

Dara did go near the water. Dara practically lived in it.

She swam twice a day, morning and last thing at night, always alone, always the same route — from the dock to the mooring buoy and back, again, again. Oliver watched her climb out one afternoon and sit on the planks of the dock with her legs hanging over the edge, dripping, with no towel, staring at the exact spot she'd just come back from.

"Two hundred forty meters there and back," she said, when she noticed him sitting down. "I counted. Three different times, in case I'd got it wrong."

"Had you?"

"Twice two hundred forty. Once two hundred thirty-six." She shrugged. "That was the time I got distracted."

They watched the water. On the far shore there were villages neither of them could name.

"I tried it on Tuesday," Dara said.

Oliver didn't ask what. With Dara he never needed to.

"Two again. Here, with nobody around." She waved a hand toward the lawn, empty at that hour. "I got both of them out and they held almost five minutes. On the wall they held less, and I told myself it was because I was about to burst. Here, sitting down, with nothing at stake, almost five."

"That's good."

"That's worse." She didn't raise her voice. "If they only hold five minutes when nothing's going on, it means the time it mattered I didn't hit the limit of what I can do. I hit the limit of what I was willing to do. And those aren't the same."

A bird crossed low over the water without touching it.

"Anka told me something at St. Gotthard," she added, more slowly. "That the reason, if there was one, wasn't in the Odem. I've been trying ever since to decide whether that was help or a diagnosis."

---

Asha had organized a laundry schedule nobody had asked for.

It was taped to the door of the annex: six names, six days, one column for sheets and another for everything else. When Oliver read it a second time, she appeared behind him with someone else's clothes folded under her arm.

"There are four machines for the whole building," she said, before he could say anything. "With the people who are left, we don't need a schedule. With the people who were here last week, we did, and we didn't have one. Someone should have set it up then."

"Those people are gone."

"Right." She kept looking at the paper a second longer than she needed to, arms crossed. "It's a bad schedule, too. I made it for six and there are six of us, but I designed it thinking of forty."

She didn't take it down. Oliver didn't suggest it.

"Have they told you anything?" she asked, and it was the first time in four days that anyone had asked that out loud.

"No."

"Me neither." She uncrossed her arms and looked at her palms, that habit of hers, a check more than a nerve. "It took Petra nine days to find out about Vienna after she asked. We've gone five without asking for anything. I don't know which of the two is worse to count."

She left before he could answer, the way she always did when the conversation was over for her.

---

Pier fished.

He'd found a rod somewhere — Oliver never learned where, and suspected Pier wasn't going to explain — and from the third day on he sat at the end of the dock every afternoon on an overturned plastic crate, with the calm of someone who has done exactly this on other docks for decades.

"There's nothing here," Oliver said, sitting down on the planks.

"There are pike." Pier didn't take his eyes off the water. "Few of them, and not at this hour. That's not the problem."

"What's the problem?"

"That I've gone three days without changing the bait because I don't want to get up." He said it with the gravity of a diagnosis, and then, barely, he smiled. "The body learns doing nothing fast. Faster than a man my age likes to admit."

The line drifted with the current, with no weight on the other end.

"Yusuf taught me a blood knot in the east courtyard," Pier said, after a while. "Years ago. He laughed that a physicist needed help tying two threads together." He took in a little line, unhurried. "I owe him a bottle of something decent. I told him so before he went off after his own business, and he said to write it down, since he was worse at keeping count."

Oliver said nothing.

"When this clears up," Pier went on, watching the water, "and someone tells us what we are now, I'm going to collect that bottle. A man who spends his life following other people's trails ought to have someone waiting for him with a debt outstanding. That's what makes you come back."

He propped the rod against his knee.

"You owe me one too, by the way," he added. "From the training room. You still haven't told me what you were doing in there alone at two in the morning."

"It doesn't matter anymore."

"It almost never does," Pier said. "That's not the same as not telling."

And he went back to the water, without pressing, with the exact patience of someone who has spent his whole life waiting for something to bite.

---

Nobody planned it. It was just that when evening came and the heat finally eased a little, they all ended up on the same stretch of shore without having said so — Pier still holding the rod, Asha with the laundry schedule forgotten for one night, Nadia sitting with her hands still for once, Riku no longer pretending he hadn't been training, Dara with her hair still damp from the last swim.

They sat in a row, bare feet in the water up to the ankle, touching now and then without meaning to — Riku's shoulder against Nadia's, Dara's foot brushing Oliver's when she moved her legs. No one talked about Milan. No one talked about what they still didn't know. Asha told a story about a building her father had worked on, one that fell down twice before it learned to stay up, and for the first time in days someone laughed for real, without the caution they'd all been laughing with since the stadium.

Pier was the first to notice, by sound — an engine unlike the town's launches, steadier, coming from the sky and not the water — but it was Oliver who, without thinking, without quite deciding, let the Registry open a second before anyone else understood what was happening.

A helicopter was coming down toward the little airstrip in the north garden, barely more than a strip of packed earth and a windsock Oliver hadn't seen used in the ten days they'd been there.

"Was anyone expecting anyone?" Riku asked, already on his feet, his voice climbing toward something like hope.

Nobody answered, but nobody moved to hide, either. The six of them stood watching, water still around their ankles, as the rotors slowed and the two silhouettes inside came into relief against the last of the light.

Oliver recognized them before the skids touched down — the signature didn't lie, even at that distance. Voss. Maren.

"It's Voss," he said, and something in his own voice surprised him: relief, pure and unfiltered, the same one he felt run through the rest of the group at nearly the same moment, as if the simple fact that someone they knew had come for them were an answer in itself.

They stood up. Someone — Dara — took a few steps toward the airstrip before the rest followed, all of them with the same held-in hurry of people who have spent ten days waiting for something, anything, to come and tell them what they were now.

The doors opened. Maren climbed down first, ducking her head under the still-turning blades.

Voss came down behind her.

It took Oliver a moment to find his face in the dusk, and when he did, the hope that had been rising in his chest stopped halfway, with nowhere left to go.

It wasn't the face of a man bringing good news.

It was grim, shut tight, with something underneath that Oliver had never fully seen in it before — not exhaustion, not worry. Something closer to anger, held down with the same discipline Voss used to hold down everything else, and for that very reason more visible than if he'd let it out.
