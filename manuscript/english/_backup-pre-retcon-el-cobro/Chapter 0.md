# Prologue — Surveillance

There's a question no treatise ever quite answers. By the time anyone gathers the vocabulary to ask it properly, they've stopped needing the answer.

The book open on Park's knees had spent more than a hundred years trying anyway. A German scholar, dead long before anyone classified anything, had spent a full chapter picking up the work of an even older preacher — private letters, business correspondence, village stories a man of faith had tried to put in order and never quite managed. He lined them up, side by side: the same handful of tales any grandmother can tell without ever having been taught them. The hero who went down to the river and came back with his sword stained to the hilt, swearing something scaled and nameless had been dragging fishermen toward the bottom. The serpent that slept under the mountain, the one a whole village swore it saw rise the night the volcano should have erupted and didn't. The family that had healed with their hands for six generations and let no outsider ask how. The plague that emptied a street except for one house, with no doctor ever finding a sanitary reason for the exception. Dragon, saint, healer, selective plague — fossils of the same species, pulled from different strata. The scholar's proposal, made without raising his voice much: maybe these weren't different stories told by different people. Maybe they were the same substance, seen from climates and centuries that never got the chance to compare notes.

And he'd given that substance a name. **Odem**, borrowed from a root older than his own language — the breath that's lent, not owned, and can therefore be taken back, leaving whatever wore it as empty as if it had never been there. **Domain**, for when that breath found a stable form in a single person, recognizable from one episode to the next: the hand that heals, the one that's been curing for six generations with no family explanation for it. **Authority**, for when the breath dispensed with a human bearer entirely and organized itself alone, hungry the way the serpent under the mountain was hungry, until someone finally named it just to talk about it without pointing straight at it. And **Ruler**, for when that same thing stopped being one village's story and became something whole generations — never once agreeing among themselves — ended up calling by its proper name.

Park thought it was the kind of exercise a professor cites in a lecture and a student forgets before the final exam. Taxonomy without consequence. Well-chosen names for something that — if the scholar himself was right to doubt its cause, and the text admitted that doubt without flinching, almost with pride — might be nothing more than the old human habit of naming what it doesn't understand, until the name sounds enough like an explanation that it stops feeling like a question.

Park never asked that question out loud. He carried it inside the book instead, in the exact way he turned the pages: slow, lingering longer on the taxonomy than on the conclusions, as if a well-chosen name could prove something a reasonable doubt couldn't. He carried it, too, in a habit he'd picked up without deciding to — somewhere along the way, at a point he could no longer name precisely — of checking things after he read about them.

He read the paragraph about the cases that organized themselves on their own, twice. The second time, without noticing the shift, he stopped reading and started checking. The table under the book: solid. The chair under him: solid. The low hum of the refrigerator across the apartment: constant. Everything insisting on existing, whether he noticed or not, until a hundred-year-old treatise made him notice anyway.

He closed the book with his thumb still inside, marking the page.

---

The apartment measured forty-two square meters and had one window, facing east. That meant the morning light came in full for twenty minutes and then vanished for the rest of the day, leaving the remaining hours in a flat gray brightness with no sharp shadows — the kind of light that suited Park more than he'd ever admitted out loud. A bed. A table. Two chairs, though he'd never once had anyone sitting in the second. A kitchen small enough that cooking was an act of precision, not pleasure.

He ate standing at the counter, rice with what was left of a chicken roasted two days earlier, without turning on the television or the computer or anything that would fill the silence with something other than the sound of his own cutlery. It wasn't loneliness. Not entirely. It was a form of maintenance. A man who lives alone learns fast which parts of a routine exist for him and which exist so that someone watching from outside finds nothing worth writing down.

After eating, he washed the plate, set it face-down on a cloth, and sat at the table with a folder that hadn't fully closed in three weeks.

---

The folder had no name on the cover. Inside, sorted by date: printed local news clippings annotated by hand, screenshots from neighborhood forums where people talked about debts and about neighbors who weren't around anymore, and a spreadsheet Park had built himself, column by column, over two months of night shifts and calls nobody at Grayhaven knew he was making on their behalf.

The spreadsheet had a pattern. Park had spotted it the first week. The next eight had done nothing but confirm it, with a regularity that stopped looking like coincidence long before it turned into proof.

Kessler lent money to people no one else would touch. No bank paperwork, no uncomfortable questions — just interest that climbed every month a payment ran late, and that, sooner or later, stopped climbing because the debtor stopped existing in any sense a collector could chase. They didn't move away. They didn't turn up dead. One day, simply, the addresses Park had written down went quiet: neighbors who didn't remember anyone by that name ever living there, employers who couldn't find the file, phone numbers reassigned to someone else with no notice in between.

And every time it happened, somewhere in Kessler's books — Park had never seen the ledgers directly, only what leaked out in bar talk and the complaints of disgruntled employees — a debt got settled. Not forgiven. Not written off. *Settled*, as if someone had paid it on the debtor's behalf, in his name, with no record of who.

It was the kind of pattern that, in any police report, would land under unsolved disappearances and unexplained personal bankruptcies. Park had read it that way the first time, before he knew what he knew now. It was also the kind of pattern that, once you learned to read the right vocabulary, started looking a lot like something that hundred-year-old treatise had already described without naming it directly: people who stopped resisting, and something that carried off whatever was left.

He had no proof of Odem. No signature, no registered activation, nothing a Geneva evaluator would take as formal classification. He had a spreadsheet, and an instinct trained over years to distrust precisely the kind of pattern that looked too clean to be just bad people doing bad things.

And he had Oliver Webb.

---

The name had been in the folder since the third week, underlined once, no comment attached because none was needed. An employee of Kessler's who showed up on no official payroll. Who kept the most recent books in handwriting that didn't match the earlier ones. And who, on top of that — this was the part that had made Park request the desk right next to him at the Grayhaven internship instead of just watching from a safe distance — worked days at the very company Park had spent three months using as cover for something else entirely.

It hadn't been hard to arrange. A new intern, good references manufactured with AMAT's usual patience, a quiet word to HR about assigning him shared tasks with the new kid in reconciliation. Grayhaven had spent years producing grid anomalies nobody in the city bothered investigating past "overload" and "maintenance pending" — perfect ground for someone like Park to go unnoticed while he watched something else entirely.

Working side by side with Oliver Webb wasn't one method among several. It was the method. Sharing minor tasks. Giving him a ride home on the motorcycle when the train had already gone. Half-listening to his theories about "faults that behave." All of it was surveillance, as real as any data pull — just slower, and cheaper in resources AMAT had no intention of spending on a case with no official classification.

Park didn't know whether Oliver was a victim, an accomplice, or just someone too desperate to prop up an absent father's business to ask the right questions about who he was working for at night. He didn't need to know yet. He needed to watch, and wait for the pattern to produce something solid enough to bring to someone with the authority to act on it.

---

The day always started the same way: Oliver already seated when Park arrived, two minutes ahead of the official start, as if he worried that exact punctuality might look suspicious and extra punctuality wouldn't. Park had gotten used to reading that too — one more data point among the rest — without deciding yet what it meant.

They split the tasks nobody else wanted: reconciling backlogged records, reviewing alarms the night shift had dismissed, filling the gaps the system left between what it claimed had happened and what the grid had actually burned. Boring work, by design — Park had chosen it for exactly that reason. No one looks twice at the man stuck doing the job everyone else avoids.

Mid-morning, an overvoltage alarm tripped on a transformer in the east sector and cleared itself before anyone else saw it. Oliver flagged it on the screen without raising his voice, the same tone he'd have used to flag a typo, and wrote the exact time in a notebook that wasn't the official one. Park watched from the corner of his eye just long enough to confirm what he already suspected: it wasn't the first time Oliver had made that note. Seventh consecutive week, by his count.

At lunch, sitting at their usual table because by now it would have looked strange not to, Oliver talked numbers the way other people talked about the weather. He quoted a six-digit sequence from memory, from a report he'd read once, three days earlier. He didn't seem to notice he'd done it.

Park did.

The afternoon left the clearest mark. A coworker from another department, hauling a box of office supplies, tripped behind Oliver and sent a heavy stapler falling straight toward the back of his neck. Oliver stepped aside before anyone shouted a warning. Before, Park calculated afterward, there was even time to hear the fall and turn it into reflex. It wasn't elegant. It was fast in a way no sport explained, no self-defense class in a résumé Park had already memorized end to end.

Oliver apologized for a scare he hadn't caused. The coworker laughed, picked up the stapler, and no one else thought twice about it.

Park did. He logged the time, the exact shape of the movement, and a word he hadn't used yet anywhere in the folder: *anomalous*.

By the time the rest of the floor started packing up, Park's mental list had three new entries and no explanation simple enough to be luck or good reflexes. He stayed late, the way he did every evening, inventing backlog work until the last employees cleared out and the main corridor was down to a single light coming from one cubicle.

He walked over slowly — not hiding, not announcing himself either — and leaned against the edge of an empty cubicle with his hands in his pockets.

"You almost killed me," Oliver said, closing his laptop faster than necessary.

"You too. I thought everyone had gone." Park let his shoulders drop a centimeter, the exact gesture he'd spent three months practicing until it stopped feeling practiced. "Where do you live? I'll give you a ride."
