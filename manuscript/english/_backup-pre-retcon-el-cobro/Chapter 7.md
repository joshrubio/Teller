# Chapter 7 — The File

Voss arrived at the facility on a Tuesday, a fact he logged with no particular purpose, the same way he logged everything else: time, coordinates, the angle of light coming through the lobby's side windows. The building was a repurposed administrative structure, thirty kilometers from the Grayhaven district, built originally for a different function. The geometry of the corridors said as much clearly enough: all of them converged toward a central point that no longer existed, like spokes on a hub-less wheel. A bank, he guessed. Or something that had needed a security vault at its heart and had decided to make it invisible from outside.

The custody wing was a later addition. It showed in the change of proportions crossing the second door: the ceiling dropped twelve centimeters, the corridors narrowed by a measure calculated to produce a certain discomfort without being identifiable as deliberate. It was a design Voss knew — he'd seen it in four different AMAT facilities, always in active custody wings, always with the same signature. The logic was to suppress Domain activation through passive spatial disorientation.

It worked well for almost every Domain.

It wasn't relevant to the case he'd come to evaluate.

---

The room they'd assigned him had a table, two chairs, and a window facing outward onto a strip of ground between two concrete walls. The window was decorative: the angles were wrong for functional lighting. Someone had calculated that a window would make the room less claustrophobic without compromising the perimeter. It was a psychological decision disguised as architecture.

Voss sat down and opened the file.

*FILE GRH-094*
*Classification: TRIPLE-RESTRICTED*
*Nature of incident: Authority activation (Class III, Erasure Category). Entity Domain. Regional-scale blackout.*
*Civilian subject involved: designated GRH-094-S*
*Current status: Active custody. Evaluation in progress.*

He read the first page twice. Not because the content was difficult but because the first reading had given him the shape of the incident and he needed the second to verify that shape was what he thought it was.

A Class III Erasure Authority. In Grayhaven. Activated, per the report, by a civilian with no prior training or history in the agency's records.

The blackout that had hit the financial district for three hours had appeared in the media as a substation failure. Adequate coverage. What appeared in no media was that twelve blocks had operated simultaneously under the rules of an active Domain, and that the displaced — one hundred forty-three confirmed people in the report, though the real number was probably higher — had reappeared with the usual marks of exposure: temporal confusion, partial loss of mnemonic sequence, personal objects missing or displaced.

No confirmed deaths.

Voss noted that mentally. A Class III Authority with no deaths was unusual. Either the entity had discrimination capacity — rare, but documented — or something in the incident had modified its behavior before it reached full operational scale.

He moved to the second section.

---

The field operation had been assigned to a single agent. Voss found that less surprising than he should have. The North American branch had a documented tendency to underestimate initial classifications on the East Coast, and an Authority operating through omissions in electrical infrastructure records was exactly the kind of anomaly that spent weeks as a "monitoring alert" before anyone authorized direct intervention.

The field agent appeared referenced in the body of the report only as a footnote.

*³ Agent J. Park, AMAT-NA. Field level 4. No prior incidents. Protocol signals ignored during the Domain's active period — see disciplinary annex 7C. Currently assigned Hartford, CT.*

Voss read the note twice.

The protocol violation didn't interest him. Violations under active Domain conditions were, in his experience, more frequent than reports captured, and most responded to operational logics the standard form had no room to document. What interested him was Hartford.

Hartford was where the North American branch sent agents it wanted kept busy while it processed something it didn't want them complicating.

Agent Park had been in the Domain. He'd been with the subject through the entire activation period. And the agency had determined that the most efficient way to manage that was to send him to investigate something in Connecticut for as long as it took to put GRH-094-S out of his reach.

Voss folded that information away mentally and kept it.

The North American branch had operated for forty-two years under a mandate that distinguished it from the European branch from the start: field response, containment, normalization. It was an efficient model for most incidents — Class IV and III Authorities, localized thread anomalies, the recurring cases a city like Grayhaven generated with statistical regularity. Cartwright had built his entire institutional career inside that framework, and he'd done it well. Coverage of the Grayhaven incident was technically flawless. Handling of the displaced, correct. The field agent contained before he could become a vector of uncontrolled information.

Everything exactly as it should be, up to the point where the system had found something the model didn't account for.

Voss had known Cartwright for fifteen years. They'd crossed paths at three intercontinental coordination conferences and one shared field situation in Lisbon that neither of them had documented to the level of detail the protocol recommended. Their relationship was cordial in the specific way relationships between professionals who respect each other without needing to understand each other are cordial. Cartwright managed threats. Voss evaluated categories. They were complementary functions that rarely needed to coordinate directly.

The referral of case GRH-094 hadn't been Cartwright's decision. It had been an automatic activation of the classification system: the Registry hypothesis in the report had triggered the referral protocol to a senior evaluator, and Cartwright's signature on the transfer document was that of someone who had approved the process without pausing over its contents. Voss knew this because the attached cover note used the term *"non-standard field anomaly"* — the vocabulary of someone who hadn't read past the first page.

It wasn't a reproach. It was simply a description of how AMAT-NA worked.

---

The third section was what had justified the trip from Geneva.

*GRH-094-S: male, twenty-two. No prior AMAT history. No known markers for latent Domain. Practitioner at electrical infrastructure company, Grayhaven. First documented contact with anomalous activity seventy-two hours before the main incident — grid record analysis showing omissions consistent with early trials of the Authority.*

*Exposure during the incident: direct contact with active Odem. Estimated duration: ninety minutes. Observed manifestations:*

*— Visual perception of Odem filaments following electrical infrastructure (onset: approximately forty minutes into Domain entry)*
*— Reading of the Authority's behavior patterns through direct Odem observation*
*— Instinctive unbinding of the Authority's anchor node (mechanism: not replicated under controlled conditions)*

*Result: neutralization of the entity. Resulting mass blackout.*

*Capacity classification: PENDING. Working hypothesis: Registry Domain.*

Voss stopped at the last paragraph.

Registry Domain was a classification that appeared in the agency's internal literature in two specific contexts: as a historical category documented three times over the past eighty years, and as a warning in the evaluation protocol — *if Registry Domain is suspected, refer immediately to a senior-level evaluator*.

There were three senior-level evaluators in AMAT with experience in Registry classifications.

He was one of them.

He read the section again, more slowly.

*Instinctive unbinding of the anchor node.* Not the most operationally striking part, though it was the most impressive in terms of result. A Class III Authority didn't yield to brute force — what GRH-094-S had done wasn't to strike the anchor but to unravel the structure that kept it coherent. The difference was substantial. Any agent with sufficient Domain level could apply pressure to an anchor. Nobody without specific training knew where the anchor was, what shape it had, or exactly which point made it vulnerable.

Unless he had *read* the Authority.

Not the surface behavior. Not the visible effects on the environment. The underlying structure. The pattern that made the entity what it was.

Voss closed the file.

---

He sat looking at the cover for a moment.

In his line of work — and "line" was a word he used more literally than most people understood — the difference between a phenomenon and a variable was simple: a phenomenon occurred, a variable could be employed. Authorities were phenomena. Field agents with combat Domains were tools. What the file described in GRH-094-S was another category.

If the Registry Domain worked the way the theory indicated — accumulation of foreign structure, pattern copying, functional reproduction — then GRH-094-S wasn't an anomaly to contain.

He was a capability-acquisition system with no classified ceiling.

The North American branch had put him in active custody and ordered a standard evaluation. It was the correct response for something they didn't understand. The problem was that "standard evaluation" assumed a subject with a defined Domain, known rules, and an estimable capacity limit.

None of those three conditions applied here.

Voss wondered whether Cartwright had understood what he was referring to his desk, or whether he'd simply executed the protocol without reading past the pending classification.

Probably the latter.

The European branch had spent eighty years documenting Registry cases for a reason AMAT-NA had never needed to understand: the three previous historical cases had all occurred on European soil, in a forty-year span running from the postwar period to the late eighties. Voss had read the complete files. They were uncomfortable reading, not for what they said about the subjects but for what they said about how the agency had managed them.

The first had died during evaluation. Not through direct negligence, but because no one had understood that a Registry Domain in a developmental phase couldn't be subjected to the same suppression protocols as any other Domain — suppression didn't deactivate the Registry, it forced it to search the immediate environment for material to copy, and the immediate environment was the evaluation team. The final report described the result with the clinical terminology the agency used to avoid saying what had happened.

The second had disappeared from custody seventeen months after his capture. Never located. The file remained technically open.

The third was the only case the agency considered a success, in the sense that the subject had survived, had been fully evaluated, and had ended up working for the Geneva branch for eleven years before retiring for medical reasons unspecified in the accessible record. Voss knew what those reasons were because he'd met the subject briefly, at the end. They weren't reasons that appeared on any form.

None of that background would appear in the file AMAT-NA had prepared for GRH-094-S. Not because they'd deliberately hidden it, but because the North American branch simply didn't have access to the archive level where it was stored.

Voss had decided to come in person for that reason. The last time Geneva had sent an evaluation team for a Registry case, the team had come back with incomplete data and the subject had disappeared six months later.

That wasn't going to happen again.

---

He knocked on the door. From outside, his assistant — a woman with short hair Voss had never seen without a tablet in her hands — opened it without waiting for confirmation.

"Is the subject available for direct evaluation?"

"Standard protocol establishes forty-eight hours of acclimation before first contact with—"

"I know. Is he available?"

A brief pause.

"Medically stable for seventy-two hours. Protocol recommends—"

"Prepare access," Voss said, standing. "Fifteen minutes."

---

The custody wing corridor measured a hundred and four meters from the briefing room to the evaluation cell. Voss walked it at a steady pace, hands crossed behind his back, the file under his arm.

The corridor's geometry changed twice over that distance. Both changes were deliberate: a four-degree angle at the first bend, a twenty-centimeter narrowing on the second stretch. Mild vestibular disturbance. Enough that someone with an active Domain would feel diffuse resistance, a subtle friction against spontaneous activation. It was the same signature he'd registered entering the wing. It was, as he'd calculated then, entirely irrelevant to the kind of Domain this held.

Registry didn't activate in space.

It activated in observation.

Whoever had designed the containment hadn't understood that, which meant they'd built a cage for the wrong bird.

He stopped in front of the last door.

Through the observation window, GRH-094-S sat on the edge of the bed, elbows on his knees, staring at a spot on the floor that held nothing visible. Twenty-two years old. Brown hair, nearly blond, deep circles under his eyes. A cut healing on his right cheekbone, already scabbing over, and a bruise turning from purple to yellow along his left cheekbone — the kind of mark that takes days to reach that phase, meaning these weren't injuries from medical processing but earlier ones, from the incident itself.

Someone had put him in custody without fully cleaning up his face.

Voss noted it.

He watched the posture a moment longer.

It was the posture of someone who'd spent days waiting for something without knowing exactly what. It was also, with nuances most evaluators wouldn't have caught, the posture of an organism guarding something. Not rest. Inverted concentration. The body protecting what it couldn't afford to lose.

He'd seen that posture in agents who had pushed their Domain past the safety threshold.

That an untrained civilian had it, days after the incident, was a data point.

Voss rested his hand on the access panel.

The door opened.
