# Chapter 3 — Lines

They regrouped in a side alley, out of the main current of fleeing people. Thirty seconds, no more. Park set his back against the wall and assessed his own shoulder with an economy of movement that suggested this wasn't the first time he'd had to run that diagnostic in situations like this.

"It works," he said, before Oliver could ask. "I just need to not use it as a lever for a while."

"How long is a while?"

"However long we get."

Oliver nodded and leaned against the opposite wall. His body took a second to report the full inventory: knees still shaking, pulse hammering in his ears harder than he wanted to admit, a metallic taste at the back of his throat that wasn't blood but pure adrenaline looking for a way out. He'd been in high-pressure situations before — impossible deadlines, systems down, the dull urgency of infrastructure failing in real time. None of them had resembled this.

He looked at the street.

From the roof, the lines had looked almost geometric. Clean, spaced, following the city's grid with a logic that was almost legible. From here they were something else. Denser, faster, pulsing at intervals Oliver couldn't fully predict. The Domain's front kept advancing, legible now in the filaments stretching between the city's infrastructure. Every power cable, every underground pipe, every tram rail was a conductor, a channel through which the Authority extended its territory into blocks it hadn't reached yet. And at every point where that network touched something still, that something stopped existing.

"It's using the power grid to expand," Oliver said. "Same as at the substation. The omissions I kept finding were this. Charges nobody saw."

Park looked at him.

"How long have you been seeing that?"

"The lines. Since the roof, more or less. Down here they're clearer."

Park didn't answer right away. He was looking at the point in the air where Oliver had pointed, seeing nothing, with the expression of someone recalibrating something he'd thought he had classified.

"Don't look at them too long at a stretch," he said finally.

"Why not?"

Park seemed to weigh how much to explain. It was the kind of pause Oliver had learned to read in the scant week they'd been working together: not hesitation, but selection. Park didn't hesitate. He filtered.

"Because what you're doing isn't only seeing," he said. "And you still don't know what you're doing."

He didn't elaborate. He pushed off the wall and pointed north, toward blocks the Domain hadn't reached yet.

"We need to get to more open ground. Less fixed infrastructure, fewer attack vectors."

Oliver went. Not because he'd decided to trust Park, but because Park was right and moving was better than standing there processing what he'd just heard.

---

It didn't erase by force.

Park understood this while they moved through the last blocks of the financial district, the ground vanishing at irregular intervals around them and the sound of the impacts piling up in the air like something percussive and without rhythm. The earlier attacks at the building had been direct, almost clumsy: jaws closing on the space where they stood. This was different. The Authority didn't chase. It separated. It erased footholds, stable surfaces, anything someone could stop on or grab, building an environment where standing still was impossible and moving was getting harder.

"It doesn't erase by force," Park said through his teeth, not stopping. "It erases when something stops pushing back."

Oliver took a second.

"Pushing...?"

"If something doesn't move, if it's not connected to anything that resists" — Park tilted his chin toward an antenna that had just bent and vanished—"it lets go. Like holding up everything in the world takes constant effort, and it just stops doing that at specific points."

Oliver processed it without breaking stride.

It fit. It fit with a precision that produced a cold distinct from the cold of fear — the cold of when something you've spent a long time not understanding suddenly has an explanation, and the explanation is worse than the ignorance. The substation omissions weren't system errors. They never had been. They were receipts. Every flow lost toward the node that didn't exist was the trace of a collection — a point in the grid where that thing had passed to settle an entry in a ledger Oliver knew too well. He'd documented the omissions, hunted for patterns, even cross-checked the dates. And he hadn't seen what it was.

He'd been staring at its footprints without understanding that he was walking in them.

It explained the briefcase. It explained the shadow. And it explained something else he hadn't put into words yet: through the entire chase, the direct attacks on him had been few. Blows fell around him, contractions erased whatever was beside him, but there was a margin between Oliver and the center of every attack that he couldn't chalk up to luck.

He didn't say it. He kept moving.

---

The next building shuddered under their feet as they crossed beneath its marquee. The lines began appearing not only on the ground but along the walls, spreading like invisible cracks trying to surround them from several angles at once. For the first time, the pressure wasn't coming from a single point.

It was closing around.

"It's boxing us in," Oliver said.

It wasn't panic. It was an observation: the lines had a direction of convergence, and that direction was them. Park didn't answer right away. His eyes swept the surroundings with cold speed, calculating. He didn't see what Oliver saw, but he read the result — surfaces vanishing, structures giving way, the space shrinking with every block.

While he calculated, he also ran the other inventory, the one he didn't like doing in front of anyone: how much he had left.

The Strength Domain wasn't unlimited. It worked in cycles — inertia captured, compressed, spent. Every change of direction in the air, every lateral push off a facade, every absorbed impact converted into useful motion had cost something. The compromised shoulder limited accumulation on that side; compensating required more concentration and more expenditure. He'd started the day with full reserves. Now he estimated he was at forty percent, maybe less.

Forty percent was enough to keep moving. It wasn't enough for a direct confrontation.

The problem was that a direct confrontation wouldn't have accomplished anything either way. The protocol laid it out clearly — find the anchor, separate the entity from what kept it coherent, let it collapse without support. Park had read it. He'd trained on it. Until today he'd never needed to apply it in the field. Brute force didn't work against something that erased whatever stopped resisting. Attacking directly was surrendering.

This Authority's anchor was somewhere in the Domain. There was always an anchor — it was the one part of the protocol Park trusted absolutely. What he didn't know was where. And without knowing, with the Domain closing in and the shoulder limiting his options, the possibilities came down to two: keep moving until they found an opening, or wait for the kid to see something Park couldn't.

Of those two options, Park had a clear preference.

The other one worried him more.

Then he saw it: zones where the filaments formed more slowly, where reality offered more resistance. Places where the environment was still in constant vibration — light signals oscillating, loose cables striking the air, damaged structures that hadn't settled yet.

Zones where the world was still moving.

"That way," he said, pointing to a street below where several fallen power poles were still swaying between dying sparks.

They didn't come down the normal way. Park sharply cut the acceleration of their bodies mid-jump, the fall turning into an irregular descent. Halfway down he pushed off a building's facade, changing direction with a dry blow that left cracks in the concrete, and they landed in the middle of the street.

The ground was covered in taut cables, vibrating metal panels, and debris from structures that hadn't settled yet. Everything gave off small, constant movements, a continuous tremor.

The pressure disappeared.

Oliver took a moment to realize he saw no line forming around them.

"It can't reach us here," he murmured.

Park didn't drop his guard. His eyes rose to the buildings around them.

"It can," he answered. "It just needs to separate us first."

And at that same moment, the cables around them began pulling taut toward a single invisible point, vibrating less and less, as if some force were forcing them still.

---

That was when Oliver saw it clearly.

Not the cracks or fractures from before. Something more precise. The lines he'd been tracking through the city's infrastructure were visible here too now, but different: shorter, denser, converging from the cables and metal surfaces toward a point in the air where nothing was visible.

And not only in the structures.

Three blocks away, where the Domain's front reached a new intersection, Oliver could see them in people too. Filaments converging from the ground and the buildings toward figures that had stopped, that had turned to see where the screams were coming from. The lines arrived first. The people disappeared after.

But no line converged toward him.

Not a single one.

Oliver processed it with the part of his brain that kept functioning as if this were all a network problem, an anomaly with a technical explanation. He scanned the space around him with his eyes: the filaments grazed the perimeter of Park's body, tested it, pulled slightly in his direction. But around Oliver the space stayed clean. Not empty — empty would have been normal, would have been the default state of someone with nothing the Authority could read. This was different. The lines approached and then veered off with an intent that couldn't be accidental, like water skirting an obstacle it can't pass through.

The Authority wasn't ignoring him.

It was *avoiding* him.

The difference between the two things was small and it was enormous, and Oliver felt it somewhere between his sternum and his throat — the specific sensation of being recognized by something that shouldn't know you existed.

Park looked at him.

"It's not attacking you," he said, low. "Have you noticed?"

"I just did."

Park didn't answer right away. He looked at the space around Oliver, then at the lines Oliver could see and he couldn't. There was something in how he looked at him that was different from every other time Park had looked at him — not the agent's assessment of whether the civilian was going to be a problem, but something more careful. Like someone who has just discovered that the variable he was managing isn't the variable he thought it was.

"That's not good," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because it means it still doesn't know how to handle you. And things that don't know how to handle something learn."

It was logical. It was exactly the kind of logic he'd applied to the ledgers for weeks — a system detecting an anomaly and starting to adjust its behavior until it found a way to resolve it. Except this time the system was an entity of urban scale and the anomaly was him.

"How long does it take to learn?"

Park opened his mouth to answer.

And in that instant, one of the lines in front of them pulled taut in the air, cutting across the edge of the building they were running toward.

The concrete began to give.

"Park, get down!"

Oliver said it before understanding why he was saying it. Before the process of *I've seen a line, that means an erasure is imminent* had finished completing. The warning came out on its own.

Park didn't hesitate. He changed course in the last second, dragging Oliver along as the ground where they would have landed vanished without leaving a speck of dust.

As they crossed through the air, Park understood something he hadn't expected.

The kid wasn't seeing the attack.

He was seeing **how it was born.**
