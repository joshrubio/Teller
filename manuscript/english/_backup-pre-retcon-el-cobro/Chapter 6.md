# Chapter 6 — Hartford

They arrived in two unmarked vehicles.

Park heard them before he saw them, the sound of tires on wet asphalt moving with too much precision to be ordinary traffic in the middle of a blackout. He stepped away from the wall and waited standing, with Oliver still unconscious behind him, in the posture the agency called delivery position: neutral, hands visible, no signals that could read as resistance or alarm.

Four agents. Two from the first vehicle, two from the second, moving in the pattern he recognized from civilian containment protocol: perimeter first, subject after. No uniforms. They wore something worse than a uniform, the kind of clothing that doesn't draw attention precisely because it's designed not to.

The one who reached Park first wasn't the highest-ranking. It never was. The highest-ranking one waited for the others to confirm the situation was what the report said it was.

"Agent Park?"

"Yes."

"The civilian?"

Park indicated Oliver with a minimal tilt of his head. The agent approached, pulled out a device, and passed it over Oliver's body without touching him, reading something on the screen Park couldn't see from his angle.

"Readings consistent with direct Odem exposure," the agent said, more for the record than for Park. "Active manifestation confirmed."

A woman got out of the second vehicle, hair pulled back, a tablet in her hand. This one was the rank. Park knew it from the way the other four adjusted their posture when she crossed the perimeter.

"Verbal report," she said, without introducing herself.

Park gave it in the right order: Domain activation, containment protocol initiated, nature of the Authority, unanticipated escalation, neutralization. He left out the moment he'd ignored the agency's first alert. Not because he thought they didn't know, but because the verbal report wasn't the place for that conversation.

The woman listened without interrupting. When Park finished, she wrote something on the tablet.

"Was the civilian informed of the nature of the operation?"

"No."

"Did he act under your direction during the neutralization?"

Park weighed the question for a second.

"He acted on his own initiative. I wasn't able to stop him."

The woman looked up from the tablet.

"Weren't able, or chose not to?"

It was a good question. Park didn't answer right away, which was itself already an answer.

"The circumstances didn't allow for direct intervention," he said finally.

The woman looked at him for a moment. Not the look of someone who doesn't believe him — the look of someone who knows exactly what an answer is leaving out and is deciding whether it's worth pushing now or saving for the formal report.

"When was the first time the civilian showed indicators of non-standard capacity?"

Park kept his expression neutral.

"During the active incident."

"Not before?"

"Not in a way that could be classified as an operational indicator."

The woman wrote something down. Park recognized that note — it wasn't transcription, it was a follow-up flag. The conversation she'd have with Park in the next forty-eight hours, when there was time to do it properly and with the complete report in front of her.

"Did you have personal contact with the civilian outside the operation perimeter?"

"Undercover coworkers. Contact within mission protocol."

"Only that?"

The question was precise. Park let it sit a second.

"Only that."

The woman wrote something more and looked up again.

"We'll take the civilian into agency custody for evaluation." She raised her eyes. "You'll receive a new assignment in forty-eight hours. Remain available."

It was an order phrased as information. Park nodded.

---

Two of the agents approached Oliver with a folding stretcher they'd pulled from the trunk of the second vehicle. They handled him efficiently, without roughness but without anything resembling care. Oliver didn't move through the process. His breathing stayed shallow, regular, the kind of breathing a body maintains when it has decided to invest all its resources elsewhere.

Park watched.

The team's efficiency was impeccable. Two people, four movements, thirty seconds from the order to the deployed stretcher. They'd done this before, many times, and it showed — not in the speed but in the absence of any friction. No extra gesture, no unnecessary word, no glance between them looking for confirmation. It was the kind of competence that only exists when a task has been repeated until it becomes mechanical, and Park had to remind himself that this was exactly what he wanted: someone who knew what they were doing taking care of Oliver.

What he couldn't control was the way that efficiency erased every trace of the night before. The kid who had jumped eight floors, who had seen lines in a city's infrastructure, who had pulled on something invisible with his hands and brought down a Class III Authority, was now a subject on a stretcher. A number on a tablet. A pending classification.

There was a moment in containment missions, a specific moment academy instructors described as the transfer threshold, when the field agent's responsibility for the subject formally passed to the custody team. It was more an administrative moment than a real one, a line on a form nobody signed in the field. But Park always felt it with a clarity he found inconvenient.

He felt it now, as they lifted the stretcher and Oliver hung a meter and a half off the ground, motionless, the line of dried blood still visible on his upper lip.

The transfer threshold.

---

One of the agents was about to carry the stretcher to the vehicle when Oliver opened his eyes.

Not all the way. Only a fraction, his eyelids rising just enough to let in the light from the emergency flashlights the agents wore on their belts. His pupils took a second to adjust — the slow kind of adjustment Park associated with someone returning from very far away.

They found Park.

He said nothing. He didn't ask where he was or what was happening or who these people were. Part of Park had anticipated that he would — that there would be a question, or an attempt to sit up, or something that required managing. But Oliver did none of those things. He just looked at Park with the kind of look that doesn't need to voice the question because the question is already answered by the fact of asking it.

*What's happening?* And also: *Did you know this was going to happen?*

Park didn't look away.

There were things he could have said in that moment. Some true, some that would have offered comfort without being it. Park chose the only one that was both at once.

"Rest," he said.

Oliver looked at him a second longer. Then closed his eyes.

They loaded him into the vehicle. The door closed. The first car pulled away thirty seconds later, and the second waited for Park to step back from the perimeter before following it.

Park stayed on the empty street.

---

The second vehicle turned the corner and disappeared. The other two agents broke down the perimeter with the same efficiency with which they'd set it up: packed the equipment, confirmed something on their devices, exchanged two low words Park didn't try to hear. They got into the first vehicle. Drove off.

Thirty seconds later, Park was the only person on that street who knew exactly what had happened there.

He stood for a moment, hands in his pockets. His right shoulder reminded him it had absorbed a direct impact he'd have to report on the medical form. Under normal conditions it was the kind of injury he wouldn't have ignored — not because of the pain, which was manageable, but because the shoulder affected Domain accumulation and Park was methodical about his own inventory of capacities. Tonight hadn't been a night of normal conditions.

Across the twelve blackout blocks, Grayhaven was beginning the process of building itself a version of events it could fit inside what it could understand. Substation failure, probably. Or grid overload. Something with a technical cause and a technical solution and no element requiring further explanation.

Park knew that process. It was part of the job — not his, directly, but the normalization teams who would arrive before dawn. By the time the city woke up, Grayhaven would have a story. The displaced would have a story. Oliver Webb would have a story, though he didn't know which one yet.

His device buzzed. The blackout was still active across the twelve blocks of the financial district. In the distance, the lights of municipal emergency vehicles were starting to cluster at the perimeter, disoriented, not sure exactly where to look.

His device buzzed.

It was a message, not a call. Two lines.

*New assignment. Hartford, Connecticut. Briefing in 36h.*

Park read the message twice. Hartford was a lesser-category mission — the kind of assignment given to agents who needed time to stabilize after a major incident, or to agents the agency wanted to keep busy and out of the way while it managed something it didn't want complicated.

Both options were possible. The first was more comfortable. The second was more likely.

He pocketed the device.

He walked to the vehicle he'd parked himself hours earlier, before any of this had started, before the terminal and the name spoken aloud and the eight floors jumped with someone who shouldn't have survived any of it. The windshield had a thin layer of dust the blackout hadn't caused but the darkness made more visible. Park stood looking at it for a moment before opening the door.

He got behind the wheel.

He didn't start the engine right away.

What the agency was going to do with Oliver depended on what Oliver turned out to be, and what Oliver turned out to be depended on how they classified what they'd seen in him tonight. Park had deliberately given an incomplete verbal report. Not to protect him — that would be too direct a motive for something he still couldn't name — but because premature classification closed off options, and right now all Park wanted was for the options to stay open long enough for someone with more information than him to make the decisions.

Hartford was the agency making sure that someone wasn't Park.

He understood it. He didn't like it, but he understood it. It was the rational response to an agent who had ignored seven alerts and let an unclassified civilian neutralize a Class III Authority. From the outside, the only possible reading was that Park had lost control of the situation. From the inside, Park knew there had been no situation to control — there had been something that fit no protocol he knew, and the only decision he'd made consciously was not to stop it.

That distinction wasn't going to appear in any report the way he saw it.

He looked at the street through the windshield. The displaced kept reappearing at scattered points across the district, confused, some searching for phones that no longer worked. Grayhaven was learning what had happened in real time, building the version of events that fit inside what it could understand.

Park started the engine.

Hartford, Connecticut. Thirty-six hours.

Enough time for the agency to finish deciding what Oliver was.

Enough time, too, for Oliver to decide whether that mattered.

Park left the district through the only exit the emergency vehicles hadn't blocked yet, and didn't look back.
