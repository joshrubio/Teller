# Chapter 2 — Propagation

The office had been talking about the same thing all morning.

"My sister lives in Halloway, she says the power went out four times between one and five." Someone in the next cubicle, coffee still steaming. "Four times. And each time on a different block, like it was taking turns."

"Cascade overload, it was on the news."

"An overload that moves in a straight line across Breakwater and ends at the bridge? That's not an overload, that's someone cutting cables with a map."

Oliver listened without lifting his eyes from the screen, his untouched coffee going cold beside the keyboard. He had slept less than an hour sitting up on the six o'clock train, and the whine behind his right eye hadn't entirely gone — it had only become discreet enough to pass for tiredness. Around him, half the floor was speculating with the confidence of people who don't have to live with the answer. Nobody mentioned the industrial district. Nobody asked why the substation grid, the same one he had spent weeks reconciling, hadn't logged a single one of the events the whole city had seen with its own eyes.

As far as the system was concerned, the night hadn't happened.

He opened the reconciliation run out of habit more than hope, and left it loading while he pretended to work on something else.

The blackout lasted exactly four seconds.

Oliver counted them without knowing why, as if the number would explain something. Four seconds of total darkness, without the hum of the building, without the flicker of the emergency monitors, without anything. And then the lights came back.

But not everything came back with them.

The room looked the same. Screens on, cubicles in place, the white light falling on the keyboard in front of him. But the background murmur was gone. The typing, the low conversations, the constant hum of the servers in the adjoining room. All of that had been part of the air of that building since Oliver's first day, and now it wasn't there.

He stood up. The room had capacity for forty people. There had been sixteen cubicles occupied when the power went out.

Now there were only two.

Park was standing beside the emergency door, back against the frame, watching Oliver with an expression that didn't fit the situation. It wasn't alarm. It was something colder. The kind of calm that isn't composure but training.

In his pocket, something buzzed.

He didn't take it out.

"It didn't come for the building," he said without raising his voice. "It came for you."

Oliver took a second.

"What?"

"Last night half the city went dark in order, block by block." Park pushed off the frame. "The order had a direction. And the direction was you."

"How do you know—"

"Later."

Oliver felt the question come out on its own, the same one as always, the one that had gotten him into all this:

"What is it? Everything that exists has a name."

Park opened his mouth to answer.

Then the far wall folded.

Not inward or outward. Toward a point. As if something on the other side had closed a fist around the brick and squeezed. The plaster cracked, the pipes behind the partition groaned with a wet metallic sound, and the entire material converged on an angle physics didn't allow before being torn away with a blow that made the floor shake under their feet. Where there had been wall, a clean edge remained. Beyond it there was no outside. No city, no sky, none of the usual noise of the street.

Only absence, with a perfect geometry.

Oliver had no time to process the horror of that edge. Park was already moving, the floor cracking beneath his feet with a sound impossible for a human body, and the impact came like a squall: Park hauling him sideways with a force that nearly put him on the ground. Oliver's desk folded in on itself behind him, the edges converging toward a point like twisted cloth, and was torn out of reality with a dry crack Oliver felt in his sternum even from two meters away.

"What—"

"Stairs. Now."

---

They got down two floors before the Authority found them again.

It happened on the eighth-floor landing. Oliver heard the crack first, the same wet metallic sound as the lab wall, and when he turned his head he saw the stair railing bending toward them from the flight below, the steel twisting into impossible angles before being torn from the floor with a blow that shook the whole building. The steps beneath that flight began to darken, losing texture, and Park pulled Oliver into the side corridor half a second before they vanished.

Where there had been a staircase, there was air.

"They're not going to work," Park said, calculating the situation out loud as they moved down the corridor. "It's using the structure of the building. Anything fixed is a target."

"What do we do?"

"Go up."

Oliver looked at him.

"We just came down two floors."

"I know. Plan's changed."

---

In the ninth-floor corridor, two people were standing beside the coffee machine. Oliver recognized them: colleagues from the infrastructure team, two guys who always ate together and came in late on Mondays. Completely motionless, staring toward the window at the end of the corridor with the kind of paralysis that comes before the brain decides what to feel.

Oliver slowed.

"Wait—"

"We can't," Park answered, without cruelty or hurry. "If they move, good. If not, we can't carry two people while trying not to disappear ourselves."

Oliver looked back as they kept climbing.

The space around the two of them began to curve.

He kept climbing.

---

The roof received them with the cold off the harbor and the whole city spread out beneath them.

Grayhaven looked normal at first glance. The traffic, the sidewalks full of people, the buildings with their windows lit. But there was something wrong in the distribution of people in the streets, an irregular geometry in the clusters of pedestrians, gaps that didn't correspond to any traffic light or crossing. Spaces with the exact shape of a human body, empty, distributed across the visible blocks as if someone had erased specific figures from a photograph and left the background intact.

Oliver narrowed his eyes trying to understand what he was seeing, and that was when he noticed it.

It wasn't only the gaps.

Between the buildings, following the run of the power cables and the underground pipes and the elevated rail lines, there was something. Not exactly visible, not exactly invisible. Filaments of something connecting the points where the world was starting to behave badly, a network underlying the geography of the city like a second infrastructure. When Oliver tried to follow one with his eyes he felt a dull pressure behind them, like straining a muscle he hadn't known he had.

He looked away.

Below, in the street directly beneath the roof, a woman stopped to look for something in her bag.

The space around her contracted without warning.

There was no progressive curvature. The air collapsed inward in a fraction of a second, the sound arriving dry and brutal all the way up to the roof, and the woman stopped being there. The bag fell to the ground. The nearest people took three seconds to notice.

Then one of them stopped to look.

And disappeared too.

Then another.

The cadence began to accelerate. One, a pause, two more, pause, three. The panic spread in waves from the intersection, and the people who ran survived without understanding it. The ones who stopped to look disappeared, and that generated more witnesses who stopped, and more gaps, and more noise.

Oliver stepped back from the edge.

He doubled over and brought up his morning coffee.

Park waited, saying nothing, looking at the horizon with that calculating expression Oliver was beginning to learn to read without yet understanding what it was calculating.

When Oliver straightened up, the back of his hand against his mouth and his eyes watering, that was when he saw it. Or rather, when he saw what was missing.

Oliver's shadow wasn't where it should have been.

It was where it had been a second earlier. Stuck to the ground, out of phase by nearly half a meter, adhered to the concrete like a forgotten stain. And as Oliver watched it, it began to come apart. It didn't dissolve. It peeled away from the world, fragmenting into finer and finer filaments until the concrete was clean again, intact, as if it had never had anything cast onto it.

"Park..."

"I saw it."

Oliver waited for him to say something more. He didn't.

"What does it mean?"

Park looked at him for a moment. Something crossed his expression that never quite became an answer.

"It means we need to move," he said finally.

Before Oliver could press, the roof floor cracked.

The crack started at the north edge and traveled toward them in less than a second, the concrete darkening as something invisible pressed from below. Oliver tried to follow with his eyes the filaments he had seen earlier between the buildings and found them now converging toward the roof, toward them, pulling at the structure of the building from inside. The pressure behind his eyes when he strained them was different here, denser.

Park grabbed Oliver by the arm.

"The building isn't safe anymore. We're going down."

"By the stairs?"

"Not that way." He gestured with his head toward the south edge of the roof. "That way."

Oliver looked at the edge. Then looked down. Eight floors. The street in chaos, cars piling up, people running in every direction.

"That's eight floors."

"I know."

"We can't—"

"I can," Park said, with a calm more alarming than any alarm. "And if you hold on tight, so can you."

The floor gave way beneath their feet in a three-meter radius, the concrete torn downward with that dry, brutal crack. Park didn't hesitate. He pulled Oliver toward the edge, gathered something Oliver couldn't see but could feel, a growing pressure in the air around Park like the moment before lightning falls, and they jumped.

The world disappeared beneath their feet.

For an instant there was only wind, height, and the sound of the street coming up too fast. Oliver felt Park's hand in his shirt, the fabric stretching to its limit, and then the fall changed. It didn't stop. It transformed, the velocity redistributing in a way Oliver couldn't describe except that his stomach stopped falling even though his body kept going down. Park pushed off the building's facade halfway down, a dry impact that left cracks in the concrete and changed the angle, and they landed.

Their feet hit the ground with a violence that should have broken their ankles.

It didn't.

Oliver took two seconds to convince himself he was still in one piece.

"Deceleration," Park said, letting go of him. "That way I can use the inertia I generate and save it for later."

He didn't wait for a reply. He pointed toward the main street and started walking.

---

The chaos of the street was different experienced from inside it.

From the roof it had had a legible geometry: waves, fronts, the methodical advance of the gaps block by block. Down here it was simply people running in every direction, without knowing from what or toward where, colliding with each other, shouting names. The cars kept moving because the engines hadn't stopped, but some of them no longer had drivers and rolled on alone until they hit other vehicles or the curb with an indifferent crunch.

Park moved with a precision Oliver tried to replicate without quite managing it. He changed direction without warning, adjusted his step half a second before something beside him stopped existing. Oliver followed his trajectory as best he could, looking not at the people but at the spaces between them, at that network of filaments he could now make out more clearly at street level, following the buried power cables, the pipes, the tram rails. When the filaments converged toward a point, that point disappeared. It was that simple and that horrible.

"There's a pattern," Oliver said, almost to himself.

"What do you see?"

"Lines. They follow the infrastructure." He pointed toward the nearest streetlight. "That one's connected. In a moment—"

The streetlight gave way.

It didn't fall. It was torn from its base with a force that threw it toward the roadway in a wide arc Oliver hadn't anticipated. Park shoved him backward before he finished processing the movement, and the metal passed less than a meter from both of them with a whistle that cut the air.

"Good," Park said, straightening up. "Keep watching."

That was when Oliver saw the man.

In the middle of the current of people fleeing in opposite directions, there was a man of about fifty in a gray jacket with a briefcase still in his hand. He wasn't running. He was standing in the middle of the sidewalk, feet planted, staring at the space where a shop window had just stopped existing. The kind of paralysis that comes when the mind decides what it's seeing is impossible and waits for reality to correct the error.

Oliver saw the filaments converging toward him.

They weren't faint. They were clear, taut, pulling the surrounding infrastructure toward the man's motionless body with an urgency Oliver knew how to read for what it was: time counted, in seconds.

He pulled free of Park.

"Oliver—"

He crossed the three meters and shoved the man with both hands. The man stumbled forward, his feet started moving again, and in that same instant the reality behind him contracted with the familiar blow Oliver was already expecting but which still landed in his chest. The briefcase, which had fallen to the ground and was still, disappeared in that same contraction.

The man turned around. His eyes were very wide and he had an expression Oliver recognized because it was the one he must have had himself, except that in the man's eyes there was something slightly out of focus, as though the threshold he had brushed against had shifted something inside his gaze.

"Run," Oliver told him. "Don't stop. Whatever happens, keep moving."

The man nodded, slowly, and ran.

Park was already beside him.

"Next time tell me," he said. "Not for protocol. Because if you stay here alone for two seconds, I'm going to have to look for you somewhere else."

Before Oliver could answer, a driverless car crossed the intersection to their right at constant speed, indifferent to the traffic lights and to the people scrambling out of the way with shouts. Park pulled Oliver toward the nearest doorway and the car passed grazing the curb where they had been, colliding further on with a delivery truck with a metallic crash that echoed down the whole block.

Then came the direct attack.

It didn't come from the ground or the walls. It came from the air between the two buildings in front of them, the space compressing all at once toward Park with a speed that left no time to dodge. Park raised an arm on instinct and the contraction hit him in the shoulder, tearing him off the ground and throwing him against the facade of the building behind him with an impact that rattled the ground-floor glass.

He fell to the ground. He got up immediately, but something had changed in his posture.

"Go," he said, his voice slightly tight.

"I'm not going to leave you—"

"I'm not asking you to leave me. I'm telling you to move, because if we both stay here it's going to come back for both of us."

The space between them began to curve.

Oliver ran.

---

It was thirty meters of separation that felt like three hundred.

Oliver didn't have Park's Domain. He didn't have training or the ability to feel the pressure on his skin before it arrived. What he had were the filaments, that network he could now read with a clarity he hadn't had on the roof, perhaps because fear had removed the filter between what his eyes saw and what his brain processed. He saw where they were going to converge before they converged. He saw which was the next point of attack, which would be the next gap.

And that, for the moment, was enough to move in the right direction.

Two blocks back, Park was moving in the opposite direction, and Oliver could hear the cracks, the blows of the ground beneath Park's feet, that irregular percussive sequence of someone using the world as a lever. Once a section of facade was flung toward the place where Park had been half a second earlier. Another time the pavement sank in a two-meter radius and Park cleared it with a distance no human body should be able to cover.

Oliver didn't stop to watch.

He kept reading the filaments. He kept moving.

When Park appeared beside him again, he was breathing with more effort than Oliver had seen from him until then. His right shoulder was at an angle that wasn't exactly natural.

"Are you all right?"

"Define all right," Park said. "Keep moving."

Oliver kept going.

But he looked at Park out of the corner of his eye, and for the first time since all this had begun he had the sense that the situation was bigger than the two of them. That Park, with all his training and his Domain and his calculated calm, hadn't entirely anticipated this either.

And that, somehow, was more terrifying than everything else.

The loose screw rolling along the ground to his right kept moving.

Nobody erased it.
