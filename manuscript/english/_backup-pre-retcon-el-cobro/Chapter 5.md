# Chapter 5 — Threshold

Oliver weighed more than Park expected.

It wasn't a matter of mass. It was the way an unconscious body distributes its weight, without even the minimal cooperation sleep still provides, and Park had to adjust his grip twice before he found a position that worked. He propped him against the facade of the nearest building, in a doorway that still held its shape, and crouched in front of him for a moment.

The kid was breathing. That was what mattered.

The line of blood running from his left nostril had already dried on his upper lip, dark and still. His eyes stayed closed with the kind of tension that suggested the body was working on something the mind couldn't supervise. Park had seen it before, in agents who had pushed their Domain past the safety threshold: that collapse that wasn't fainting but shutdown, the organism protecting what it couldn't afford to lose.

But Oliver didn't have a Domain.

Or not one in any form Park could recognize.

He stood and looked at the street.

Grayhaven had been dark twelve minutes. Park knew because he'd started counting from the moment the blackout spread in waves across the financial district, the same professional reflex that made him log distances and times whether anyone asked him to or not. Twelve minutes of electrical silence in a city this size wasn't a power cut. It was a statement.

And it was only after those twelve minutes that Grayhaven began giving back what the Domain had taken.

They arrived in small groups, at first. A woman who appeared in the middle of the roadway, sitting on the asphalt, staring at her own hands as if checking they were still there. Two men who reappeared meters apart from each other, not seeing each other, not speaking, with the expression Park had learned to recognize: someone trying to remember the last few minutes and finding only static. A boy of maybe eight, standing beside the streetlight Oliver had predicted would fall, crying without making a sound.

Expelled. Not destroyed.

Park watched the pattern with professional attention. The reintegration zone seemed to follow the same geography as the Domain, the displaced reappearing at points close to where they'd been when they were expelled, though with a margin of error that widened the longer they'd spent inside. Those who'd been in longest came back farther from their original spot. And some didn't come back with everything they'd had on them.

Damaged records. The agency had terminology for this.

What the terminology didn't quite capture was the specific look of a person with damaged records. Park had read it in reports. Seeing it was something else. The woman still sitting on the asphalt staring at her hands wasn't crying, wasn't screaming — she just kept looking, with the expression of someone trying to find a difference between the hands she remembered and the ones she had now, unable to pin down exactly what had changed. One of the men kept searching his pockets with repeated movements, always the same pocket, always the same gesture, as if the object might appear if he searched enough times. The other man still wasn't speaking to anyone. He stared at the space between the buildings with a concentration that went nowhere.

The boy by the streetlight had stopped crying. That was, somehow, worse.

---

Park ran the calculation he always ran after an incident of this scale: how many of them would remember anything coherent the next day, how many would have gaps their brains would fill in with plausible narratives, how many would end up at a doctor's office in the coming weeks with symptoms no conventional diagnosis could explain. The agency had protocols for every category. They worked, broadly speaking. What they didn't have was a protocol for what Park was thinking at that moment, which was whether they worked well enough to be worth it.

Park reached into the inside pocket of his jacket and pulled out the device. The screen showed seven missed notifications, all from the same number. The first had come in forty-two minutes earlier, during the first attacks at the building, when Park had made the conscious decision not to answer.

He looked at the number for several seconds.

Then he looked at Oliver.

The kid had said something before he lost consciousness. *That shouldn't have been so easy.* Park had heard it and hadn't answered, not because he had no answer but because the right one was too long for that moment and probably more than Oliver needed to know yet. The truth was it shouldn't have been that easy for Park either. There were agents with ten years of experience who hadn't survived an active Domain of this scale. Park had two prior missions, neither with direct contact.

And the kid had dismantled an Authority with his fists.

No training. No recognizable Domain. With something Park still couldn't name but had seen work, and that made it more dangerous than any name he could put on it.

The agency was going to want a lot of explanations.

Park had known that since the moment Oliver said *tell me you see it* in front of that terminal, three days ago, with the intensity of someone who's found something he can't let go of. He'd known it and made decisions accordingly: the motorcycle ride, the conversation at the door in Breakwater, the plan for a night downtown that never happened. Small attempts to redirect. None of them had worked.

And now he was here, on a dark street with a regional-scale blackout at his back and an unconscious civilian who'd just done something the agency would classify inside of forty-eight hours.

He called.

The line rang once.

"You took your time," the voice on the other end said. Not a reproach. A statement of fact.

"The situation escalated," Park answered.

"We know. We've got sixteen active readers in the zone. The Authority?"

"Neutralized."

Another silence, this one longer.

"By you?"

Park looked at Oliver.

"No," he said.

The voice on the other end didn't answer right away. Park heard the sound of someone processing information, the kind of pause that at the agency meant someone was taking notes or checking something in real time.

"Is a civilian involved?"

"Yes."

"Exposure level?"

"Direct contact with Odem. Active manifestation of unclassified capacity." Park chose each word carefully. "I couldn't tell you what it is yet."

"Understood. Stay where you are. Team in twelve minutes."

The call cut off.

Park pocketed the device and leaned against the wall next to Oliver.

---

He hadn't lied. He'd answered every question with just enough precision for the answer to be technically correct, and he'd left out what hadn't been asked directly. It was a distinction the agency would recognize in the formal report — verbal field reports carried an accepted margin of imprecision, precisely because field conditions didn't always allow for the clarity the forms required. The written report would be different. In the written report he'd have to be more precise.

What Park still didn't know was how to describe what he'd seen.

Not the unbinding itself — that had technical terms, the evaluation protocol would classify it efficiently and file it under the corresponding category. What had no terms was the way Oliver had done it. No training. No vocabulary for what he was looking at. With the same concentration he brought to the monitors at eleven at night, as if the Authority were a network problem with a logical solution if you knew where to look.

*That shouldn't have been so easy.*

Park hadn't contradicted him, because the truth was it shouldn't have been that easy for him either, and that was exactly the kind of observation that didn't fit in any standard report without opening questions Park wasn't ready to answer yet.

Twelve minutes. Enough time for the city to keep giving back its displaced, for the first sirens to start sounding at the district perimeter, for someone in some nearby building to find a flashlight and point it at the street.

Not enough for Oliver to wake up.

He looked at him again. He was twenty-two, lived alone in an attic in Breakwater with the bulbs burned out and the breaker panel tripped from the grid's fluctuations, and three days ago he'd found something in an electrical company's records that had gone unseen for months by analysts with twice his experience. Park had read his file before the mission. He'd memorized the operationally relevant details: height, weight, routines, points of social contact, no close family in the city, absent father. Everything he needed to talk him out of it and leave.

What wasn't in the file was how he looked at the monitors at eleven forty-five at night, with a concentration that didn't distinguish between what was important and what simply couldn't be ignored.

Park wasn't given to sentimentality. But he did recognize when he'd made an assessment error.

The error hadn't been saving him during the Domain. That was containment protocol, protecting the civilian while the situation stabilized. The error had been earlier. It had been the motorcycle, and the conversation outside his house, and the fraction of a second in which he'd calculated whether to let Oliver save the man with the briefcase or keep running, and had chosen to wait for him.

That wasn't in any protocol.

What was in the protocol was this: when a civilian showed unclassified capacity under field conditions, the field agent was required to report it in real time and wait for instructions. Park had ignored seven alerts. Not through carelessness — consciously, one by one, while calculating that the situation required his immediate attention and that instructions would arrive too late regardless.

It was a technically valid justification.

It was also, in the most honest version of events, a rationalization.

The honest version was that Park had watched Oliver look at the monitors with that specific concentration of someone who can't stop seeing something, and had made the wrong call that it was a character trait manageable with a conversation and a motorcycle and a planned night downtown. Not an indicator of capacity. Not something he should have reported the moment he noticed it, three days before the Authority activated.

That was the real error. Everything else was consequence.

In the distance, the first emergency lights began appearing at the district perimeter. Park watched them advance, slow, systematic, the kind of coordinated response a city activates when something exceeds local response capacity.

Eight minutes.

---

One of the displaced, a middle-aged man in a gray jacket Park hadn't seen before, reappeared on the sidewalk across the street. He stood there for a moment, looking at the space around him with the same static expression Park had seen in the others. Then he looked down, searching for something that wasn't there.

A briefcase, Park guessed. The same man. The pedestrian Oliver had decided was worth waiting for.

The man raised his eyes. He swept the street until he found Park, then Oliver. In his eyes there was something that wasn't recognition in the ordinary sense of the word — damaged records didn't leave clean recognition, they left fragments, impressions with no frame. But something in the way he stood there looking at Oliver suggested a residue was there. Not memory of the event. Something hazier: the sense that this unconscious body was connected to something that had happened, even if the connection had no name or sequence.

He stood looking at Oliver for several seconds, lips slightly parted, as if about to say something.

He said nothing.

Park watched the whole process — the moment the man decided he had no words for what he was looking at, the moment that decision turned into motion. He would show up in the agency report as displaced number sixteen or seventeen, with a records assessment scheduled sometime in the coming weeks, and would probably end up with a medical explanation his brain would accept because brains prefer explanations that fit.

Finally he turned and walked off in the opposite direction, slowly, like someone still learning that his legs work.

Park watched him until he turned the corner.

Then he checked his device. Six minutes.

Enough.
