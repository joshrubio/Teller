# Chapter 50 — Interior

Cartwright found him still in Budapest, two days after the bridge — Katalin's shoulder barely out of the sling, the incident report still not fully closed in any system Park could see.

"Venezuela," she said, without preamble, the voice coming down the same secure channel as always. "Anomalous readings near a mountain called Sorte, Yaracuy state. A concentration of disappearances in the same area that doesn't match the pattern of local violence. AMAT-Caribbean requested you specifically, by name."

"Why me?"

"Because Claudia Ferrer, who runs that branch with whatever she has left of it, wants a reading with no prior assumptions about that country," Cartwright said. "And because you already proved in Budapest that you can operate without a net when you have to. Your objectives are three: confirm whether the readings and the disappearances are the same phenomenon, identify the source, and classify the threat with something more specific than 'we don't know.' In that order."

"And if it's nothing?"

"Then you'll have spent a few days confirming it's nothing, and Ferrer will have had the only outside asset she had this month," Cartwright said. "One last thing, and I'm telling you this because I owe it to you, not because protocol requires it: Washington has something big prepared for that region, weeks out, maybe days. I don't know the full detail and I wouldn't tell you over this channel if I did. Whatever you find needs to be contained and kept quiet before that happens. Absolute discretion, agent. More than anywhere I've sent you before."

"Do you know what's down there?"

"We know there's something," Cartwright said. "We didn't know, until Ferrer's call this week, that it had spent a decade forming right under our noses without anyone seeing it coming. That should also tell you something about that country. It's not just that the government lost control of what's happening down there. All of us did."

She hung up without giving Park the chance to ask anything else.

---

The flight left him with too many hours to think and no desire to do it. Budapest still weighed on his right shoulder, on the rib that hadn't finished settling, in the fixed image of a bridge that had stopped groaning because it had finished deciding how much it could hold. He hadn't spoken to anyone on the team since the airport. He hadn't written the report that technically fell to him. He told himself it was because he hadn't had time, and he didn't fully believe the excuse for a single one of the eight hours it took to cross the Atlantic.

Caracas airport still functioned, in the strict sense that planes landed and took off. Everything else — the double lines at immigration, the portable generators humming beside dead baggage belts, the welcome signs bearing the name of a government that no longer controlled even half of what it claimed to — told Park, before he'd cleared the first gate, that this was going to be a different kind of mission than the previous four.

In Hartford he'd arrived at a contained scar, with seventy years of protocol wrapped around it. In Seoul, at an operation with its own history and a hierarchy that preceded him. In Lisbon and Budapest, at small but functional institutions, with a building, a chain of command, something to hold onto even when it failed. Here, the country itself was the unstable variable — not the mission's backdrop but an active part of it, with its own rhythm of collapse that wasn't going to wait for AMAT to finish understanding what was happening.

There had been a country, not that many decades ago, where this same capital had one of the highest per-capita incomes on the continent — new highways, universities that exported professionals instead of receiving remittances, a currency people from other countries lined up to exchange for. None of that vanished from an earthquake or a flood or any of the discrete catastrophes Park had learned to trace in Lisbon or Budapest. It vanished slowly, over two decades of promises of a revolution that swore to redistribute wealth and ended up concentrating it in fewer hands than before, until the line between government and organized crime stopped being a line and became an almost total overlap. Seven million people had left the country in that time — the kind of figure no AMAT report knew how to classify, because it wasn't an Authority Level or a Ruler's residue. It was, simply, what happens when an entire society decides, generation after generation, that corruption is the price of surviving one more day.

What Cartwright hadn't told him, and maybe didn't know either, was that this same pattern — power concentrating in the hands of whoever was willing to do anything to keep it — hadn't stayed limited to the economic. At some point in those two decades, more than one faction within the power apparatus itself had gone looking for advantages in places no official should have been looking. Not just the one Park didn't yet know existed. Venezuela wasn't falling apart from mismanagement alone. Part of what kept it standing, for better and worse, had for some time been something neither the government nor the opposition could fully name.

---

Iván Salcedo was waiting for him with a cardboard sign that had no name written on it, only three wavy lines it took Park a second to recognize as an inside joke of the Caribbean branch.

"Yaracuy river level," Salcedo said, tucking the sign under his arm before anyone else could see it. "Courtesy of whoever designed our discreet recognition protocols and evidently has a lot of free time."

"Claudia Ferrer?"

"Claudia Ferrer," Salcedo confirmed. "Welcome to the branch holding the operation together with whatever's left."

Salcedo drove a pickup fifteen years old with an engine that sounded better cared for than the body. He talked the whole way west with the same economy Park recognized from well-run field briefings — dense information, no filler, not one word about Salcedo himself until Park asked directly.

"You're going to need to adjust expectations before we get there," Salcedo said, already on the highway, before Park had asked him anything. "In Lisbon or Budapest, I'd guess, you had a branch behind you with a building, protocol, people. Here you have a woman in an office with her own generator because the power goes out three times a day, and me."

"How long has the branch been like this?"

"This small? Five years. This bad? The last two." Salcedo swerved to dodge a pothole the size of a wheel. "People here don't expect AMAT to save them. Most of them don't even know we exist. What they have been waiting for, some for twenty years, is for someone from outside to do something. Anything. You'll notice that fast."

"Ten years in the branch," he said then. "Before that, civil engineering, if you care. You learn more from structures failing than from structures holding, and this country's spent a decade being the former."

The change in landscape, as they moved away from the capital, wasn't gradual. It came in blocks: the last gas station with a three-hour line, the last checkpoint with more different uniform colors than a single checkpoint should have, and then kilometers of dense vegetation broken up by towns that seemed to stand out of habit more than for any other reason.

The town where Salcedo finally stopped the pickup had no name on any map Park had consulted before leaving Budapest — a main street of cracked asphalt, unpainted block houses left half-finished years ago and never picked back up, power lines hanging between poles at angles no engineer would have approved. A generator, somewhere out of sight in a yard, held up with its hum the few lights still on after seven. It smelled of woodsmoke, of burnt gasoline, and beneath both, closer to the river, of wet turned earth — the specific smell of a place where something was growing without anyone cultivating it on purpose. People on the street walked with the efficiency of those who have no time to stop and look twice at a foreigner, though almost all of them, out of the corner of their eye, did it once.

"From here on," Salcedo said, lowering his voice even though there was no one else in the vehicle, "neither SEBIN nor the Guard are people you want to cross paths with by accident. They're not declared enemies. They're variables we don't control."

---

The video call with Claudia Ferrer came that night, in a hotel room with more empty rooms than occupied ones. Ferrer spoke from Caracas with a calm that had cost her months of allowing herself nothing else.

"Official cover: study of minor seismic anomalies, attributable to illegal mining," she said. "It's only half a lie. There is illegal mining in the area. It isn't the cause of what we're seeing."

"Cartwright gave me the general objectives," Park said. "Give me yours."

"Confirm whether the disappearances and the readings are the same phenomenon. If they are, don't go straight to the mountain — neither you nor Salcedo have any way to read that terrain without someone seeing you arrive. Go first to the people who've spent months documenting every disappearance better than any official report we're going to get."

"Who?"

"Family members. Mostly mothers," Ferrer said. "There's an informal network around a woman named Nélida Rondón, in the town closest to Sorte. They're not an official source. They're the only source we have."

"How many agents do you have assigned to this, besides Salcedo?"

"None. The rest of the branch is holding, right now, against the very real possibility that the whole country reorganizes itself in the next few weeks in a way that's going to give no one time to prepare. It's not that I don't want to give you more. It's that I don't have more to give."

"One last thing," Ferrer said, before Park could end the call. "You're not going to find here what you found in Lisbon or Budapest. No guardian lineage with generations of discipline behind it. Whatever this is, it's recent, and it was born wrong. We have no precedent to use as a map."

"How recent?"

"I don't know yet," Ferrer said. "Find out, agent. That's literally why I called you in."

The call cut off without a goodbye — not rudeness, just the rhythm of someone weighing every minute against a longer list of more urgent things.

---

Salcedo knew someone who knew Nélida, and that someone turned out to be Yusmary Cordero, who reached the door of the house before they did, already forewarned, already decided to form an opinion before Park had finished getting out of the pickup.

Nélida's porch was little more than a concrete slab under a zinc roof, with two white plastic rocking chairs already yellowed by the sun and a framed image of the Virgin next to another, smaller one, of a figure Park didn't fully recognize — a woman crowned in vegetation, a bird perched on her shoulder. Yusmary stood between the two chairs, arms crossed, blocking the way to the door with her whole body, not just her attitude.

"Before you go in," she said, cutting off his path on the porch, "I want you to know Nélida has had journalists, activists, a couple of NGO people, and none of them came back a second time with anything useful. If you're the kind who comes to listen and then leaves, I'd rather you leave now."

"I don't know yet what kind of help I can offer," Park said. "What I can tell you is what I came to confirm: whether what happened to the people in this area is one thing, not a hundred different things. If it's one thing, it makes more sense to fight it than if it's a hundred."

Yusmary measured him a moment longer than the answer deserved.

"I was a teacher for twenty years," she said. "You learn to tell someone reciting a script from someone thinking through the answer while they say it. You're thinking it through."

"Is that enough?"

"No," she said. "But it's more than I've been given in months. Come in. And I don't promise Nélida is going to trust you as fast as I do."

---

Nélida Rondón's house had walls covered in photographs — not decorative, functional: names, dates, last known locations, written by hand under each face, some already faded by humidity and rewritten over with a different pen. She herself opened the door with the calibrated distrust of someone who'd opened it too many times for people who never turned out useful past the first visit — a small woman, her hair pulled back in a gray braid, her hands rough from still washing other people's clothes to keep the house running.

"My son is up there," she said, pointing at one photo among fifteen others, with no need for Park to ask which. "Eleven months. The Guard said he'd gone off with a gang. The gang, when someone worked up the nerve to ask one of them directly, said they didn't know what she was talking about."

The living room was small — a two-seat sofa with the fabric worn through at the arms, a dead television with a crocheted cloth on top instead of underneath, and a standing fan turning slowly without cooling much of anything. There were five more women, sitting on plastic chairs each had brought from her own home because Nélida's didn't have enough, and none introduced herself by full name — only the name of the person she was looking for, as if that were the information that actually mattered. One of them, the youngest, couldn't have been more than twenty-five and carried a sleeping baby against her chest, wrapped in a blanket despite the heat. Nélida served coffee in glass jars from different preserve brands, no two alike, each filled to a finger from the rim, before anyone asked — the kind of gesture that survives any amount of despair because it no longer depends on having the spirit for it, only the habit. Park held his without drinking, the glass still warm against his fingers, while he listened.

Park didn't ask them to trust him. He asked for the dates, the places, the order in which people had disappeared — and listened, without interrupting, while Nélida and the others reconstructed, between them, a more complete timeline than the one he'd put together since the airport. Only when they finished did he start asking what he actually needed to know, and by then he'd already earned the answers.

The pattern, once he'd finished assembling it, was exactly what Ferrer had pointed to: dates clustered, not distributed at random; every disappearance within a radius that kept closing, tighter each time, around the same mountain.

A young man in the doorway was talking fast to another about something Park couldn't quite follow — the thick interior accent, the S's disappearing halfway through words, the rhythm no language course prepares anyone to follow in real time. Yusmary, noticing Park's pause, translated without being asked.

"He says last night, near the river, the ground moved where nothing should move," she said. "A small tremor, localized, with no official seismic report."

---

Park left Nélida's house already at night, with Salcedo waiting in the pickup and a mental notebook full of names, dates, and a radius on the map that kept narrowing around the same mountain.

"Anything?" Salcedo asked.

"First objective confirmed," Park said. "It's a single phenomenon, not a dozen separate cases. And people who've been documenting it better than any official report we're going to get."

They pulled out toward the hotel on the only decent road in the area. Salcedo drove in silence, his hands tighter on the wheel than the straight road called for.

"How many like Nélida are there in this area?" Park asked.

"More than any official report has bothered to count," Salcedo said. "That's the problem with this country right now. There are so many people disappeared for so many different reasons that one more, even a strange one, doesn't catch the attention of anyone with the authority to investigate it."

Half an hour from the town, with no warning, the whole pickup shook — not a pothole, not a badly taken curve. Salcedo braked hard, both of them looking toward the window in the direction of Sorte, where there was nothing to see yet except the way the trees, for one whole second, stopped being still at all.

"That," Salcedo said, his voice lower than the size of the vehicle required, "wasn't geological."

Park already had his hand on the door handle before Salcedo finished the sentence, his gaze fixed on the same dark patch of the mountain where the trees, now, had gone perfectly still again — too still, as if something had held its breath at the same moment they did.
