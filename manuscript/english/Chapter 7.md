# Chapter 7 — The Exact Weight

Park had been at Dunmore nine weeks the afternoon Oliver let him up to the attic for the first time.

It wasn't a decision he'd thought through. It was more the kind of thing that happens once someone has already seen you up close enough — a violent customer at Foster, whole shifts closing the register in shared silence — that hiding the rest starts to weigh more than showing it. Gary was still out on the road three, four nights a week, and the attic was still the one room in the house where the air felt entirely his own. His father's debt was still there, over three thousand dollars of it, forty less every week; that hadn't changed. What had changed, without Oliver quite deciding it, was that he no longer wanted to keep the rest to himself alone.

Silas was the first to notice something different, though he never said it in those words. "Yamal doesn't hand the closing keys to just anyone," he said one afternoon, watching Oliver close Dunmore alone for the second time that week. "It took me eight years to earn that. You — just over one." It didn't sound like a complaint. It sounded like the same tone Silas used to file away anything that didn't quite sit right with him — and kept, without comment, for some day that hadn't come yet.

In those nine weeks, he and Park had settled into a rhythm Oliver found hard to explain without it sounding more important than it probably was: Park always showed up a few minutes early, sat on the same stool in the back going over appraisals that had already been checked, and waited — never saying so out loud — for Oliver to finish closing out the till so they could walk to the bus stop together. They didn't talk much on the way. For Oliver, who'd spent half his life preferring the silence of numbers to the noise of people, that was enough.

Ninety percent.

He'd spent over a year logging the same figure, every night before bed, on a separate sheet no one else had reason to look at: the three accounts Farid handled in person, always closing at the same percentage, never a hundred, never far from ninety. He knew, with the cold certainty of someone who'd worked with numbers his whole life, that it wasn't chance. He didn't know yet what it was. He'd never told anyone — not because it struck him as dangerous, but because he had no way to explain it without it sounding like a fixation with no object.

"And this?" Park asked from the attic doorway. Nine weeks wasn't much, but it was already enough that he'd become the one person Oliver could have shown this to without rehearsing how to say it first.

"A fixation." He handed him the sheet. "The accounts Farid keeps himself never close at a hundred percent. Almost always ninety. Exactly, most of the time."

Park read it twice, with that stillness of his that, after only two months, Oliver already had trouble picturing in anyone else his age.

"Have you told anyone?"

"Who would I tell?"

Park didn't answer that directly.

"Farid keeps a separate notebook," he said, after a silence too long to be a simple pause. "Older. Black cover. I've seen him put it away a couple of times, out of the corner of my eye, when he thinks no one's watching. He never logs it with the rest."

Oliver stared at him a second too long. "Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because you've spent over a year staring at that number without doing anything about it." Park shrugged, almost apologizing for the bluntness. "And I like things that don't add up too."

He didn't ask anything else. In two months he'd already learned that certain things Park said sounded more like a warning than curiosity, and that it was better not to pull too hard on the thread until he knew how much pulling he could afford.

---

The safe had sat behind the Dunmore counter for years, in the exact same spot it had always occupied, and Oliver had looked at it so many times without permission to touch it that he knew every detail of its surface better than the palm of his own hand: the dial worn smooth at the number seven, brighter than the rest from decades of a thumb's friction; the lower right corner, slightly dented, from a blow no one had ever explained to him. Farid always opened it with his back to the room, his whole body wedged between the dial and any eyes that might be watching — a discretion so practiced that it took Oliver a while to realize it was, precisely, too practiced to be simple habit.

But a man who repeats the same gesture in front of the same eyes, year after year, sooner or later stops seeing those eyes as a threat.

Oliver didn't memorize the combination all at once. He pieced it together, without quite meaning to at first, the same way he memorized anything else that mattered to him: the first number, on a rainy Tuesday when Farid, hands full of boxes, told him to "turn the seven" without thinking about who was watching; the second, months later, deduced from the exact angle where Farid's wrist paused before the second turn; the last two, almost by elimination — the only combination left standing once Oliver had ruled out, night after night, every variant in his head, like a man solving a crossword no one else knows exists. Then one day, going over an ordinary Tuesday's till, he realized he'd been carrying all six digits in his head for, probably, over a year.

He did nothing with it for months. Knowing something and using it were, as he'd learned from Farid without Farid ever meaning to teach him, two entirely different decisions.

What finally pushed him wasn't the conversation in the attic, or even the notebook itself, but something almost comically small: Farid, closing the safe in front of him one Monday afternoon with no special caution at all, mentioned in passing that thirty-some years had gone by without anyone — employee or customer — ever daring to ask what he kept in there besides the money. He said it with the same pride a man takes in a lock that's never failed him. Oliver said nothing that afternoon. But that night, in the attic, he crossed off, mentally, the one real reason he had left for not trying: the fear that someone would notice.

The night he decided to use it was a Tuesday in October, two weeks after that afternoon in the attic — Park was already in his eleventh week at Dunmore. Farid had left early, a doctor's appointment he didn't explain and Oliver didn't ask about, leaving him the closing keys, as he'd taken to doing more and more often lately.

He stayed past ten, the shop lights low, pretending to finish a reconciliation he'd actually finished two hours earlier. Once he was sure Halloway had gone as empty as it was going to get, he stood, crossed to the counter, and for the first time put his fingers on the dial he'd spent half his life watching from a distance.

Seven. Twenty-three. Forty-one. Seven again, reversed.

The mechanism gave with a click softer than he'd imagined.

---

Inside, among bundles of old contracts and an envelope of cash Oliver didn't bother counting, was the notebook.

Black cover, worn at the corners down to the cardboard underneath, with nothing to set it apart from any notebook bought at any stationery shop in the last thirty years. Oliver held it with both hands before pulling it all the way out, with the same exaggerated care you'd use to hold something you suspect — without being able to prove it yet — shouldn't be this easy to hold.

It weighed less than it should have.

It wasn't a dramatic difference — he couldn't have sworn to it in front of anyone, couldn't have convinced anyone else by just handing it over — but a notebook of that size, with that many pages, should have weighed more than this. It should have weighed the way real things weigh. And this weighed the way an object weighs once it has decided, at some point in its existence, to stop fully honoring the laws that govern ordinary objects.

How much does that weigh, he'd asked once himself, thirteen years old, standing beside this same counter, with no idea yet that the question had spent all those years waiting for its own answer.

More than you can carry, Farid had told him then.

He'd been wrong. Or he'd lied, in the only way Farid knew how to lie: saying something that sounded like the whole truth without quite being it.

Oliver opened it on the counter, under the one low light he'd left on, expecting something like the rest of Farid's bookkeeping — columns, figures, the same cramped handwriting keeping real accounts. It wasn't that.

There were no figures. No columns. Each entry ran, at most, two lines: a name, a date, and beside each one, without exception, the same symbol — a circle struck through, neat, drawn the same way every time, the line never shaking, not in any decade, not on any page. No dollar sign, anywhere. As if money, in this notebook, was the one thing that no longer needed writing down.

Names he didn't recognize. Dozens of them, spread across almost fifty years — never more than two or three a year — in the same cramped hand of Farid's, never once wavering from one decade to the next.

And then he understood, all at once, with the same cold certainty he'd spent over a year staring at a nameless number: this was the other thing. The ten percent that never squared in any till closing hadn't gone missing. It was here, turned into a name and a circle, page after page, for half a century.

There was no need to look for his father in this. This notebook didn't keep Farid's accounts. It kept the account of something else. And every struck-through name wasn't a debt paid off.

It was a payment.

He closed his eyes for a second. Opened them again.

And then, tucked along the inside edge of the cover, something that wasn't part of the original binding: a loose note, in a hand that wasn't Farid's — older, more foreign in its strokes, almost ceremonial — that read, unsigned, with no date visible:

*One in every ten, always. The rest is yours.*

Oliver didn't fully understand what it meant. He would understand, much later, that he hadn't needed to understand it that night for something to have already started changing.

---

The sound came first: not a noise, but the sudden absence of every other one. The drinks fridge by the door, which had been humming for fifteen years with no one ever switching it off. The wall clock Farid never bothered to set right. The distant murmur of traffic on Halloway. All of it cut out at once — not like a volume fading, but like a word someone stops saying halfway through.

The shop didn't change how it looked. It changed how it weighed. Oliver felt it in his chest before he had a name for it — the same drop you feel riding an elevator down too fast, except nothing around him was moving. As if the air itself had suddenly become part of some outstanding account.

On the notebook's first blank page, with no hand touching it, no visible ink falling from anywhere, four words appeared in the same cramped hand as Farid's, as if the notebook itself had decided, after thirty years of silence, to write on its own for the first time:

*REGISTRY SETTLED.*

Oliver stepped back without deciding to. His heart was slamming hard enough that his chest ached with every beat, and for an instant — so brief that afterward he wouldn't fully trust that he'd seen it — the counter, the register, the very edge of the notebook lost the faint contours that had kept them apart, as if everything had suddenly become the same indistinct substance seen through dirty glass.

And in the exact center of wherever whatever had just woken should have stood, he saw nothing. Not a shadow, not a silhouette, not a contour of its own — an empty node, a clean gap roughly the size of a person, precisely where his sight should have found something and instead found nothing at all. A void shaped like an absence — and the sudden, physical sensation that it was watching him with more attention than he had ever paid to anything in his life.

It passed as fast as it had come. The counter had edges again. The register went back to being just a register. But something in his sight itself kept aching, a thin, steady pulse, like the afterimage of looking straight at the sun a second too long.

Oliver snapped the notebook shut and stood still a moment longer than was wise, waiting for the shaking in his hands to settle enough to think in some kind of order.

The first thing he did, before anything else, was the thing he'd spent half his life knowing how to do better than anyone: leave things exactly as they were. He put the bundles of contracts back in the order he'd found them, checked that the envelope of cash hadn't shifted, and closed the safe door with the same quiet care he'd used to open it — the mechanism didn't need the combination dialed again to catch. He switched off the one low light he'd left burning. When he walked out of the shop, the notebook under his jacket, everything in Dunmore looked exactly the way it had that same afternoon, except for the one thing that was no longer there — and that no one else had reason to miss until the moment they needed it.

He locked the front door with his own key and walked — didn't run, though every part of him wanted to — toward the stop where he usually waited for the ten thirty bus, at the same pace as any other night he closed alone.

It was only turning onto Halloway that he finally let himself look back.

There was nothing. No strange lights, no shadow, no sound that couldn't be explained by an October street at that hour: a cat crossing between two dumpsters, a distant engine shifting gears. But for a second, maybe two, he had the physical and entirely irrational certainty that if he turned around once more he'd find something watching him from a darkened storefront two blocks back. He didn't turn around again.

He thought about calling Park. He held his thumb over the name on the screen for a couple of seconds before putting the phone away without dialing — not yet, not until he knew exactly what he was going to ask Park to do about this. Park had asked him, in the attic, what he planned to do. He hadn't had an answer then. He still didn't, walking toward the stop with the notebook against his chest, lighter now than it had ever been, now that hands other than Farid's had finally touched it.

And Oliver, for the first time in all the time he'd spent counting other people's money, understood that there was an account — somewhere that wasn't paper at all — that had just opened in his name.
